Marketing a listing in a commuter suburb

In a commuter suburb the buyer is buying a trip as much as a house. Measure door to platform, show the route, and sell the hours they are home.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

In a commuter suburb the buyer is buying a trip as much as a house. Every morning and every evening of their week will start with the walk or drive to the station, the time on the train, the parking and the walk home, and they are comparing your listing against three others on exactly those numbers. Market the listing by measuring the door-to-platform time yourself, showing the route in a short portrait video, stating the service facts as facts without promising a timetable, and shooting the rooms a commuter actually uses at the hours they use them: the kitchen at 7am, the living room at 7pm, the mudroom with the coats in it.

The buyer's arithmetic is time

A commuter has already worked out how many minutes they are prepared to give the trip, and the house either fits inside that number or it does not. Your job is to give them the number precisely so they can place your listing correctly. Walk from the front door to the platform with a timer and write down the minutes. Drive it too, at 7:30am, and note where you parked and whether the lot was full. Give the time to the city terminal as the operator states it for the morning service, and say which station and which line, with a note that timetables change. Give the drive to the highway on-ramp as well, because the second car goes a different way.

Then give the evening. A buyer leaving the city at 6pm wants to know what time they are in their kitchen. If the station has a second exit nearer the house, say so. If the walk is lit, say so. The buyer is picturing a Tuesday in November, not a Saturday in June.

A colonial seven minutes from the station

A four-bed colonial on a side street, a seven-minute walk to the station by the footpath at the end of the road, with a detached garage and a mudroom off the kitchen. The agent walks the route with the phone in portrait at 7:20am: out the front door, down the street, through the footpath, onto the platform as a train pulls in, with the minutes in the captions. The photographer shoots the kitchen at 7am with the light coming in, the mudroom with coats and boots in it, the living room and the porch at 7pm, the yard at 4pm, the bedrooms and baths through the morning. The page gives the walk, the drive, the line, the station, the morning service time to the terminal as the operator states it, the station parking situation, the drive to the on-ramp and the nearest grocery store.

A couple who both commute find the listing on a Wednesday night, watch the walk video, check the operator's timetable in another tab, and fill in the enquiry form for Saturday. At the showing they walk the route themselves and get seven minutes. The kitchen at 10am is not the kitchen at 7am, but the page showed them 7am. They offer on Sunday; the deciding sentence, they say later, was the footpath at the end of the road.

Show the route, not just the house

  • The walk in portrait. Door to platform, real time, captions with the minutes, shot at commuting hour so the light and the traffic are honest.
  • The station itself. One frame of the platform, one of the parking, one of the entrance nearest the house. Buyers who commute care about the station the way others care about the yard.
  • The hours they are home. Kitchen in morning light, living room and porch in the evening. The house is being bought for those hours; show them.
  • The arrival spaces. Mudroom, entry closet, the spot where the bags land. The commuter sees them twice a day.
  • The second commute. The drive to the highway for the partner who does not take the train, measured at the same hour.

What goes wrong in commuter listings

  • Minutes to the city as the headline. That is the train's number, not the house's. The house's number is door to platform, and the buyer knows the difference.
  • Walking distance without a time. Walking distance is anything under an hour to an optimist. Give the minutes.
  • Promising the timetable. State the operator's current morning service and say timetables change. Do not guarantee a train.
  • Shooting everything at noon. The commuter is not home at noon. A kitchen at 7am with the sun in it is the photo that sells the mornings.
  • Hiding the station's downsides. A full lot, a long second exit, a level crossing. The buyer will find out on the first Monday; better they find out on the page.
  • Edits that will not survive the walk. A buyer who walks the route will also walk the rooms. Compare every edit against its original, keep the disclosure, and keep the wide shots honest.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What is the most important fact in a commuter suburb listing?

The door-to-platform time, measured by you at commuting hour, with the station and line named. Minutes to the city is the train's number; door to platform is the house's number, and buyers compare listings on it.

Can I state the train time to the city on the listing?

State the operator's current morning service time as a fact with a note that timetables change, and do not guarantee it. Pair it with the walk or drive to the station and the parking situation so the buyer can add the pieces up.

Which rooms should I photograph for commuter buyers?

The kitchen in morning light, the living room and porch in the evening, and the arrival spaces such as the mudroom and entry closet. The house is being bought for the hours before and after the trip, so show it at those hours.

Should the listing mention station parking or a level crossing?

Yes. The buyer will discover a full lot or a crossing on their first Monday. A listing that stated it on the page earns trust; one that left it out loses it, often after the offer.
