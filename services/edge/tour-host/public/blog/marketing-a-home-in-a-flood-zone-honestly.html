Marketing a home in a flood zone honestly

Disclose per your state's rules and ask your broker. Then market the home as it is: show the water, the lot and the documented work; never edit them away.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Disclose the flood zone according to your state's rules and ask your broker what to say, where and in what words; that part is not a marketing decision. The marketing part is to show the home as it sits: the water or the low ground nearby, the lot's grade, the raised foundation if there is one, the drainage and the work the seller has documented with dates, and the reason people live there anyway, which is usually the same water. Never use a photo edit to move, hide or dry up what is around the house.

Two separate jobs. The first is disclosure: a home in a flood zone is disclosed according to your state's rules, and your broker tells you what to say, where to say it and in what words. That is not a marketing question, and this post stops there on it. The second job is marketing the home as it actually sits, which means showing the water or the low ground nearby, the lot's grade, the foundation, the drainage and the work the seller has documented, and showing the reason people choose to live there, which is usually the same water. Buyers who learn at inspection what the listing left out do not come back; buyers told up front who still book are the real prospects.

Honesty is the strategy, not a cost of it

A home near a creek, a bay, a river or a low corner of a subdivision has a buyer pool that already knows what it is looking at. They reward the listing that saves them a wasted visit, so the marketing does not pretend the creek is not there; it shows the creek from the deck as the asset it is, shows the ground between the creek and the house, shows the foundation and the sump and the dated invoices, and lets the buyer price the rest with their own advisors. The post on honest listing marketing covers the general principle; this is the property type where it matters most.

Shot list: the house and its ground

  1. Front exterior, at the hour the light suits it, with the whole foundation visible. If the house is raised, shoot it so the height reads; do not crop the piers or the stairs.
  2. The lot from the street, showing the grade, the swale, the drainage ditch, the culvert, as they exist.
  3. The water or the low ground, from the house and from the yard. If it is a view, treat it as one and shoot it well. If it is a ditch, show the ditch.
  4. The yard between the house and the water, with the slope readable, in two frames.
  5. The foundation, crawlspace or lowest level, lit, dry, as it is on the day.
  6. Documented work: the sump pump, the French drain outlet, the backflow valve, the regraded bed, the new foundation vents, each with the invoice date in the text.
  7. Utilities and where they sit: the water heater, the furnace, the panel, at whatever height they are.
  8. The interior in the usual order, with rooms facing the water shot toward it.
  9. The street and neighbors, so the buyer sees the house is not alone in its situation.

The listing video can open on the water if the water is the view, and it should include the foundation and the yard frames rather than skipping them. Keep the vertical social video to the house and the view; the documentation belongs on the page.

What the page states

  • The disclosure, placed and worded as your broker advises for your state.
  • Where the house sits relative to the water or the low ground, in feet and in plain words.
  • Foundation type and whether the home is raised, with the height from the seller's documents if a figure exists.
  • Drainage and mitigation work the seller has done, each with a year and a document available on request.
  • Water events the seller discloses, in the words and form your broker advises.
  • What the water gives the house: the view, the dock, the trail, the quiet.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in the fields.

Say nothing about insurance costs or coverage beyond pointing the buyer to get their own quote; your broker will tell you what, if anything, you may provide to help them.

What goes wrong, and it is usually an edit

  • A tidy or custom edit that removes the ditch, the standing water or the high-water mark. This is the most damaging edit in listing photography. Compare every edited frame against the original; better, do not edit the ground at all.
  • A blue sky edit after a storm that also dries the lawn. The buyer sees the saturated yard at the showing.
  • Cropping the piers. A raised home is a raised home; hiding the height hides the point.
  • Skipping the crawlspace frame. The buyer assumes the worst and the inspector confirms whatever they find.
  • Leading with the water and never mentioning it in the text. The gallery says one thing and the page another.
  • Writing the disclosure yourself. Ask your broker.

Worked example: a 3-bed on a creek lot

A 3-bed, 2-bath on a lot that slopes down to a creek at the back, with a crawlspace, a sump pump installed in 2020, a French drain along the rear foundation from the same year, a regraded side yard from 2022, and a deck that looks over the water. The agent asks the broker how the disclosure is handled and follows that. The photographer shoots on a dry Thursday afternoon: the front with the full foundation, the lot from the street showing the slope and the side swale, the creek from the deck, two frames of the yard from the deck down to the water, the crawlspace access with the sump visible, the drain outlet at the corner, the regraded bed, the water heater and furnace on their platform, the interior, and the street with the neighbors' homes on the same slope. The page carries the disclosure as the broker directed, then: creek roughly 80 feet from the rear foundation per the survey, crawlspace, sump 2020, French drain 2020, regrade 2022, documents on request, deck over the creek with afternoon shade. Fifteen landscape frames make the listing video, opening on the creek from the deck and including the foundation frames. The deck view is the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How do I disclose that a home is in a flood zone?

According to your state's rules, in the place and words your broker advises. Ask your broker before the listing goes live. That part is not a marketing decision.

Should the listing photos show the creek or the low ground?

Yes. Show the water as the view it often is, and show the ground between it and the house, the foundation and any documented drainage work. Buyers who know what they are looking at reward the listing that does not hide it.

Can I use an AI edit to remove standing water or a ditch from a photo?

No. That is the most damaging edit in listing photography. Compare any edit against the original, keep the disclosure on anything you use, and leave the ground alone.

What should the page say about flood insurance?

Nothing beyond suggesting the buyer get their own quote. Ask your broker what, if anything, you may provide to help them do that.
