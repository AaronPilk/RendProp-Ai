Open house for a new construction model

A model home open house sells a lot, a builder and a house that does not exist yet. The page should show the model, the real lots and what is standard.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A model home open house is selling three things the visitor cannot see: the lot they would actually buy, the builder's reliability, and the version of this house with standard finishes rather than every upgrade. The property page should show the finished model, progress photos of the homes actually for sale, and a plain list of what is standard and what is an upgrade. Follow-up runs for months, not days.

A model home open house is selling what the visitor cannot see. They are standing in a finished house on a corner lot with every upgrade, and what is for sale is an unfinished house on a different lot with the standard kitchen. The job of the open house, and of the property page behind it, is to close that gap honestly: show the model, show the real lots as they stand today, and say plainly what is standard and what the model added.

Visitors to a resale open house decide about a house. Visitors to a model decide about a builder. They are judging whether what they see is what they would get, and every unlabelled upgrade erodes that.

Meadowbrook Lane, Saturday and Sunday 11 to 5

A model at 102 Meadowbrook Lane in a forty-lot community, three homes under construction, twelve lots released. The model is open both weekend days, 11 to 5, for the next three months.

The page. Published before the first weekend: the model's gallery, exterior to interior; a listing video of the model with a voiceover that names the plan and says “shown with upgraded kitchen, flooring and lighting”; then, further down the gallery, progress photos of lots 7, 9 and 14 as they stood that week, framed and roofed; the base price for the plan and what it includes; a short description of the community, the timeline and the next release.

The sign at the door. The page link and a QR code, with “see the homes under construction and the standard finishes” beside it, because that is what visitors want after they have seen the kitchen.

Saturday. Visitors walk the model. At the island, the page is open on a tablet. When someone asks “is this the kitchen,” the answer is “this is the upgraded one; the standard list is on the page, and lot 9 is at framing if you want to walk it.” They scroll, they see lot 9, half of them ask to walk it. That walk is the real showing.

Over three months. The page's gallery gets the week's progress photos each Friday, so the visitor from January who is deciding in March can see the same lot nearly finished. The enquiry form is where that visitor asks for a second walk.

What the page carries that the model cannot

  • Progress photos by lot. Dated, in order. The lot the buyer would get, not the one with the fountain.
  • The standard-versus-upgrade list. In plain words: standard counters, standard flooring, standard lighting, and what the model has instead. Buyers forgive upgrades they were told about and resent the ones they discover at contract.
  • The base price and the timeline. Price for the plan as standard; a realistic month for the next completions.
  • The community. The entrance, the road, the lots as a whole. One wide photo from the entrance does more than a rendered map.
  • A way to ask. The form, with the date field, because new-construction buyers come back three or four times.

Rendered images and real ones

Builders often have rendered exteriors and rendered interiors for plans that are not built yet. Use them if you must, but keep them separate from the photos and label them as renderings every time. An image that looks like a photo of a house that does not exist is the fastest way to lose a buyer who later stands on the real lot. The same goes for any AI edit of a progress photo: a green lawn on a lot that is mud until spring should either be labelled or left as mud.

Where model open houses go wrong

  • Every upgrade, a base price. The visitor falls for the model, learns the price is for a different kitchen, and feels sold to.
  • No photos of the actual lots. The buyer cannot picture lot 9 and does not want to drive to it in the rain. Weekly progress photos fix it.
  • The builder's rep and the agent giving different answers. Agree the standard list and the timeline before the first weekend and put both on the page so there is one version.
  • Follow-up that stops after a week. A buyer who visited in January and did nothing is often the March buyer. The page gives you a reason to text them: “lot 9 is at drywall, here is the photo.”
  • A video of the model with no words. Music and pans, and no mention of what is upgraded. The voiceover is where the honesty lives.
  • Stale page. Progress photos from October on a page in February say the project has stalled.

Write the standard list first

Before the model opens, agree the standard-versus-upgrade list with the builder and put it on the page, then put the first progress photos of the released lots beneath the model's gallery. The page becomes the handout, and the weekly photo becomes the follow-up. FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should the property page for a model home include?

The finished model's gallery and video, dated progress photos of the lots actually for sale, a plain list of what is standard and what the model upgraded, the base price for the plan, a realistic timeline, and an enquiry form. The page is the handout, and the progress photos are what you update each week.

How do I handle upgrades in a model home open house?

Say it before they ask. The voiceover in the video names the upgraded items, the page lists standard finishes, and at the island the answer to “is this the kitchen” is “this is the upgraded one; the standard is on the page.” Buyers accept upgrades they were told about and resent the ones they find at contract.

Can I use rendered images on a new construction page?

If the plan is not built yet, renderings may be all there is. Keep them separate from the photos and label them as renderings every time. An image that looks like a photo of a house that does not exist costs you the buyer who later stands on the real lot. The same applies to AI edits of progress photos.

How long should follow-up run after a model home visit?

Months. A visitor in January who did nothing is often the buyer in March. Weekly progress photos on the page give you an honest reason to text them, and the enquiry form with its date field is where they ask for the second walk.
