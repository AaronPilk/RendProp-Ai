Pricing a listing video add-on as a photographer

How to price a photo-based listing video as an add-on to a photo shoot: what the market pays for filmed video, where your cost sits, and three tiers.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Price a photo-based listing video as an add-on below the filmed-video market, not at it. Typical U.S. rates as of August 2026 put filmed listing video at $250–600; a video built from your stills costs you minutes rather than a second visit, so a lower add-on price wins on volume and is an easy yes for the agent on an ordinary listing.

Price a photo-based listing video as an add-on below what the market pays for a filmed one. Typical U.S. rates as of August 2026 put listing video at $250–600 and listing photos at $150–300 per shoot. A video built from your own stills costs you minutes rather than a second visit or an editor, so you can price it where an agent says yes on an ordinary listing without a conversation, and make the margin on volume across the week.

The mistake is pricing by what video used to cost. Price by what it is worth to the agent on that listing and what it costs you to deliver, and show the agent exactly what arrives.

Start from what the agent is comparing you to

The agent has three reference points. A filmed walkthrough from a videographer at $250–600, and a social cutdown from an editor at $75–150, both typical U.S. rates as of August 2026. Doing nothing, which is what most of their listings get. Your add-on sits against all three. If your photo-based video is priced near the filmed walkthrough, the agent will ask why it is not filmed. If it is priced near the cutdown, it reads as a bonus and gets added to most shoots.

So the question is not what video is worth. It is what price makes the add-on a default rather than a decision.

Your cost is time, not kit

Work out your delivered cost honestly. With a photo-based workflow the inputs are:

  • Ten extra minutes on site shooting establishing frames and transitions so the set cuts together.
  • Time to add photos to the listing, review any AI edits against the originals, and build the video.
  • Time to watch the video through, record or script a voiceover, and send the links.
  • Your subscription to whatever tool builds it. Rendprop shows current plan features and included usage in the app, and Apple shows the price and billing period before you confirm.

That is the whole cost. No second shooter, no gimbal, no editor, no render farm. Which means the add-on can be priced to clear comfortably even at a fraction of the filmed-video rate.

Three tiers that are easy to explain

A worked structure for a photographer whose photo shoot sits inside the typical U.S. range of $150–300 as of August 2026:

  1. Photos. The edited set and a published property page with the gallery, the details and the agent's contact card. Base price.
  2. Photos plus landscape video. Everything in tier one plus a 16:9 video for the property page, the MLS link and YouTube. Add a modest amount, well under the filmed-video floor.
  3. Photos plus landscape video plus social reel. Everything in tier two plus a 9:16 vertical video with captions the agent can post the same day. Add a little more.

Leave the exact numbers to your market, but keep the gap between tiers small enough that the top tier looks like the obvious choice on any listing the agent cares about. Agents shown this on the booking form tend to pick tier three for the listings they care most about.

Say what the video is

Write the description so the agent cannot be surprised: a listing video built from the property photos with planned camera motion, a voiceover you record or an AI voice reading a script, word-by-word captions and optional music, exported in 16:9 and 9:16. Not a filmed walkthrough. If the agent wants handheld footage, that is a separate product at a separate price, and the walkthrough versus photo video post explains when it is worth it.

Pricing mistakes that cost you the client

  • Charging per-video prices for a per-listing deliverable. Agents think per listing. Quote per listing.
  • Discounting the first one to zero. It anchors the add-on at nothing. Offer a sample from your own portfolio listing instead.
  • Hiding the add-on in an email. Put it on the booking form where the agent ticks it.
  • Underpricing the social reel. It is the deliverable agents post the same day and show their seller. It carries value beyond its minutes.

Next step: write the three tiers on your booking form with one sentence under each describing exactly what arrives, and test it on your next five bookings.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I price the video add-on near filmed-video rates?

No. Typical U.S. rates as of August 2026 put filmed listing video at $250–600. A photo-based video costs you far less to deliver, so price it where an agent adds it without a second thought and make the margin on volume.

What should the add-on description say?

That the video is built from the listing photos with planned camera motion, a recorded or AI voiceover, word-by-word captions and optional music, exported in 16:9 and 9:16. Make clear it is not a filmed walkthrough.

Is the property page part of the add-on or the base?

Make it part of the base. The page with gallery, details and contact card replaces something agents otherwise pay $9–150 a month for at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, and it makes your base package stand out before the agent considers video.
