Urban condo market marketing: the building sells the unit

In a city condo market the buyer chooses the building before the unit. Show the lobby, the amenities, the view and the walk to the door, then the rooms.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. In an urban condo market the buyer decides on the building before the unit: the block, the lobby, the elevator, the amenities, the view from that floor. Your photos and video have to carry that order of decision, which means the gallery starts outside and on the way up, not in the kitchen. The unit itself is often one of several similar layouts, so the building and the floor are what make it different.

In an urban condo market the building sells the unit. A buyer shortlists by block, then by building, then by floor, and only then compares kitchens. In many buildings there are a dozen units with your layout, so the kitchen is not what makes yours different; the floor, the exposure, the view and the building's common areas are. Market the listing in the order the buyer decides: the street, the entrance, the lobby, the ride up, the hallway, the door, the view, then the rooms.

The buyer's order of questions

A city buyer on a phone at 11pm is asking, in this order: where is it, what is the building like, what floor, which way does it face, what do I see from the window, is there parking, is there a gym or a roof, how is the unit. The MLS answers the first and last of those. Everything in between is yours to show. A buyer who has been in the building before skips to the floor and the view. A buyer who has not needs the whole walk.

That walk is also the part most listings leave out, because the photographer was booked for the unit and the lobby felt like someone else's property. It is not. The buyer is paying for the lobby every month in fees, and they know it.

What goes wrong: a gallery that is all unit

  • Twenty interior photos, zero of the building. The buyer cannot tell your ninth-floor one-bed from the ninth-floor one-bed two doors down, or from the one in the building across the street. Price becomes the only difference.
  • No view photo, or a view photo with blown windows. The view is the thing the floor is paying for. Shoot it at the hour the light is behind you, from the window the buyer will stand at, and include a frame at night if the city lights are the point.
  • Amenities shot empty and cold. A gym at 6am with the lights half on reads as unused. A roof deck with furniture stacked reads as closed. Shoot them as they are used, with the building's permission, at the hour they look alive.
  • The wrong edits. A tidy-the-room edit that removes a radiator or a column changes the layout in the buyer's mind. Virtual staging in a studio that makes the room read larger than it is sets up a disappointing first step through the door. Compare every edit against the original and keep the label on it.
  • Nothing about the walk to the door. Parking, the entrance from the garage, the elevator wait, the hallway. Buyers who have lived in buildings care about this more than about the backsplash.

Example: a one-bed on the ninth floor

A corner one-bed, south and west exposure, in a mid-sized building with a roof deck and a small gym. The photographer arrives in the afternoon and starts outside: the street with the entrance in frame, the lobby, the mailroom, the elevator doors, the ninth-floor hallway, the front door with the unit number. Then inside: the living room with the window, the window itself at 5pm when the sun is to the side, the bedroom, the bath, the kitchen. Up to the roof for three frames with the skyline and the seating in use. Portrait frames of the walk from the lobby to the door for the social video.

The page describes the building, the floor, the exposure and the fees in plain words, and the gallery runs in the order of the walk. The listing video opens on the street, moves through the lobby and up, pauses at the view and finishes in the living room. The main photo is the living room with the view, because that is the one frame that says ninth floor, corner, this building. The social video is the walk to the door with captions that name the block and the floor. The branded link goes in the email; the unbranded link goes in the MLS.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should condo listing photos include the lobby and amenities?

Yes. The buyer is choosing the building before the unit and paying for the common areas in fees every month. Shoot the entrance, lobby, elevator, hallway, roof or gym as they are used, with the building's permission, and put them early in the gallery.

What should the main photo of a city condo be?

Usually the living space with the view in frame, shot at the hour when the light is to the side rather than through the glass. It is the one photo that says which floor, which exposure and which building, and nothing in the kitchen can do that.

How do I make my condo stand out from identical layouts in the same building?

By showing what is different: the floor, the exposure, the view, the condition and the walk to the door. A video that starts on the street and ends at the window tells the story the layout cannot.

Is virtual staging a good idea for a small condo?

It can be, as long as the furniture is sized to the real room and the photo is labelled as virtually staged. Staging that makes a studio read larger than it is produces a disappointing first step through the door. Compare the staged version against the original before you publish.
