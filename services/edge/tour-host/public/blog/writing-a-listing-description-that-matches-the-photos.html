Writing a listing description that matches the photos

Write the description after you pick the photos, in gallery order, so every claim has a photo behind it and every photo that matters gets a line.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

When the text promises a chef's kitchen and photo four shows a 1994 galley, the buyer stops trusting both.

Write the listing description after you have chosen the photos, not before, and write it in the order the gallery runs. Every claim in the text should have a photo behind it, and every photo that carries the sale should get a sentence. When the text and the pictures tell the same story in the same order, a buyer reads both as one honest account of the home.

The usual failure is the other way round. The remarks get written from the listing agreement and the seller's notes on a Tuesday, the photographer shoots on Thursday, and nobody goes back to reconcile the two. The text says “chef's kitchen” and photo four shows a clean but ordinary galley with a 30-inch electric range. The buyer does not think the kitchen is bad. The buyer thinks you exaggerate, and reads the rest of the description with that in mind.

Why the words and the pictures drift apart

Three causes account for most of it. The description is written before the shoot, from memory or from the seller's own list of what they love. The description is adapted from a previous listing, so phrases survive that never applied to this house. Or the photographer made choices the writer never saw: the basement was too dark to shoot, the third bedroom was full of boxes, the back deck turned out to be the best photo in the set.

None of these are writing problems. They are sequencing problems. Fix the order of work and the copy mostly fixes itself.

A method: one line per photo, then cut

Lay the selected photos out on the screen in gallery order. For each one, write a single sentence that says what the buyer is looking at and the one fact that makes it matter. Not an adjective, a fact: the year, the material, the size, the direction the window faces.

  • Front exterior: “A 1962 brick ranch on a level lot, with a new roof in 2023.”
  • Living room: “The living room runs the width of the house, with oak floors under the carpet in the photo and a wood-burning fireplace.”
  • Kitchen: “The kitchen keeps its original layout, with a gas range and a window over the sink facing the back yard.”
  • Primary bedroom: “The primary bedroom is at the back, away from the street, with two closets.”
  • Back yard: “The yard is fenced on three sides and gets afternoon sun.”

You now have twelve or fifteen plain sentences in the right order. Cut the ones about rooms that do not sell the house (the second bathroom is a bathroom). Merge the ones that belong together. Add one opening sentence that says the single most important thing, and one closing sentence about showings. That is the description, and it cannot contradict the photos because it was written from them.

The same house, written twice

Before: “Welcome to this beautifully updated home featuring a gourmet kitchen, spa-like baths and an entertainer's back yard. Move-in ready and close to everything.”

After: “A 1962 brick ranch on a level lot, re-roofed in 2023. The kitchen keeps its original layout and gets a gas range and a window over the sink. Both baths have their 2019 tile and vanities. The fenced back yard faces west and holds a 12 by 16 deck. Showings start Saturday.”

The second version is longer and says less about how to feel. It also matches photos one, four, six, seven and eleven, in that order, which is the point.

Where the text still gets ahead of the photos

  • Rooms you could not shoot. If the finished basement was too dark or too full to photograph, the description can still mention it, but say it plainly: “Finished basement with a second living area, not pictured.” Better still, shoot it.
  • AI edits described as facts about the home. A twilight edit is a photo choice, not evening light the buyer will find. A virtually staged living room is empty. Write about the room, not the edit, and put the disclosure line where the photos are first mentioned. There is more on that in describing AI-edited photos in the listing text.
  • The seller's adjectives. Sellers describe the home they remember. Thank them, then check each phrase against a photo.
  • Features the camera cannot see. New furnace, new water heater, the quiet of the street at night. These belong in the description, but in a separate short paragraph about systems and condition so they do not read as claims the photos should prove.

How this works in Rendprop

On a Rendprop property page, the gallery sits with the details you typed into the listing, a story section for the description, the listing video, and your contact card. The gallery order and the story order are both yours to set, so the one-line-per-photo method carries straight across. If the video has a voiceover, you can record it yourself or have an AI voice read your script, and captions appear word by word, so the same sentences do a third job. Rendprop does not write the description for you; it shows what you wrote next to the photos you chose, which is exactly where mismatches become visible before a buyer sees them.

The next step

Open the photo set for your current listing, put the photos in the order you want a buyer to see them, and write one sentence under each before you touch the remarks field. When the sentences are done, the description is mostly done, and it will match.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should the listing description follow the same order as the photo gallery?

Yes, as closely as the writing allows. Buyers read the remarks and flick through the photos at the same time, and when both move exterior to living room to kitchen to yard, the two feel like one account. The exception is a feature that sells the house but photographs badly, which can move up in the text.

What if the best feature of the home did not photograph well?

Say it in plain words and flag that it is not pictured, or go back and shoot it properly. A sentence like “finished basement with a second living area, not pictured” is honest. A sentence that implies a photo exists when it does not is where trust breaks.

How long should a listing description be?

Long enough to give one sentence to each photo that sells the home, plus an opener and a line about showings. For most homes that lands between 120 and 250 words. Check your MLS's current character limit before you write, and keep a longer version for the property page.

Can I reuse a description from a similar listing?

Reuse the structure, not the sentences. Phrases that survive from a previous home are the most common source of claims the photos cannot back up. Write each line from this listing's photos, even if it takes an extra twenty minutes.
