Salon and spa marketing with a property page

Show a first-time client the space they are nervous about: the chair, the treatment room, the quiet. One link with hours, a video and a date to book.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A salon or spa page works when it shows a first-time client the space they are nervous about: the chair they will sit in, the treatment room they will lie in, and how quiet and clean it is. Put a short video of the rooms on one link with the hours and a form that asks for the date they want, and send that link wherever a new client first finds you.

A property page for a salon or spa does one thing a booking app does not: it shows a first-time client the room before they commit to being in it. The chair at the basin, the treatment room with the door closed, the waiting area, the light.

New clients are nervous in a way regulars forget. A short video of the rooms, on one link with the hours and a form that asks for the date they want, settles that nerve and gets the first appointment booked.

The first-time client is the whole audience

Regulars do not look at your page. They text you. The page is for the person who has just moved to the area, or has been let down by their last salon, or has been given a spa voucher and is deciding where to spend it. That person wants to know three things: is it clean, is it calm, and will I feel looked after. Photos of finished hair and nail art do not answer any of those. Photos of the rooms do.

What to photograph in a salon

  1. The street door and the sign. Shot from the pavement so the client recognises it.
  2. Reception and the waiting area. Clear counter, a chair, the retail shelf tidy. This is where they will stand unsure for thirty seconds on the first visit.
  3. The floor from the door. Wide, level, every station clean, chairs turned the same way.
  4. One station close. The chair, the mirror, the tools laid out. The product, essentially.
  5. The basins. From the side, with the towels folded. People ask about the basins more than you would think.
  6. The colour bar or the nail desk, if you have one. Clean and set up.

What to photograph in a spa

  1. Reception, with the lighting low and warm. Candles if you use them.
  2. The relaxation room. The loungers, the blankets, the water. This photo sells the visit.
  3. A treatment room, bed made, towels rolled, lamp on. From the door. Not from above.
  4. Changing room and showers. Clean, lit, empty.
  5. Any wet facilities. Sauna, steam, pool. Shot with the door open and the lights on.
  6. The thing that is yours. The garden, the view, the fireplace, the tea.

Shoot a salon in the morning with the lights on and the blinds open. Shoot a spa the way it looks during a treatment: low, warm, no overheads. Hold the phone still against a door frame for the dark rooms rather than turning the lights up.

Worked example: a six-chair salon with two treatment rooms

A salon with six chairs, two basins and two treatment rooms at the back shoots at 8:30am on a Tuesday before the first client. Nine landscape photos: the door, reception, the floor from the door, one chair close, the basins, the colour bar, treatment room one with the bed made, treatment room two set for a facial, the small garden out the back where clients wait on a sunny day. The listing video opens on the door, moves through reception to the floor, pauses at a chair, shows the basins, goes down the corridor to the treatment rooms and ends in the garden. Forty seconds with a plain voiceover: who they are, the hours, and that new clients get a consultation before the first cut. The page shows the hours by day, the gallery, the video on request, a contact card and a form that asks for the date the visitor would like. The first enquiry is from someone who just moved to the street and wants a Saturday morning; she mentions the garden.

Hours and the booking question

Hours by day near the top of the page, including the late evening if you have one. Prices are better left to your booking system or the consultation; a price list on the page goes stale and invites comparison on the one number that does not reflect the work. The enquiry form asks for the date, which is the right first question for a salon or spa; the service can be sorted in the reply.

What goes wrong on salon and spa pages

  • Only work photos. Finished hair, nails, lashes. Fine for Instagram; useless for showing someone the room.
  • Products everywhere. A retail shelf with sixty bottles reads as clutter. Tidy it or crop it.
  • Overhead lighting in the spa. Shoot in the light a client experiences.
  • A treatment room shot from the doorway with the bed unmade. Make it as you would for a client.
  • AI adding plants, candles or furniture. The client is coming to this exact room. Edits that change it cost trust on arrival. Keep any edit disclosed and compared with the original, and prefer moving the real plant.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a salon page show instead of finished hair?

The rooms. The chair, the basins, the treatment room with the bed made, reception and the waiting area. A first-time client wants to see where they will sit and whether it is clean and calm. Keep the work photos for Instagram.

How should I light a spa for photos?

The way a client experiences it: low and warm, overheads off, lamps and candles on. Hold the phone still against a door frame for the darker rooms rather than brightening them. Daylight and fluorescents make a spa look like a clinic.

Should prices be on the page?

Usually not. A price list goes stale and invites comparison on the one number that does not reflect the work. Keep prices in your booking system or the consultation, and let the page do the job of showing the space and taking the date.
