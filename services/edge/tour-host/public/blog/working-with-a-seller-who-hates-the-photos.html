Working with a seller who hates the photos

When a seller hates the photos, find the cause: one room, the light, their belongings or the house. Fix the first three honestly; say so about the fourth.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

When a seller hates the photos, find out exactly what they hate before you change anything. It is almost always one of four things: one room, the light, their own belongings in frame, or the house itself. The first three have fixes that keep the listing honest: a reshoot of one room, a different main photo, a labelled tidy-the-room edit. The fourth cannot be fixed with photography, and the kindest thing you can do is say so, because the alternative is a listing that misleads buyers and disappoints at the showing.

Ask them to point, not describe

A seller who says the photos are terrible usually means two photos. Open the gallery on your phone, hand it to them, and ask them to stop on the ones that bother them and say why in one sentence each. Write the sentences down. Then ask which photo they would be proud to send to their sister. That one is often the new main photo, and the complaint list is usually three items long, not thirty. Doing this in person or on a call, with the gallery in front of you both, prevents a week of emails.

Four causes and four fixes

  • One room. The kitchen looks small, the bedroom looks dark, the bathroom shows the toilet first. Reshoot that room at a better hour, from a better corner, with the lamps on. One room, one return visit, done.
  • The light. Everything looks grey. The shoot was at the wrong hour or with the blinds half-closed. This is a reshoot of the main rooms at a better time of day, and it is the photographer's call to make right.
  • Their belongings. The seller is embarrassed by the stack of mail, the dog bed, the fridge magnets. For small things, a tidy-the-room edit with its disclosure, compared with the original, is honest: it removes clutter without changing the room. For a room full of furniture they hate, a return visit after they clear it beats any edit.
  • The house. The kitchen is dated, the ceilings are low, the lot backs onto the road. The photos are accurate. Say so: the photos show the house as a buyer will find it, and the price will do the work the photos cannot. A seller who hears this early is a seller who prices well.

Edits you can offer and edits you should refuse

Offer: a blue sky on an overcast exterior, labelled. Tidy the room for clutter, labelled, with the original compared and kept. Virtual staging in an empty room, labelled as virtually staged. A twilight conversion of the front if the street looks better at dusk, labelled. Refuse: anything that changes the room. Widening a kitchen, removing a wall, adding a window, taking the power lines out of the view, making a low ceiling look higher. Those are not edits of a photo; they are edits of the house, and the showing reveals them. Check your MLS rules on altered images for anything you do offer.

A 4-bed and a seller who hates the kitchen shot

A 4-bed colonial, good photos overall, and a seller who texts at 10 p.m. that the photos are awful and she wants them all redone. The next morning you sit with her and the gallery. She stops on three: the kitchen, which was shot from the doorway and looks narrow; the main bedroom, which shows her laundry basket; and the front, which was overcast. The rest she likes, and the living room is the one she would send to her sister. Fixes: the photographer returns Thursday evening for the kitchen from the far corner with the pendants on, and shoots the front at dusk while there. The laundry basket goes with a tidy-the-room edit, labelled, compared against the original, and she approves it on the spot. The living room becomes the main photo. Three fixes, one return visit, and the listing launches Monday with a seller who now sends the link to everyone. What she hated was three photos, not the set.

Conversations that go badly

  • Agreeing to a full reshoot before finding out which photos are the problem.
  • Using an AI edit to hide a defect the seller is sensitive about. The buyer finds it; the seller blames you.
  • Arguing about taste. If the seller hates the angle, it is a cheap fix; do it.
  • Letting the launch slip two weeks while the seller thinks about it. Fix the three photos and launch.
  • Promising the photos can make the house something it is not. That conversation is about price, and it is better had now.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I reshoot everything if the seller asks?

Not before finding out which photos are the problem. Sit with the seller and the gallery and ask them to point. The list is usually two or three photos, and those can be reshot in one return visit or fixed with a labelled edit.

Can I use an AI edit to fix what the seller does not like?

For clutter, an overcast sky, or an empty room, yes, with the label on and the original compared and kept. For anything about the house itself, a small kitchen, a low ceiling, a road behind the fence, no. Those are showings waiting to go wrong.

What if the seller hates the photos because they hate how the house looks?

Say so, kindly. The photos show the house as a buyer will find it, and the price is what does the work the photos cannot. A seller who hears that early prices well; one who hears it after three reshoots does not.

How do I stop this delaying the launch?

Do the gallery review with the seller within a day of delivery, book the one return visit immediately, and launch on the original date. Three fixes and a Thursday evening should not move a Monday launch.
