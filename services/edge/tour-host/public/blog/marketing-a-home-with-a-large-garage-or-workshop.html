Marketing a home with a large garage or workshop

A big garage or shop sells on dimensions, door height, power, heat and floor. Clear it, shoot from the door, photograph the panel, list the numbers.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A large garage or workshop sells on numbers: interior dimensions, ceiling and door height, power with the panel and 220-volt outlets, heat and insulation, floor finish and drainage, and whether a boat or RV fits through the door. Clear it enough to see the floor and the walls, shoot it from the open door with the house in frame and from inside looking out, photograph the panel and the lift, and put every number on the page.

A large garage or workshop sells on numbers, and the buyer for it will check every one: interior width and depth, ceiling height, door height and width, amperage at the panel and where the 220-volt outlets are, heat and insulation, the floor finish and whether there is a drain, and if a boat, a trailer or an RV will get through the door. The photos prove the space; the page carries the figures. Most listings say three-car garage with workshop and show a frame full of boxes. Clear it, measure it, and treat it as a room.

Shooting the shop

  1. Clear it first. Not empty, but enough that the floor, the walls and the ceiling are visible. Vehicles out, benches cleared, tools on their hooks, bins stacked along one wall or gone.
  2. From the driveway with the doors open and the house in frame, so the buyer sees scale and how the shop relates to the house.
  3. From the open door looking in, lights on, so depth reads. A tape measure stretched along the floor in one frame is not elegant and is often the most useful photo in the set.
  4. From the back wall looking out through the door, for door size and the driveway beyond.
  5. The panel, open, with the breakers labelled, and the 220-volt outlets, the compressor lines, the dust collection if it stays.
  6. The lift, the pit, the drain, the floor finish, close.
  7. Heating and insulation: the unit heater, the mini-split, the insulated door, the finished ceiling.
  8. Storage: attic access, mezzanine, cabinets that convey.
  9. The approach: driveway width, turning room, side access to a rear shop, gate width.
  10. The house, in the usual order, before or after the shop depending on which is the headline for the likely buyer.

In the listing video, the shop deserves two or three scenes, not one, when it is the reason the house is different from the one next door. A long room reads deeper when both ends are in the set, so include the frame from each. A short handheld walkthrough from the driveway into the shop and back to the kitchen door tells the buyer how far they will carry things. For the vertical social video, the frame from the open door looking in, in portrait, works because a tall door and a long floor both fit.

Worked example: a 3-bed with a 30-by-40 detached shop

A 3-bed, 2-bath ranch on half an acre with a detached 30-by-40 shop built in 2016: 12-foot ceilings, a 10-by-12 insulated door, a 100-amp subpanel, two 220-volt outlets, a gas unit heater, an epoxy floor with a center drain, a two-post lift that conveys, and a 12-foot-wide gravel side drive with a gate. The seller moves the truck and the project car out on a Saturday morning, sweeps, and hangs the tools. The agent shoots at 10 a.m.: the shop from the drive with both doors up and the house to the left, from the door looking in with a tape stretched along the floor, from the back wall looking out, the subpanel open and labelled, the two outlets, the lift, the drain and the epoxy, the heater, the attic ladder, and the side drive from the street through the gate. Then the house. Sixteen landscape frames make the listing video, with the shop frames in the set because the likely buyer is coming for the shop. The page lists every number above, and the open-door frame is the main photo.

What the page should list

  • Interior dimensions, ceiling height, door width and height, number of bays.
  • Attached or detached, year built, construction.
  • Power: amperage at the subpanel, 220-volt outlets and where, lighting type.
  • Heat, cooling, insulation in walls, ceiling and door.
  • Floor finish, drain, lift, pit, compressor and lines, dust collection, and which of these convey.
  • Water: a sink, a hose bib, a half bath.
  • Access: driveway width, side drive, gate width, turning room for a trailer.
  • Storage above and around.
  • What is allowed on the property for business use, only as the seller's documents or the local authority state it, with a note to verify; ask your broker how to word it.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet for the house in the details fields; shop square footage in the text so it is not confused with living space.

The buyer for this listing may be the same person who reads warehouse and workshop marketing; the questions are the same, scaled down.

Where shop listings go wrong

  • Boxes to the ceiling. The buyer cannot see the room. Clearing it is the single highest-value hour in the whole shoot.
  • A tidy edit that removes a post, a slope or a stain. A support post in the middle of a shop is a fact a buyer plans around. Compare any edit against the original and keep the disclosure; in a shop, the honest frame is usually the better one. The post on tidy the room covers the line.
  • No door height. The RV owner will not call to ask; they move on.
  • Shop square footage folded into the house. Keep them separate.
  • Stating what business use is allowed. Say what the documents say and tell the buyer to verify.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What numbers does a workshop listing need?

Interior dimensions, ceiling height, door width and height, amperage at the panel, 220-volt outlets, heat and insulation, floor finish and drain, and what conveys. Put them all on the page; the buyer for a shop reads numbers before photos.

Should the garage be empty for photos?

Clear enough to see the floor, walls and ceiling: vehicles out, benches cleared, tools hung. It does not need to be empty, but a buyer cannot judge a room they cannot see.

Can I use a tidy edit on a cluttered garage photo?

Clearing it in person is better. If you use an edit, compare it against the original to be sure it has not removed a post, a slope or a stain, and keep the AI-alteration disclosure on it.

Does the shop square footage go in the square feet field?

No. Keep the house figure in the details field and give the shop's dimensions and square footage in the text, so living space and shop space are never added together.
