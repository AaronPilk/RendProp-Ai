Showing feedback: what to ask and what to change

Ask three questions after every showing. When the first answer repeats, change the marketing. When the third repeats, talk to the seller about price.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Ask three things after every showing: what the buyer expected from the photos that the house did not deliver, what surprised them in a good way, and whether they would make an offer at any price. When the first answer repeats, the marketing is wrong and you fix the photos, the video or the description. When the third answer repeats, the price is wrong and that is a conversation with the seller.

Ask three questions after every showing. What did the buyer expect from the photos that the house did not deliver. What surprised them in a good way. Would they make an offer, and at what number. The first answer tells you whether the marketing matches the house. The second tells you what the marketing is missing. The third tells you about the price.

Then follow a simple rule. When the first answer repeats across showings, change the marketing. When the third answer repeats, talk to the seller about price. Do not do either after one comment.

Why “any feedback?” gets you nothing

Buyer's agents are busy and polite. “Any feedback?” gets “nice house, not for them.” It is true and useless. A specific question gets a specific answer because it gives the agent something to pass on. “Was there anything the photos led them to expect that was not there?” is easy to answer and the answer is usually the one thing you need to hear.

Ask by text, within an hour of the showing ending, while the agent is still in the car with the buyer. Three short questions, numbered. Agents answer numbered questions.

The split-level on Fernwood

A split-level on Fernwood Drive, four bedrooms, priced in line with the street. Six showings in the first nine days, no second visits. The feedback, when asked properly:

  • Four of six: “smaller than it looked in the photos.” Specifically the living room and the main bedroom.
  • Three of six: “the garden was a nice surprise,” and “the view from the kitchen window.”
  • Offer question: two said the price was fine for the right buyer, four did not engage.

Read that. The price is not the complaint; the gap between photo and room is. The living room and the bedroom were shot at the widest lens from the corner, and the photos promised rooms that do not exist. Buyers walked in and felt cheated before they reached the kitchen, so they never got to the garden, which is the thing they liked.

The fix is marketing, not price. Reshoot the two rooms from a normal focal length, or pick the less dramatic frames already in the set. Make the garden and the kitchen view the second and third photos instead of the eleventh and twelfth. Lead the video with the kitchen window and the garden so the buyer arrives wanting those and finds the rooms are, simply, rooms.

Two weeks later the same house with the same price and a rearranged set gets second showings. Nothing about the house changed.

What each answer tells you to change

  • “Expected more from the photos.” Wide-angle distortion, a staged photo next to an empty room, a twilight exterior that oversold the lighting, or a description that used words the house does not earn. Fix the photos and the words.
  • “Did not realise it had…” The thing they liked was buried. Move it up the gallery, put it in the first ten seconds of the video, lead the description with it.
  • “Layout did not work.” Nothing to change in the photos; the video should show the flow honestly so the wrong buyers filter out before they book.
  • “Price” from most agents. Now it is a seller conversation, with the feedback log in hand.
  • Silence. No answer is an answer. If half the agents do not reply, the showing was forgettable, which usually means the house is fine and the price is high.

How agents get this wrong

  • Changing the price after one comment. One agent's opinion is one agent's buyer. Wait for a pattern of three or four.
  • Ignoring the gap. When buyers say the rooms felt smaller, the instinct is to defend the photos. The photos are the problem.
  • Fixing two things at once. A price cut and new photos in the same week tells you nothing about which one worked.
  • Not telling the seller the specifics. “Feedback has been positive” is not a report. “Four of six said the living room photo oversold the space; here is what changes Thursday” is.
  • Asking only after the open house. Private showings produce the honest answers, because there is one buyer and one agent and no crowd.

How this works in Rendprop

When the fix is marketing, it is a return to the listing rather than a new project. Choose a different main photo, reorder which photos are used, and if you made AI edits, compare each one against the original again; the untouched original stays in photo history, so a wide, bright edit that oversold a room can be swapped for the plain frame. Create a new listing video from the reselected photos and publish the property website again. Showing requests that come through the page arrive in the inbox with the buyer's preferred date, which makes them easy to log alongside the feedback. See the photo studio and the property website.

Start with the next showing

Save the three questions as a text template and send it within the hour, every time. Keep the answers in one note per listing. After four showings, read the note and decide whether it is the marketing or the price. If it is the photos, which photo should be the main photo of a listing is the first thing to revisit.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What are the best questions to ask for showing feedback?

Three: what the buyer expected from the photos that the house did not deliver, what surprised them in a good way, and whether they would make an offer at any price. Send them by text within an hour of the showing, numbered, so the agent can answer quickly from the car.

How many showings should I wait before changing something?

Three or four with the same answer. One agent's comment is one buyer's taste. When the same gap between photos and house comes up repeatedly, change the marketing. When the price comes up repeatedly, bring the log to the seller.

Buyers keep saying the rooms are smaller than the photos. Is that a price problem?

Usually not. It is a photo problem, often wide-angle frames shot from the corner, or a staged image next to an empty room. Replace the frames that oversell, move the features buyers liked higher in the gallery, and lead the video with them. Then see whether second showings appear before touching the price.

Should I change the price and the photos at the same time?

No. Change one and wait a week. If both change together you cannot tell which one produced the second showings, and the next listing gets the wrong lesson.
