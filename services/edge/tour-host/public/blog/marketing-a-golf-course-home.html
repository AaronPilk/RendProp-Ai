Marketing a golf course home

A golf course home sells on which hole it backs onto and what the patio sees. Shoot the fairway early, name the hole, and do not hide the cart path.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A golf course home sells on two things: which hole and which part of it the lot backs onto, and what the back patio actually sees. Shoot the fairway from the house early in the morning before play, state the hole and the membership situation plainly, and show the cart path and the screening rather than cropping them out.

A golf course home is bought for the view down the fairway and for a membership the buyer may or may not want, so the marketing has to show the first and be honest about the second. Shoot the course from the back of the house early in the morning before play starts, name the hole and the part of the hole the lot sits on, and show the cart path, the screening and the distance to the tee box as they are. A buyer who has looked at three fairway homes already knows the questions.

Mistakes that cost a fairway listing a showing

  • Shooting during play. Golfers in every frame, a cart parked in the view, and you cannot publish faces without thinking about it. Book the shoot for first light or after the course closes.
  • Editing the course. The club already keeps the fairway green. An AI green lawn edit that bleeds onto the course makes the one honest part of the photo look fake. Keep lawn edits to the seller's own yard and carry the disclosure, as covered in green lawn edits.
  • Cropping out the cart path. If the path runs twenty feet from the patio, every buyer will see it at the showing. Show it in a frame and say so in the text.
  • Implying membership conveys. State what the seller and the club tell you about membership, and nothing more. If you are unsure how to word it, ask your broker.
  • Leading with the front door. On a golf course lot the front of the house is the least interesting side.

Photos, in the order a golfer would look

  1. From the patio down the fairway. The first frame and probably the main photo. Chairs out, cushions on, early light.
  2. From the fairway back at the house. Ask the pro shop before you step onto the course. Stand where a player would and show the rear of the home in context.
  3. The best room facing the course. Usually the family room or kitchen. Window pull so the green reads outside.
  4. The outdoor living detail. Grill, covered area, fire feature, pool if there is one.
  5. The side yard and screening. Hedges, fence, netting. Buyers who have lived on a course want to see what stands between the patio and a sliced drive.
  6. Kitchen, primary suite, baths, the rest. Normal order, course-facing rooms shot toward the window.
  7. Garage, including the cart bay if there is one.
  8. Front exterior and streetscape.
  9. Clubhouse and amenities. With the club's permission, two or three frames at most.

Video follows the same logic: open on the fairway from the patio, cut to the room that faces it, walk through the house, step outside again at the end. Portrait versions of the patio view and the window shot carry the vertical social video. If the course has water or bunkers in view, give them a frame of their own so the video does not rush past them.

The page: hole, position, membership, screening

Put the course facts in the first lines of the listing text, in this order:

  • Which course and which hole, and where along it: tee side, mid-fairway, green side.
  • Direction the patio faces and when it gets sun and shade.
  • What the seller and the club say about membership availability, categories and whether anything transfers. Quote the club's wording rather than your own.
  • Cart path distance and screening.
  • Outdoor living: covered square footage, pool, kitchen.
  • Whether the community is gated; the related post on marketing a home in a gated community covers the access side.

Price, beds, baths and square feet sit in the details fields. The course facts are text, and they go above the finishes.

Worked example: a 4-bed on the seventh green

A 4-bed, 3-bath on a green-side lot of the seventh hole, west-facing patio, cart path along the left boundary behind a hedge. The agent gets the pro shop's go-ahead to shoot from the fringe at 6:45 a.m. on a Monday, the course's maintenance day. The set runs: patio down the fairway with the flag visible, from the fringe back at the house, family room toward the slider with the green outside, the covered patio with the grill, the hedge line with the path visible beyond it, kitchen, primary bedroom toward its window, three baths, two more bedrooms, the three-car garage with the cart bay, the front, and two frames of the clubhouse. Fourteen landscape photos make the listing video, opening on the patio. The text reads: green side of the seventh, west-facing, social and golf memberships available by separate application per the club, cart path behind the hedge on the north boundary. The patio frame is the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

When should a golf course home be photographed?

First light before play begins, or on the course's maintenance day. The fairway is mowed, there are no golfers in frame and the early sun is low across the grass. Ask the pro shop before stepping onto the course.

Should the listing say which hole the house backs onto?

Yes, and where along the hole: tee side, mid-fairway or green side. Buyers who know the course care about the difference, and buyers who do not will ask. Put it in the first lines of the text.

How do I describe club membership on the page?

Use the club's own wording about what is available and whether anything transfers, and state that it comes from the club. Do not imply membership conveys with the house. If the wording is unclear, ask your broker.

Should I use a green lawn edit on a golf course listing?

Only on the seller's own yard, and only if it is brown. Never edit the course itself. Any edit you keep needs the AI-alteration disclosure and a check against the original.
