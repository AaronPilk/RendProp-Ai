Listing photo order: the sequence that keeps buyers scrolling

Order the gallery the way a buyer walks the house: exterior, living, kitchen, dining, primary, baths, other beds, yard. Three rules that override it.

Order the gallery the way a buyer would walk the house: front exterior, entry or living room, kitchen, dining, primary bedroom, primary bath, the other bedrooms, the other baths, then the yard and anything extra. Put the three strongest frames in the first five positions, keep two shots of the same room apart, and end on the rear exterior or the view so the last thing a buyer sees is the reason to book. A gallery that jumps from kitchen to basement to bedroom loses buyers at the jump.

Why the walk order works

A buyer scrolling a gallery is building a mental map of the house. If the photos arrive in the order they would see the rooms on a tour, the map builds itself and every frame adds to it. If the photos arrive in the photographer's shooting order or by file name, the buyer has to place each frame, and after a few they stop trying.

The walk order also front-loads the rooms that decide a showing. Exterior, living, kitchen and primary are in the first six frames. A buyer who leaves after six has seen the house.

The sequence, with the rules that go with it

  1. Front exterior, three-quarter angle. The main photo. If a twilight edit is used anywhere, it is here.
  2. Entry or the first living space. Whatever the buyer sees when the door opens.
  3. Living room, the wide frame.
  4. Kitchen, from the entry.
  5. Kitchen, down the island. The only room that gets two frames back to back, and only because the second shows finish the first cannot.
  6. Dining.
  7. Primary bedroom.
  8. Primary bath.
  9. Second living space, office or family room, if there is one.
  10. Bedrooms two, three, four, each one frame.
  11. Hall bath and any other bath.
  12. Laundry, garage, basement, storage. Buyers who get this far want them.
  13. Rear exterior, deck, yard, pool.
  14. The view, if there is one, as the closing frame.

Three rules that override the sequence

The strongest frame goes early. If the house has a remarkable room, a glass-walled family room or a chef's kitchen, it moves up to position two or three regardless of where it sits on the walk. The walk order is the default, not a law.

Same room, not adjacent. Two bedroom frames back to back read as a stutter. If the primary needs two frames, put the bath between them.

Utility rooms late. No buyer books a showing from the laundry room. It goes after every room they would.

A worked example: the 41-frame shoot

A 4-bed colonial on a corner lot. The photographer delivers 41 frames in shooting order, which began in the kitchen because the morning light was there. The agent re-orders: three-quarter exterior, straight-on exterior, entry, living wide, living toward the kitchen, kitchen from the entry, kitchen down the island, dining with the slider, primary bed, primary bath, bed two, bed three, bed four, hall bath, laundry, garage, deck, yard from the deck, yard back toward the house. Twenty-one frames used in the gallery, twenty held back as duplicates or details.

The listing video uses twelve of those twenty-one, in the same order. A buyer who watches the video and then scrolls the gallery sees the same house twice in the same sequence, which is reassuring rather than repetitive.

Orders that lose the scroll

  • Opening with an interior on a house whose exterior is fine. The buyer cannot place the room.
  • Three exteriors in a row. One, maybe two, then inside.
  • Every bathroom together in the middle.
  • Details scattered through the set. A faucet between two bedrooms breaks the map.
  • The yard before the kitchen. The buyer has not seen the house yet.
  • Ending on the garage.

Re-order before you upload anywhere

Do the ordering once, in the camera roll or a folder, before the photos go to the MLS, the property page or the app. Rename them with a number if it helps. FAQ

Questions on this topic

What order should listing photos be in?

The order a buyer would walk the house: front exterior, entry or living room, kitchen, dining, primary bedroom, primary bath, other bedrooms, other baths, utility rooms, then the rear exterior and yard. Close on the view if there is one.

Should the best photo always be first?

The front exterior is usually first because it tells the buyer what the property is. If the house has one remarkable room, move it to position two or three. Keep the three strongest frames in the first five positions.

Can two photos of the same room be next to each other?

Avoid it, with one exception: the kitchen from the entry and then down the island, because the second shows finish the first cannot. For any other room, put a different space between the two frames.

Where should the backyard photos go?

After the interior, before the view. A buyer who sees the yard before the kitchen has not seen the house yet. Ending on the deck or the yard leaves the buyer with the reason to book.
