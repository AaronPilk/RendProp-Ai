Marketing budget per listing: where the money actually goes

A per-listing marketing budget split into four buckets (media, page, distribution, time), two worked budgets for one 3-bed, and the leaks that drain both.

Short answer. A per-listing marketing budget goes to four places: media buyers see (photos, video, edits), the page that holds it, distribution (print, promotion) and your time. Media is the only bucket buyers ever look at, so it gets the first dollar. The leaks are monthly fees that outlive the listing and vendor handoffs that cost days.

A listing marketing budget goes to four places: media (photos, video, edits), the page that holds it and its hosting, distribution (printing, any promotion you put behind a post), and your own time. Media is the only bucket a buyer ever sees, so it should get the first dollar. The other three are where budgets quietly leak. Here is how a typical budget splits at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, and two versions of the same listing's budget side by side.

Bucket one: media buyers look at

At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, this is the photo shoot at $150–300, a listing video at $250–600 if filmed separately, an aerial session at $150–350, virtual staging at $5–15 per photo and twilight conversions at $5–25 per photo. The photo line is close to mandatory. Everything else in this bucket should be a decision per home: staging when rooms are empty, twilight when the street looks better at dusk, aerial when the lot is the story. A video filmed separately is the line most often paid out of habit rather than need, because a listing video can be built from the photo set for most homes.

Bucket two: the page that holds it

A single-property site runs $9–150 per month at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026. The page matters because it is where the enquiry form lives and where the branded link points, but a monthly fee for one address is a strange shape for a cost. It bills by the month while the listing sells by the week, and it keeps billing after closing unless someone cancels. Agents who list often can end up paying for a dozen dormant pages. If the page can come with the listing media instead of as a subscription beside it, this bucket shrinks to nothing on its own line.

Bucket three: distribution

A one-page flyer with a QR code, a sign rider, and whatever you choose to put behind a social post. The flyer and the rider are cheap and useful. Paid promotion is worth it only after an organic post has shown the hook works, and the amount is yours to set; there is no typical figure worth quoting. A marketing agency on retainer at $2,500–5,000 per month, typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, belongs in this bucket too, and it buys your brand's presence across all listings, not the launch of one address.

Bucket four: your time

Unbilled and usually unmeasured. Count it anyway: the hour chasing a video delivery, the evening rebuilding a page because the price changed, the three messages to find the unbranded link. A listing that takes six hours of coordination to launch cost six hours of your showings. Most of that time is handoffs between vendors, which is why collapsing vendors saves more than it appears to on the invoice.

Two budgets for the same 3-bed

A 3-bed, 2-bath on a corner lot, two empty bedrooms, an overcast week.

  • Budget A, separate vendors: photos $150–300, a filmed video $250–600, an aerial session $150–350, staging two rooms at $5–15 each, twilight on one exterior at $5–25, a reels edit $75–150, a site at $9–150 for the month, all at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026. Seven lines, four or five vendors, and a launch that waits for the slowest of them.
  • Budget B, one photo set: photos $150–300, then the listing video, the vertical cut, the staging and the twilight produced from that set with each edit compared against its original and labelled, and the page published from the same listing. The photographer is the only outside invoice, plus whatever the app's plan costs, and the launch waits for nobody.

The difference buyers see between A and B is small if both are done well. The difference in days to launch and hours of coordination is not.

Leaks to plug

  • Page fees after closing. Put the cancellation date in your calendar the day you publish, or use a page that comes with the listing and can be marked sold.
  • Aerial by default. Stand on the street first. If the lot cannot be seen from above without three neighbours in frame, skip it.
  • Re-edits. Every round trip to a vendor for a floating sofa or a purple sky is a day. Reviewing edits yourself against the originals, immediately, is cheaper.
  • Printing before the price is final. Flyers with a crossed-out number look like what they are.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What is the one line in a listing budget not to cut?

The photo shoot, at $150–300 at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026. Every other piece, video, page, staging, social, is built from or depends on the photos. A weak photo set makes everything downstream weaker.

Should I budget for a filmed listing video on every home?

No. A listing video built from the photo set covers the page, the MLS and social for most homes. Budget for a separately filmed or recorded walkthrough only when the home's flow is the selling point and stills do not carry it.

How much should I spend on promoting a post?

Only what you decide after an organic post has shown the hook works. There is no typical figure worth quoting, and promotion behind a post nobody engaged with organically is the most common wasted line.

Why count my own time in the budget?

Because coordination between vendors is the largest hidden cost. Six hours chasing deliveries and rebuilding a page is six hours of showings. Fewer handoffs is usually worth more than a cheaper vendor.
