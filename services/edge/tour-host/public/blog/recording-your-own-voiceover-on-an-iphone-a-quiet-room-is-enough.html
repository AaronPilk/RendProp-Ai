Recording a voiceover on an iPhone: a quiet room is enough

You do not need a microphone to record a listing voiceover. A quiet room with soft surfaces, the phone a hand's width away and three takes is enough.

You do not need a microphone to record a listing voiceover. You need a quiet room with soft surfaces, the phone held about a hand's width from your mouth, and three takes read a touch slower than feels natural. The room does more for the sound than any piece of equipment. A parked car with the engine off, a walk-in closet or a bedroom with the curtains drawn will each give you a cleaner recording than an open office with a good mic.

Why the room matters more than the mic

Most of what makes a home recording sound amateur is not the microphone. It is echo. Hard floors, bare walls and big windows bounce your voice back a fraction of a second late, and the phone picks up both. The result sounds like a bathroom. Carpet, curtains, a bed, hanging clothes and upholstered furniture absorb that bounce, and the same phone suddenly sounds close and warm.

The second thing is background noise. A fridge, a fan, traffic through a window, a laptop cooling itself. You stop hearing these after a minute in a room; the recording does not. Turn off what you can and move away from what you cannot.

Setting up in two minutes

  • Pick the room. The softest, smallest, quietest one. Closets are good. Cars are better than most rooms because they are padded on every side.
  • Hold the phone, do not prop it. About a hand's width from your mouth, slightly off to the side so your breath does not hit the mic straight on.
  • Silence the phone. Notifications arriving mid-sentence are the most common reason for a fourth take.
  • Have the script in front of you on paper or a second screen, in large type, so your head does not drop as you read.
  • Record ten seconds of nothing first. Play it back with the volume up. If you hear a hum or a hiss, find it before you read.

Reading the script

Read it slower than feels right. Nerves speed people up, and a listing script read at conversation speed often lands five seconds short of what the photos need. Pause at the end of each sentence for a breath. Those pauses are where the next photo arrives, and they make the word-by-word captions easier to follow.

Smile on the opening line. It is a cliche because it works: the voice lifts and the first three seconds sound like someone who likes the house.

Do three takes and stop. The first is a warm-up, the second is usually the one, and the third is insurance. Ten takes make you worse, not better, because you start listening to yourself instead of talking about the home.

A worked example: the Tuesday morning recording

You have a reel to post before an open house. The office is loud. You walk to the car, park in the shade, turn the engine off and close the windows. Phone on silent, script in a note on the passenger seat in 24-point type. Ten seconds of silence, played back: clean. Three takes of a 68-word script, each about 29 seconds. The second take has a slight stumble on the street name; the third is clean. You use the third. Total time, six minutes, including the walk.

Three things that ruin a take

  • Hard rooms. Kitchens, bathrooms and empty listings. Tile and glass everywhere. If you must record on site, do it from inside the car in the driveway.
  • Plosives. Words starting with p and b hitting the mic straight on make a thump. Angle the phone off to the side of your mouth, not in front of it.
  • Reading, not talking. If it sounds read, it is because you are watching the words. Look up at the end of each sentence as if the buyer is across the table, then look down for the next one.

When to skip your voice

If you have a cold, if the only room available is a hard one, or if you are producing reels for other agents, an AI voice reading the same script is the better call for that video. Nothing about the script changes. The trade-offs are in AI voice or your voice.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Do I need an external microphone to record a listing voiceover on an iPhone?

No. A quiet room with soft surfaces does more for the sound than a microphone does. Echo and background noise are what make a recording sound amateur, and a closet, a bedroom with curtains or a parked car fixes both.

How far should the phone be from my mouth?

About a hand's width, held slightly off to the side so your breath does not hit the microphone directly. Hold it rather than propping it, so the distance stays constant through the take.

How many takes should I record?

Three. The first is a warm-up, the second is usually the keeper and the third is insurance. More takes tend to make you self-conscious and the reading gets stiffer.

Can I record the voiceover at the listing?

Empty homes are hard rooms full of echo, so avoid recording in them. If you are on site, record from inside your car in the driveway with the engine off and the windows closed.
