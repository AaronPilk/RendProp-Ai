Reviewing AI video for property details before publishing

A listing video can carry a wrong room order, an unlabelled staged photo, a script that misstates the baths or a crop that hides a feature. Three passes.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Watch the video twice before it goes anywhere: once with the sound off, checking which photo versions went in, the scene order and the crops; once with the sound on, checking every fact the voiceover and captions state against the listing details. Then watch the first three seconds a third time, because that is all most viewers see.

Watch the listing video twice before it goes anywhere: first with the sound off, checking which photo versions went in, whether the scene order makes sense for this home, and whether the 9:16 crop hid anything; second with the sound on, checking every fact the voiceover and the captions state against the listing details you typed. Then watch the first three seconds a third time, because for most viewers that is the whole video. Fifteen minutes for a thirty-second reel is not too much.

Pass one: sound off, eyes on the stills

Which versions went in

The video is built from the photo versions you selected. If you accepted a staged living room, the staged version is in the video, and it needs its label in the caption or on screen. If you rejected a twilight edit but it ended up in the video anyway, that is a selection error to fix before publishing. Pause on each scene and name the version: original, staged, sky, tidy.

Scene order

Automatic ordering is usually sensible: exterior, main living spaces, kitchen, bedrooms, bathrooms, outdoor, close. Check it against this home. A condo with a view should not bury the view at scene nine. A home where the kitchen is the sale should get there early. If the automatic order misses the point of this home, change the photo selection so the point comes through, and check the result again.

Camera motion and what it reveals

A slow push across a photo magnifies the centre of the frame. If the centre is the one spot where an AI edit went wrong, the video will show it larger than the still did. Watch the push on every edited scene.

The vertical crop

A 16:9 photo cropped to 9:16 loses the sides. Sometimes the sides had the window, the fireplace or the door to the yard. Check that the vertical version still shows what the scene is for. If the kitchen scene in 9:16 is a close-up of a cabinet, swap the photo or accept that the reel and the listing video will differ.

Pass two: sound on, facts against details

The voiceover and the word-by-word captions are claims. Every number and every adjective gets checked against what you typed on the property page.

  • Beds, baths, square feet, price. If the script says four bedrooms and the details say three plus a study, fix the script.
  • Features. “Updated kitchen,” “new roof,” “walk to the station.” Each has to be true and the seller has to agree with it.
  • Rooms named. If the voiceover says “the primary suite” over a photo of the second bedroom, the timing is off or the script is.
  • Captions. Word-by-word captions follow the audio. Read them for misheard words. “Fourth bathroom” and “four bathrooms” sound alike.
  • Disclosures. If the voiceover mentions staging, good. If not, the caption must.

Pass three: the first three seconds

Most viewers decide in the opening. Check that the first scene is the right photo, that nothing in it was altered without a label, and that any generated opening is disclosed. If the video opens with an AI aerial, it is generated footage built from a reviewed original front exterior photo, not a drone recording; in Rendprop it is an optional paid feature that requires eligible paid access, a remaining Aerial allowance, available processing capacity and separate processor permission, results vary, and the review before use is this one. The aerial openings post covers what to look for.

A worked example: the Wednesday reel

A reel for a three-bed goes through review on Wednesday afternoon. Pass one finds the staged family room is in scene three; the agent notes it needs “Virtually staged” on screen and in the caption. It also finds the 9:16 crop of the back yard cut off the deck, which is the yard's best feature. She swaps in a portrait photo of the deck for the vertical version.

Pass two catches the voiceover saying “two full baths” when the listing is one full and one half. She fixes the script and re-generates the AI voice. Captions update with it.

Pass three: the opening scene is the front exterior with a blue sky edit. The caption gets “sky edited in exterior” alongside the staging line. Total review: twelve minutes. The reel goes out Thursday morning with nothing a buyer could catch.

Where video review goes wrong

  • Reviewing only the finished video. Motion hides still-photo errors. Review the stills first, then the video; the review habit post explains why.
  • Trusting the script because you wrote it. You wrote it before the details were final. Check it against the page.
  • Reviewing one format. The 16:9 and 9:16 versions are different videos. Watch both.
  • Skipping it because the tool is good. The tool built what you selected. Selection errors are yours to catch.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should I check in a listing video before publishing it?

Three passes. Sound off: which photo versions went in, scene order and crops. Sound on: every fact in the voiceover and captions against the listing details. Then the first three seconds again, since that is what most viewers see.

Why review the 9:16 version separately from the 16:9?

The vertical crop loses the sides of each photo, which may hold the window, the fireplace or the door to the yard. A scene that works in 16:9 can become a close-up of a cabinet in 9:16. Watch both and swap photos where needed.

What if the voiceover states a wrong fact?

Fix the script and regenerate the AI voice or re-record. Word-by-word captions follow the audio, so they update with it. Do not publish and correct it in the caption.

Does a good video tool remove the need for this review?

No. The tool builds what you selected. Which versions went in, what the script claims and whether the crop hid a feature are selection decisions, and only you can check them.
