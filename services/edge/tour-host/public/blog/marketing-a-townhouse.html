Marketing a townhouse

A townhouse sells on end or interior, how the levels are laid out and the private outdoor space. Show the stairs, identify the unit, state what is shared.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A townhouse sells on whether it is an end or interior unit, how the rooms are split across levels, the private outdoor space and the parking. Show the stairs instead of hiding them, photograph the unit so a buyer can pick it out of the row, and state what is private and what is shared in the first lines of the page.

A townhouse buyer is weighing a house's space against a condo's sharing, and the listing has to make that trade clear. Say whether it is an end or interior unit, show which rooms sit on which level, photograph the private outdoor space and the parking, and state what is shared. Show the stairs. A buyer who discovers three flights at the showing was never going to make an offer, so let them discover it on the page and save the appointment for someone who will.

What a townhouse buyer is weighing

Four things, usually in this order: is it an end unit with windows on three sides or an interior one with two; where are the kitchen, living room and bedrooms relative to each other and to the front door; is there a patio, deck or roof terrace that is theirs alone; and where does the car go. Behind those sit the shared questions: what wall, roof and yard are common, what the monthly fee is, and who looks after what. The photos answer the first four, the page answers the rest.

The shot list, level by level

  1. Front exterior with the unit identifiable. Shoot the row at an angle, then the unit's own door. A buyer should be able to find it from the street.
  2. Entry level. Foyer, the garage if it is attached, and whatever flex room sits at ground level. Shoot the garage interior with the door up and the car out.
  3. The stair, looking up. One frame. It tells the buyer the layout and it tells them the truth.
  4. Main level. Kitchen toward the living room, living room toward the kitchen, dining, the powder room, and the door to the outdoor space.
  5. The private outdoor space. Patio, deck or roof terrace, shot from the door looking out and from the far corner looking back, so its size is readable and the neighbor's space is clearly not included.
  6. Upper level. Primary bedroom, primary bath, secondary bedrooms, hall bath, laundry.
  7. Roof deck, if there is one, at the hour it gets light, with the view it actually has.
  8. Community. Visitor parking, the mailbox cluster, the pool or green if there is one, the street.

For the video, the order is the walk: front door, up to the main level, out to the patio, up to the bedrooms, and finish on the roof or back at the front. A townhouse is the one property type where the stair shots belong in the video and not just the gallery, because the video is how a buyer reads the levels. A short recorded walkthrough, handheld, covering the same route, is worth adding; viewers can scroll it as they would a plan.

What the page should say first

  • End or interior unit, and how many levels.
  • Which rooms are on which level, in one sentence each.
  • Private outdoor space with approximate dimensions.
  • Parking: garage, assigned space, driveway length, visitor parking.
  • The monthly fee as the association states it, and where a buyer reads what it covers. Point to the documents rather than summarizing them.
  • What is shared: walls, roof, yard maintenance, as the documents describe it.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in the details fields, with the levels in the text.

Townhouses draw first-time buyers and downsizers in equal numbers, and the two groups read the same page for different reasons. The posts on first-time buyers and downsizers cover what each is checking.

Townhouse listings that misfire

  • No exterior context. A close-up of a front door that could be any of forty. Shoot the row.
  • Hidden stairs. The gallery skips from the kitchen to the bedroom and the buyer finds the climb in person.
  • The neighbor's patio in your frame. If the fence line is ambiguous, say in the text where the private space ends.
  • A staged roof deck with an invented view. Add-furniture edits can change what is beyond the rail. Compare against the original and keep the Virtually staged label.
  • Restating the association's rules. State the fee, point to the documents, and let the buyer read them.

Worked example: a three-level end unit

A 3-bed, 2.5-bath end unit on three levels with a one-car garage, a 12-by-14 patio off the kitchen and a roof terrace facing west. The agent shoots at 4 p.m. on a Thursday: the row from the corner with the end unit in the foreground, the front door, the garage interior, the entry-level office, the stair looking up, the main level in four frames, the patio out and back, the upper level in five frames, the roof terrace at 5:15 as the sun drops, then the visitor parking and the mailbox cluster. Sixteen landscape frames make the listing video, which runs front door to roof. The page opens with: end unit, three levels, bedrooms on the top floor, kitchen and living on the main, office and garage at entry, private patio and roof terrace, one-car garage plus a driveway space, fee as stated by the association with its documents available on request. The roof terrace at 5:15 is the main photo. A two-minute handheld walkthrough of the same route is published alongside the video.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a townhouse listing show the stairs?

Yes, in the gallery and in the video. The levels are the layout, and a buyer who cannot manage three flights should learn that on the page rather than at the showing.

How do I photograph a townhouse so the buyer knows which unit it is?

Shoot the row at an angle with the unit in the foreground, then the unit's own door. Mention end or interior position in the first line of the text.

What should I say about the monthly fee and shared maintenance?

State the fee as the association currently gives it and point to the association's documents for what it covers. Do not summarize the rules in your own words; let the buyer read them.

Is a recorded walkthrough useful for a townhouse?

More than for most property types. A handheld walk from the front door to the top level shows how the levels connect, and viewers can scroll it on the published page alongside the photo-based video.
