What not to post about a listing

Five things that never go in a listing post: the seller's private life, anything that misrepresents the home, the deal, rule-breaking content, people.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Five things stay out of every listing post: anything about the seller's private life or routine, anything that misrepresents the home including unlabelled edits, anything about the state of the deal, anything your MLS or brokerage rules forbid, and people who did not agree to appear. Most real trouble comes from the first two, and most of it is in the background of a frame nobody checked.

Five things never go in a post about a listing: anything about the seller's private life or routine, anything that misrepresents the home including an unlabelled edit, anything about the state of the deal, anything your MLS or brokerage rules forbid, and any person who did not agree to be in the frame. Most of the trouble agents actually get into comes from the first two, and most of that is in the background of a photo nobody looked at twice.

The seller's life

The home is for sale. The household is not. Keep out of every frame and caption:

  • Names, including a child's name on a bedroom door or a monogrammed towel.
  • Mail, documents, calendars, whiteboards, fridge notes.
  • Medication, medical equipment, anything on a nightstand.
  • Family photographs close enough to recognise faces.
  • Valuables: jewellery on a dresser, art you can identify, the safe.
  • Security: alarm panels, key boxes, camera positions, the gate code on a sticky note.
  • Why they are selling, where they are going, when they are moving, and when the home is empty.

A caption that says the sellers are relocating in March and the home will be vacant is a caption telling a stranger when a home will be empty. It is also none of the buyer's business, and it weakens the seller's negotiating position.

The home's truth

Nothing in a post should show the home as something it is not. That includes the obvious, like a replaced sky with no disclosure, and the less obvious.

  • An AI edit without its disclosure line. Sky, lawn, twilight, tidying, staging: each one gets named in the caption. The one-line version is enough.
  • A frame composed to hide the road, the power line or the neighbor's wall, presented as the view.
  • Words like quiet, private or secluded for a home on a bus route.
  • A square-footage or lot figure you have not confirmed against the record.
  • A photo of a room that has since changed, posted as current.

The test is the showing. If a buyer who saw the post walks in and feels misled, the post was wrong, whatever the caption technically said.

The deal

Offers received, the number of showings, what the seller will take, when the seller needs to close, the inspection findings, the appraisal. None of it. A post that says multiple offers already is either true, in which case it is the seller's confidential position, or false, in which case it is worse. Status changes get a post; the inside of the negotiation does not.

The rules

Your MLS and your brokerage have current rules about pre-marketing, about what may be said before a listing is entered, about sold prices, about other agents' listings and about branding. They differ by area and change over time. The safe habits are these: no coming soon content until you have checked, no sold price without the seller's written consent and a check of the rules, no posting of another agent's listing without their permission, and the right link in the right place, which means the unbranded link for the MLS field and the branded one for your posts. Ask your broker when in doubt.

The people

Visitors at the open house, the neighbors on the sidewalk, the children in the park down the street, the photographer if they did not agree, the buyers at closing. Nobody appears without saying yes. And no caption describes who the home is for or who lives nearby; describe the home and the places, and ask your broker about fair housing language before any sentence about people.

The wall calendar

An agent posted a story frame of a kitchen in an occupied four-bed. Clean counters, good light, nothing wrong with the shot. On the wall by the fridge was the family's calendar, legible if you paused the frame: the week in April marked Florida, the school's name on a Tuesday, a dentist appointment with a child's first name. The frame was up for a day before a colleague mentioned it.

No harm came of it, which is the usual outcome, and the agent took it down and apologised to the seller, which is the right one. The fix going forward took no time at all: before posting any frame of an occupied home, look at the edges and the walls, not the subject. The subject is what you composed. The edges are what you missed.

A thirty-second check before posting

  1. Look at every edge of the frame and every wall. Names, paper, screens, panels.
  2. Read the caption for anything about the seller, the deal or the kind of person the home suits.
  3. If any photo is edited, confirm the disclosure line is there.
  4. Confirm the link is the branded property page.
  5. If a person is in the frame, confirm they said yes.

The same check applies to the property page itself; see privacy on a property page.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Can I say why the seller is moving in a listing post?

No. It is the seller's private business, it can weaken their negotiating position, and if it reveals when the home will be empty it is a security problem. Describe the home, not the household.

Is it a problem to post that a listing has multiple offers?

Yes. If it is true, it is the seller's confidential position. If it is not, it is a misrepresentation. Post status changes such as under contract, not the inside of the negotiation.

What should I check in a photo before posting it?

The edges and the walls, not the subject. Names, mail, calendars, screens, alarm panels, medication and family photos are what get missed. Then confirm any AI edit has its disclosure line in the caption.

Can I post another agent's listing to my followers?

Only with that agent's permission and within your MLS's current rules. Without both, do not. Ask your broker if you are unsure what your area allows.
