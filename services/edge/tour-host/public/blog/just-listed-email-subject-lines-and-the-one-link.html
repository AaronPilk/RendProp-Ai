Just listed email: subject lines and the one link

The subject line names the home and the place in plain words, key words first. The body carries one link to the property page, so every click lands there.

A just-listed subject line should name the home and the place in plain words, key words first: “New listing: 4-bed colonial in Oak Hill, $612,000.” The body should carry exactly one link, to the branded property page. The subject line decides whether the email opens; the single link decides whether the open turns into a visit to the page, where the gallery, the video and the enquiry form do the rest.

Both halves get overworked. Subject lines reach for urgency and capitals, and get filtered or ignored. Bodies carry a link to the portal, a link to the gallery, a link to the video, a link to your site and a link to book, and the reader, offered five doors, opens none. One clear subject and one link is not minimalism for its own sake; it is how the email gets read and acted on.

What a subject line has to do on a phone

Most of your database reads email on a phone, where the subject line is cut short after a handful of words. The words that survive have to say what this is and where. Put the type of home and the town before anything else; put the price last so it is a bonus if it fits. Avoid starting with your name, your brokerage or a greeting, because those are the words that survive the cut and they say nothing.

Eight subject line shapes that work

  • Type and town: “New listing: 4-bed colonial in Oak Hill”
  • Street and town: “Just listed on Granite Street, Maplewood”
  • The one fact: “A quarter-acre inside the village line, just listed in Oak Hill”
  • With the price: “Oak Hill 4-bed, $612,000, listed today”
  • For a segment: “New in your area: 3-bed ranch on Ferry Road”
  • The open house in the subject: “Open Saturday 1 to 3: Granite Street 3-bed”
  • The plain one: “Granite Street is listed”
  • Second email, different angle: “The kitchen at Granite Street” (sent two days later, one photo, same link)

None of them shout. All of them tell the reader what they will see if they open.

The preheader does the second job

The line of preview text after the subject is the second sentence of the pitch. Write it rather than letting the client pull the first line of the body. “Four bedrooms on one floor, a 2022 roof, showings from Friday.” Facts, not a repeat of the subject.

Why one link and not five

Every link in an email is a decision the reader has to make. A link to the portal sends them to a page with your competitors' listings and someone else's lead form. A link to the video sends them to a player with no form. A link to your homepage sends them to a search. The property page has everything in one place: the gallery, the details, the video that plays on request, your contact card and the form. Point the one link there and say what it opens: “See the full gallery and the video.” Use it as a button and again as a plain link near the end, both to the same page. That is one link, shown twice, not two links.

The body around it is short: one photo, the four headline facts, three lines of text. The post on a listing page in the email to your database lays out the body in full; this post is about the top and the link.

One email, two versions

Before, subject: “HOT NEW LISTING ALERT - You Won't Want To Miss This One” and a body with links to the portal, the video, the virtual tour, the agent's site and a booking tool.

After, subject: “New listing: 4-bed colonial in Oak Hill, $612,000”. Preheader: “Four bedrooms on one floor, 2022 roof, showings from Friday.” Body: one photo, the four facts, three lines, one button reading “See the gallery and video”, and the same link once more as plain text above your name.

Subject lines that get deleted

  • All capitals and “alert”. Reads as spam to both the reader and the filter.
  • Manufactured urgency. “Won't last”, “act fast”, “don't miss”. Your database has read these for years and learned to ignore them.
  • Symbols and pictograms. They render differently on every client and look like promotions.
  • Your name first. “Jordan at Maplewood Realty: new listing” buries the listing after the cut.
  • The full address with unit and zip. Nobody opens an email for a postcode. Street and town is enough.
  • Teasers with no information. “You have to see this” gives the reader no reason to.
  • Describing the buyer. “Perfect for young families” describes people rather than the property and is the kind of phrase to run past your broker. Describe the house.

Write three subjects, send one

Before your next launch email, write three subject lines in the shapes above, read each on your own phone's lock screen, and send the one that still says what and where after the cut. Then make sure the body has one link, and that it opens the page.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long should a just-listed email subject line be?

Short enough that the type of home and the town survive being cut off on a phone, which usually means the key words in the first five or six. Put the price last so it appears when there is room. Test by reading the subject on your own phone's lock screen before you send.

Should the subject line include the price?

If you and the seller are comfortable sharing it by email, yes, at the end of the subject where it is a bonus if it fits. Price is the fact readers most want and it filters the opens to people who are a match. If not, leave it to the property page.

Why only one link in a listing email?

Each extra link is a decision and a chance to land somewhere without your form. The property page holds the gallery, the details, the video and the enquiry form in one place. One link there, shown as a button and again as plain text near the end, gets more useful clicks than five destinations.

Is it all right to send a second email about the same listing?

Yes, two days later, with a different subject and a different photo: one room, one fact, the same link. Then a third before the open house and a fourth when it sells. Readers tolerate several short emails about one home far better than one long one.
