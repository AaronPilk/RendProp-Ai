What a single-property website should include

A single-property website needs a main photo, the core facts, a gallery, a video that plays on request, a contact card and an enquiry form.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A single-property website needs six things: a main photo, the core facts (price, beds, baths, square feet), a photo gallery, a listing video that plays when the buyer asks for it, a contact card and an enquiry form. Anything beyond that is a nice-to-have, and most of it slows the page down or distracts from the enquiry.

A single-property website needs six things: one strong main photo, the core facts (price, beds, baths, square feet), a photo gallery, a listing video that plays when the buyer asks for it, a contact card and an enquiry form. That is the whole list. Everything else is either optional or a distraction from the one action you want, which is a buyer asking to see the home.

The test for anything you add is simple. Does it help a buyer decide to book a showing, or does it help you get the enquiry when they do. If it does neither, leave it off.

The six things, in the order a buyer meets them

Think about a buyer on a phone, opening the link from a text their partner sent. They scroll top to bottom in about fifteen seconds before deciding whether to stay. The page should answer their questions in the order they ask them.

  1. Main photo. The first thing on the page. Usually the front exterior, sometimes the kitchen or the view if that is what sells the home. One image, full width, no carousel fighting for attention.
  2. Core facts. Price, beds, baths, square feet, and the address or at least the neighbourhood. A buyer who cannot find the price in two seconds assumes it is hidden for a reason.
  3. Gallery. Every photo you would put on the MLS, in the same order. Buyers who are interested will swipe through all of them. Buyers who are not will have left already.
  4. Video. The listing video, with a play button, not autoplaying. A buyer on a bus does not want sound starting on its own, and a buyer on a slow connection does not want a video loading before the photos.
  5. Contact card. Your name, your business, your phone number and your email. One tap to call. If the buyer wants to talk right now, the page should not make them hunt.
  6. Enquiry form. Name, contact method, a message, and the date they want to see the home. Asking for the date turns a vague "more info please" into a showing request you can act on.

A worked example: a 3-bed in a cul-de-sac

Say the listing is a 3-bed, 2-bath on a quiet cul-de-sac, 1,850 square feet, asking $485,000. The photographer delivered 28 photos. The page should open on the front exterior shot taken in the late afternoon with the garage door closed. Under it: $485,000, 3 bed, 2 bath, 1,850 sq ft, the street name and town.

The gallery runs exterior, entry, living room, kitchen, dining, primary bedroom, primary bath, the other two bedrooms, the second bath, the backyard, the garage. Then the video, which is the same photos cut together with a short voiceover. Then your card and the form.

Nothing about the school district rating, nothing about the mortgage calculator, nothing about your other listings. A buyer who wants the school information will ask, and that is an enquiry.

What to leave off, and why

Most property pages fail by addition, not omission. Each of these looks helpful and costs you.

  • A mortgage calculator. It invites the buyer to talk themselves out of the home before they have seen it, and the numbers are rarely accurate for their situation.
  • A map with nearby amenities. Useful on a portal, noise on a single-property page. The buyer already knows the area or they would not be looking.
  • Links to your other listings. You spent money marketing this home. Do not send the buyer somewhere else from its page.
  • Autoplaying video with music. It is the fastest way to get a page closed on a phone.
  • A long agent biography. Your card is enough. The buyer is deciding on the home, not on you, at this stage.
  • Floating chat widgets and pop-ups. They cover the photos on a phone screen and they rarely produce a better enquiry than the form.

The details page for a business, not a home

The same six-part shape works for an event venue, a restaurant or a gym, but the facts change. A venue page shows capacity instead of beds. A restaurant page shows hours and a menu. A gym shows membership options. The structure stays, the fields change, and the enquiry form still asks for a date because a planner or a prospective member is also trying to book a time.

Before you share the link

Open the page on your own phone, not your desktop. Check that the main photo is the one you meant, that the price is right, that the gallery order matches the MLS, and that tapping the phone number dials you. Then send the link to the seller before anyone else sees it. Sellers notice typos in their own address faster than anyone.

The single next step: pull up your current listing's page on your phone and count how many of the six things are present and how many extras are in the way. If the enquiry form is missing or buried, fix that first. FAQ

Questions on this topic

Does a single-property website need its own domain name?

No. Buyers arrive from a text, an email, a social post or the MLS, not by typing an address. A memorable path on a shared domain works as well as a custom domain and costs nothing to set up. What matters is that the same link is used everywhere.

Should the price be on the property website?

Yes, unless the listing is genuinely price-on-application. A buyer who cannot find the price assumes it is out of reach or being hidden, and leaves. Put it directly under the main photo with the beds, baths and square feet.

Is a listing video required on the page?

It is not required, but it helps when it is there. The video should play when the buyer taps it, not on its own. If you have no video, the page should simply skip that section rather than show an empty player.

What should the enquiry form ask for?

Name, a way to reach them, a short message, and the date they want to see the home. The date question is the one that turns a casual enquiry into a showing request. Avoid long forms; every extra field loses people.
