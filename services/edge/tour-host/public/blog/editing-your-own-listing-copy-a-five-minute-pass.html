Editing your own listing copy: a five-minute pass

Five passes, one minute each: facts against the source, adjectives to facts, the first sentence, people versus property, and read it aloud. Then publish.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Edit listing copy in five one-minute passes, each looking for one thing: every number against its source, every adjective replaced by a fact or cut, the first sentence doing its job, any phrase about people instead of property, and a read-aloud for anything that trips the tongue. Five minutes, one thing at a time, catches more than twenty minutes of general rereading.

Edit your listing copy in five passes of one minute each, looking for one thing per pass: numbers against their source, adjectives against facts, the first sentence against the title's job, phrases about people against the property, and the whole thing read aloud. Five minutes, one thing at a time, catches more than twenty minutes of rereading for “anything wrong”, because the eye only finds what it is looking for.

The method matters because listing copy is usually written in a hurry, in the MLS input box, right before the listing goes live. That is the worst place and time to edit. Write it in a notes app, leave it for an hour if you can, and run the five passes with a timer.

Minute one: the numbers

Read only the numbers. Price, beds, baths, square feet, lot size, year built, the year of every update, taxes, the fee, the room dimensions, the distances. For each, ask where it came from. The tax record, the seller's receipt, the measurement you took, the walk you timed. If you cannot name the source, confirm it or cut the number. The seller's recollection that the roof is “about five years old” is not a source; the invoice is. A wrong number is the error that costs the most later, and it is also the easiest to catch when you look only for numbers.

Minute two: the adjectives

Circle every adjective. For each one, write the fact that made you use it: “spacious” becomes 15 by 22, “updated” becomes 2022, “charming” becomes 1954 with the original terrazzo. Where there is a fact, swap it in. Where there is not, cut the adjective and ask whether the sentence still says anything. The post on words to avoid in listing copy has the usual list and its replacements.

Minute three: the first sentence

Read only the first sentence. Could it open any other listing in town without changes. If yes, rewrite it around the one checkable fact that makes this house different: the position, the year, the lot, the renovation, the room that carries the showing. Then check that the main photo and the first sentence are about the same thing.

Minute four: people versus property

Read for any sentence about people rather than the home: who it is perfect for, who lives nearby, what kind of household it suits, how safe it is. Swap each for the property fact behind it, or cut it. This is not legal advice; it is the pass where you catch the phrases to ask your broker about before publishing rather than after. If anything feels borderline, text the phrase to your broker now, while the listing is still a draft.

Minute five: read it aloud

Read the whole thing out loud at normal speed. Anywhere you stumble, the sentence is too long or the word order is wrong; fix it. Anywhere you hear yourself slip into a brochure voice (“boasts”, “offers”, three adjectives in a row), say what you would say to a buyer in the hallway and type that. If the same text will be the voiceover script, this pass is doubly worth it, because the voice, yours or an AI voice, will read exactly what is there.

One paragraph through the five passes

Draft: “Gorgeous updated 3-bed in a family-friendly neighbourhood. Spacious living room, newer roof, beautiful kitchen. Approximately 1,900 sq ft. Must see.”

After the numbers pass: the tax record says 1,840 square feet; the roof invoice says 2021; the kitchen receipts say 2022.

After the adjectives pass: “gorgeous” and “beautiful” go; “spacious” becomes 15 by 22; “updated” and “newer” become years.

After the first-sentence pass: the opener becomes the position, which is the thing that makes the house different.

After the people pass: “family-friendly neighbourhood” becomes the street layout.

After the read-aloud: “Must see” goes, and the sentences shorten.

Result: “The end house on Hadley Street, with the town conservation land behind the back fence. A 1,840-square-foot three-bed with a 15 by 22 living room. Roof 2021; kitchen and both baths 2022. Showings start Friday.”

Where self-editing fails

  • Editing in the MLS input box. It is small, it times out, and it tempts you to publish. Write and edit elsewhere, then paste.
  • Editing immediately after writing. You read what you meant, not what you typed. An hour's gap, or even a walk to the car, resets the eye.
  • Skipping the numbers pass because you know the house. The number you know is the one the seller told you. Check the source anyway.
  • Looking for everything at once. The eye finds what it is told to find. One thing per pass.
  • Forgetting the other texts. The story section, the caption, the email and the script need the same five minutes. They can share the first four passes if they were cut from the same source; the read-aloud is separate for each.

How this works in Rendprop

The text on a Rendprop property page is what you typed: the details fields, the story section, and the contact card. Set a timer

Open the draft for your current listing, set a five-minute timer, and run the passes in order: numbers, adjectives, first sentence, people, aloud. Then paste it where it goes. FAQ

Questions on this topic

Why edit listing copy in separate passes instead of one reread?

Because the eye finds what it is looking for. A general reread for anything wrong catches the obvious typo and misses the wrong square footage. One minute looking only at numbers, then only at adjectives, and so on, catches more in less time.

How do I check the numbers in a listing description?

Name the source for each one: the tax record, the invoice, your own measurement, the walk you timed. If there is no source, confirm it or cut it. The seller's memory is a lead, not a source, and the number from it is the one most likely to be wrong.

Should I edit the voiceover script the same way?

Yes, and the read-aloud pass matters most there, since whatever is typed is what your recording or an AI voice will say and what the captions will show word by word. Cut the script from the edited description so the facts and the order already match, then read it aloud with a timer.

When is the best time to edit listing copy?

An hour or more after writing it, in a notes app rather than the MLS input box. Right after writing, you read what you meant rather than what you typed. Even a short gap resets the eye enough for the five passes to work.
