Resort listings: second-home buyers who shop from far away

Second-home buyers decide from a distance, on a phone, with a partner. The page has to be the first showing and the video has to be worth forwarding.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

In a resort market the buyer is almost always somewhere else. They are in another state, on a phone after dinner, showing the listing to a partner across the table, and they are deciding whether to book a flight on the strength of what you published. They cannot drop by on Saturday. So the property page has to work as the first showing, the video has to be good enough to forward to the person who was not in the room, and the page has to answer the questions a distant owner asks before anything about the kitchen: how the place is looked after when they are not there, what the quiet season is like, how long the drive from the airport takes.

Who the buyer is and what they cannot do

A second-home buyer is buying a feeling and a logistics plan at the same time. The feeling is the view from the deck, the walk to the lift, the beach at 7am. The logistics are the management company, the rental rules if they intend to rent it, the cost of keeping it, the distance from the place they actually live. They are usually shopping across several resort towns at once, and they are doing it from a couch. Everything they cannot do in person, the page has to do for them: walk the unit, see the view at the right hour, understand the building, check the drive, and ask a question without picking up the phone.

They also decide with someone who was not looking at the screen. A forwardable video does the work of the second viewing. A gallery of thirty photos does not get forwarded; a ninety-second video does.

The page as the first showing

Build the page to answer the distant buyer in the order they ask. The view and the walk to whatever the resort is for: the slope, the beach, the course, the marina. Then the unit. Then the building or the community: parking, storage for gear, the pool, the clubhouse. Then the practical paragraph: who manages it, what the association covers, what the rental situation is and a note to check the current local rules, the drive time from the nearest airport. Then the enquiry form. A buyer who reads all of that and asks for a date has done most of the qualifying themselves.

Shoot the resort as well as the unit. The walk from the door to the lift or the sand, in portrait, is the social video. The view from the deck at the hour it is best is the main photo. The lobby, the ski locker, the pool at 5pm, the restaurant across the street are the context that makes this listing different from the identical layout one building over.

Example: a two-bed ski condo sold to a family three states away

A two-bed, two-bath on the third floor of a slopeside building, with a deck facing the mountain and a ski locker downstairs. The listing goes live in October with photos from a September shoot: the deck at 4pm with the peak in light, the walk from the locker to the lift in portrait, the pool and hot tub, the unit itself. The description gives the management company, what the association fee covers, the rental history as a fact the seller provided, and the airport drive.

A buyer in another state finds it on a portal, opens the unbranded link from the MLS tour field, watches the video twice and sends the branded link to her partner. He watches it on his phone at the airport. She fills in the enquiry form with a date three weeks out, the week they were already planning to visit. The agent replies with the showing time and the name of the restaurant across the street. They see four condos that weekend and buy the one they had already walked through on a screen.

What goes wrong with resort listings

  • A unit shoot with no resort. Twenty photos of a two-bed and nothing of the mountain, the beach or the walk to it. The buyer cannot tell why this one costs more than the one down the hall.
  • The view at the wrong hour. A deck photo at noon with the peak in haze or the sea in glare. The view is the listing; shoot it when it looks like the reason to buy.
  • No management or rental facts. The distant buyer's first worry is what happens to the place in the months they are not there. If the page does not say, the enquiry is that question alone.
  • Twilight and sky edits that overpromise. A buyer who flies in for a showing and finds cloud where the page showed sunset has spent a plane ticket on the difference. Keep the disclosure on any edit and compare it with the original before publishing.
  • Assuming they will call. They will not. They are in a different time zone and they do not know you. Give them a form that asks for a date.

Next step: open your listing on a phone, hand it to someone who has never been to the resort, and ask them to tell you where the lift or the beach is relative to the door. If they cannot, that is the first scene the video is missing.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How do second-home buyers in a resort market usually shop?

From a distance, on a phone, in the evening, often across several resort towns at once, and usually with a partner who is not looking at the same screen. The page has to work as a first showing and the video has to be worth forwarding.

What should a resort listing page say beyond the unit details?

Who manages the property, what the association fee covers, the rental situation with a note to check current local rules, the drive time from the nearest airport, and what the resort is like in the quiet season. These are the distant owner's first questions.

Should I photograph the resort itself for a condo listing?

Yes. The walk from the door to the lift or the beach, the pool, the locker room and the view at the right hour are what separate your unit from the identical layout one building over. The unit alone cannot show that.

Is a twilight edit a good idea for a resort view?

Only if the real evening looks like it. A buyer who flies in for a showing and finds the sky does not match has paid for a plane ticket to feel misled. If you use one, keep the disclosure and compare it with the original.
