Rental listing videos: what tenants actually want to see

Tenants want the real kitchen and bath, storage, laundry, parking, light, noise and what is included. Show those plainly and you get applications.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Tenants want to see the kitchen and bathroom as they are, where things get stored, where laundry happens, where the car goes, how much light the main room gets, what the windows face, and what is included in the rent. They do not want music, adjectives or a staged living room with no label. Why rental video is a different job

A buyer is choosing a home for years and will forgive a lot for the right street. A tenant is choosing where to live for twelve months and is deciding mostly on whether the place works day to day. They have also been burned: a listing photo that was wider than the room, a kitchen that was not the kitchen, a parking space that turned out to be the street. Suspicion is the default.

So the video's job is not to sell. It is to show, plainly enough that the tenant trusts the rest of what you say.

The eight things to show

  1. The kitchen, as it is. Counter space, the stove, the fridge. If the fridge is included, say so. If it is small, show it small.
  2. The bathroom, as it is. One honest frame from the doorway. Shower or tub. No wide-lens trick.
  3. Storage. The closet open. The hall cupboard. Tenants are moving boxes in and want to know where they go.
  4. Laundry. In-unit, in the building, or not at all. One frame or one line. It is often the first question.
  5. Parking. The space, the garage, the permit situation. A frame of the actual spot.
  6. Light and what the windows face. The main room at the hour it is brightest, with a line about the direction. A tenant working from home cares more about this than about the finish.
  7. Noise, honestly. If the unit is on a busy road, say it faces the road and show the window. If it is quiet, say that too; it is a feature.
  8. What is included. Heat, water, internet, the fridge, the washer. In the caption and in one spoken line.

Six to eight frames, one sentence each, in that order or close to it.

The script: facts, no sell

One bedroom on the second floor, facing the courtyard, so it is quiet. Kitchen has a full-size fridge and a gas range, both included. Laundry is in the basement, two machines. One parking space in the lot behind. Heat and water included. Available the first of next month. Apply through the link.

Fifty-two words. Nothing to argue with at the viewing. Your own voice is the better choice here; a tenant wants to know the person they will be dealing with sounds reasonable.

Music and edits on a rental video

Leave the music off. A track under a rental video reads as sales pressure to someone who is already wary, and the quiet of the recording tells them something about the unit.

Be careful with AI edits. A greened lawn, a tidied counter or furniture added to an empty room can help a tenant picture the place, and each one also risks the moment at the viewing when they realise the photo was not the room. If you use them, label every one: Virtually staged on staged frames, a plain disclosure on any other alteration, in the video and in the caption. Compare each edit with the original first, because an edit that removes a radiator or adds a window is a problem you will hear about. Tenants screenshot listings; the label needs to be in the frame, not just the caption. More on that in AI edits on rental listings.

A worked example: the courtyard one-bed

A second-floor one-bed over a courtyard, small kitchen with a full-size fridge, tub shower, one deep closet, basement laundry, one parking space, heat and water included. Photos: main room at nine in the morning with the window, kitchen, bathroom from the door, closet open, basement laundry, the parking space with the number on the wall, the courtyard from the window. Seven frames, 52-word script in the landlord's agent's own voice, captions on, no music, 26 seconds. The caption lists rent, deposit, what is included, pet policy and the available date. Posted Monday. Anyone who enquires through the page that week already knows the kitchen is small, which is the point.

Rental video mistakes that cost you the good tenants

  • A wide lens that doubles the bedroom. The tenant feels it the moment they walk in, and the rest of the viewing is about what else was stretched.
  • Skipping laundry and parking. Those are the two questions in every message you will get.
  • Music. Reads as a pitch.
  • Staged furniture with no label. The tenant arrives to an empty room and wonders what else was not real.
  • No included-in-rent line. A tenant who assumes heat is included and finds out it is not walks away annoyed, and leaves a review.
  • Posting the horizontal video to a feed. Same as a sales listing: 9:16 for social, 16:9 for the page.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a rental listing video have music?

No. A tenant is already wary, and a track under the voice reads as sales pressure. A plain voice, captions and honest frames do more. The quiet of the recording also says something true about the unit.

What do tenants want to see first in a rental video?

The kitchen and bathroom as they really are, then storage, laundry, parking, the light in the main room and what is included in the rent. Those are the questions every message asks, and a video that answers them brings applications from people who will not be surprised at the viewing.

Can I use virtual staging on a rental video?

You can, with a label on every staged frame inside the video and in the caption, because tenants screenshot listings. Compare each edit with the original first, and avoid any edit that changes the room itself rather than the furniture in it.

How long should a rental video be?

Twenty to thirty seconds for the social version, six to eight frames, one plain sentence each. The rent, deposit, inclusions, pet policy and available date go in the caption and on the property page rather than in the voiceover.
