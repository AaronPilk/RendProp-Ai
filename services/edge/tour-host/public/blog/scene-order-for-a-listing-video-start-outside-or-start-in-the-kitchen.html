Scene order for a listing video: start outside or in the kitchen

Start outside when the exterior sets the price and the setting. Start in the kitchen when the inside is the story. Then follow the walk a buyer would take.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Start outside when the exterior tells the buyer what they are getting: a lot, a setting, a style, a price bracket. Start in the kitchen, or whichever interior room is the selling point, when the outside is ordinary and the inside is the story. After the opener, follow the walk a buyer would take through the front door, and end on the yard, the view or the best room again.

Start outside when the exterior tells the buyer what kind of home this is: the lot, the street, the style, the price bracket. Start in the kitchen when the outside is ordinary and the inside is the reason to come. After that first scene, follow the walk a buyer would take from the front door, room by room, and end on the yard, the view or the best room a second time. The order is a tour, and a tour that jumps around makes the home feel smaller than it is.

When to open outside

Open on the exterior when it carries information the rest of the video needs. A house on an acre, a corner lot, a craftsman on a tree-lined street, a townhouse with a roof deck visible from the road, a lake house. In those cases the front shot answers the buyer's first question, which is where is it and what is it, and everything after makes sense.

Open outside, too, when the video is the 16:9 tour on the property page. The viewer chose to watch and expects to arrive at the house before they go inside.

When to open in the kitchen

Open inside when the exterior would cost you the viewer. A plain facade on a plain street, a condo building that looks like every other condo building, a split-level with a garage door for a face. The buyer is not scrolling past the house; they are scrolling past the first frame. Give them the island, the window wall, the view, and show the exterior second so they know what they are looking at.

This applies with more force on a 9:16 reel than on the page video. A reel has about three seconds to earn the next three. The exterior rarely wins that.

It does not have to be the kitchen. It is whichever room sells the home. Kitchen most often, then the main living space, then a view, then a pool or a yard in summer.

The order after the opener

Walk the home the way a buyer would on a showing.

  1. Entry or hall. One frame, brief. It tells the viewer they are inside.
  2. Main living space. Two frames if the room is big enough to need two angles.
  3. Kitchen and dining. If the kitchen was the opener, show it again from a second angle here rather than skipping it.
  4. Main bedroom and its bath. The second thing most buyers ask about.
  5. Other bedrooms and the hall bath. One frame each. Count matters more than detail.
  6. Anything special. The office, the finished basement, the garage workshop.
  7. Outside again. Yard, deck, view, pool. End where people want to spend time.

The pattern is outside, in, through, and out again. It matches the memory a buyer forms on a showing and makes the video feel like a visit.

A worked example: the split-level

A 1970s split-level, garage front, three beds up, family room down, renovated kitchen, big flat back yard with mature trees. The exterior is honest but it does not stop anyone.

The reel opens on the kitchen island with the window behind it. Second frame, the front of the house, so the viewer knows it is a split-level on a suburban street. Then living room, main bedroom, the downstairs family room, and the last frame is the back yard from the deck with the trees. Six photos, 26 seconds.

The property page video opens on the front exterior because the viewer has already decided to look. Then entry, living, kitchen from two angles, dining, three bedrooms, two baths, family room, garage, deck, yard. Fourteen photos, about 70 seconds. Same home, two orders, each right for where it plays.

Orders that confuse

  • Bouncing between floors. Upstairs bedroom, then basement, then another upstairs bedroom. The viewer loses the map of the house.
  • Bathroom as the second scene. It happens when the photographer's file order is used as the video order. Nobody decides to view a home from the bathroom.
  • Three exteriors in a row. Front, front angle, side. One strong exterior and one yard shot is enough.
  • Ending on a closet. The last frame is what the viewer remembers. Make it the yard, the view or the best room.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a listing video always start with the front of the house?

No. Start outside when the exterior tells the buyer something that matters, such as the lot, the street or the style. Start with the kitchen or the strongest interior room when the facade is ordinary, especially on a social reel where the first frame has to stop a scroll.

What order should the rooms come in after the first scene?

The order a buyer would walk on a showing: entry, main living space, kitchen and dining, main bedroom and bath, other bedrooms, anything special, then outside again. Ending on the yard, the view or the best room leaves the right last impression.

Should the reel and the property page video use the same order?

Usually not. The reel needs the strongest frame first because the viewer has not decided to watch. The page video can start outside because the viewer opened the page on purpose and expects to arrive at the house.

How many photos should the tour include?

Enough to walk the home once without repeating. For a 16:9 page video that is often ten to fifteen frames; for a reel, six to ten. Leave out second angles of small rooms and anything that would make a buyer ask why it is there.
