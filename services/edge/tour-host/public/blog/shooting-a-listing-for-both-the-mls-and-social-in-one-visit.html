Shooting a listing for both the MLS and social in one visit

One visit covers the MLS set and the social set if you shoot landscape photos for the listing and portrait photos for the reel. The plan and the order.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Shoot the full set of landscape photos for the MLS, the video and the property page first, then go back through the four to six hero rooms in portrait for the social reel. Separate frames, not crops. It adds a few minutes to the visit and gives the agent everything they post from one shoot.

One visit covers both the MLS and social if you treat them as two short passes rather than one set cropped two ways. Pass one is the full set of landscape photos: every room, the exteriors, the transitions, which feeds the MLS, the property page gallery and the landscape video. Pass two is a handful of portrait frames of the rooms that sell the home, which feeds the vertical reel. Separate frames. Not crops. It adds minutes to the shoot and gives the agent everything they post from a single appointment.

Photographers who crop the landscape photos for social deliver reels where the room is gone and the ceiling is the subject. Agents notice, even if they cannot say why the reel feels thin.

Why two passes instead of one

A landscape photo and a portrait photo of the same room are different pictures. The landscape photo shows width: the run of the kitchen, the length of the living room, the spread of the yard. The portrait frame shows height and depth: the ceiling, the floor and the far wall in one glance, which is what a phone screen holds. Cropping one into the other throws away the thing that made it work.

The post on landscape photos or portrait photos covers the framing differences room by room. The practical consequence is simple: shoot both, deliberately, in two passes.

Pass one: the landscape photos

Your normal MLS shot list, with the video in mind. Walk the path through the home: front exterior, entry, living, kitchen, dining, stairs, primary bedroom, primary bath, secondary rooms, rear yard. One clean wide per room, a transition at each doorway, detail shots where they earn their place. Consistent tripod height, consistent exposure, straight verticals. This is the set that becomes the gallery, the MLS photos and the landscape video, so it has to be complete.

The shooting for video post goes through the transitions and the path, which is what makes this set work as a video as well as a gallery.

Pass two: the portrait set

Before you pack up, walk the home again with the camera turned. You are looking for four to six frames:

  • The exterior. Usually a slightly tighter angle than the landscape photo, with the front door and the roofline both in.
  • The room that sells the home. The kitchen in most homes; the living room or the view in some.
  • The primary bedroom. Portrait holds the bed, the window and the ceiling together.
  • One feature. A staircase, a fireplace wall, a tall window, a deck with a view.
  • The ending. The yard or a second exterior.

Shoot each at the same height as the landscape photos, straight verticals, and leave a little headroom at the top of the frame for captions. Five minutes, maybe eight on a big home.

A worked visit

A four-bed on a Wednesday, 10:00 booking. 10:00 to 11:05, pass one: twenty-six landscape photos along the path, plus eight detail shots. 11:05 to 11:12, pass two: portrait of the front, the kitchen from the doorway, the living room toward the fireplace, the primary bedroom from the foot of the bed, the deck. Pack up by 11:20. In the car you add both sets, build both videos, record a short voiceover for the reel. The agent has the MLS set, the gallery, the landscape video for the MLS link and the property page, and a reel to post on launch day, all from one appointment.

What goes wrong with the one-visit approach

  • Doing the portrait pass first. The landscape photos are the set that cannot be incomplete. Do it first, then the reel frames.
  • Changing height between passes. The two videos should feel like the same visit. Keep the tripod where it was.
  • Shooting portrait of every room. A reel needs five frames, not twenty-five. More portrait frames means more review and no better reel.
  • Forgetting the captions need room. A portrait frame with the subject at the very top gets covered by the caption.
  • AI edits applied to one set and not the other. If the landscape photo of the exterior gets a blue sky, the portrait exterior should too, and both carry their disclosure. Compare each with its original.

Next step: add a portrait pass of five frames to the end of your next shoot and build the reel from them, then look at it next to a cropped version of the landscape photos. You will not go back to cropping.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How many portrait frames does a social reel need?

Four to six. The exterior, the room that sells the home, the primary bedroom, one feature and an ending frame. More than that makes a long reel and more review for no gain.

Can I crop the landscape photos instead of shooting portrait?

You can, but the crop loses the room's width and often puts the ceiling in the middle of the frame. Shooting separate portrait frames takes a few minutes and the reel reads as intended.

Does the MLS need the portrait photos?

No. The MLS set is the landscape photos. The portrait pass is for the vertical social video. Both can sit in the same listing in Rendprop, feeding their own video.

Should both sets get the same AI edits?

If the landscape photo of the exterior gets a blue sky, give the portrait exterior the same treatment so the two videos match. Compare every edit with its original and leave the disclosure on.
