Warehouse and workshop marketing for lease

A warehouse or workshop leases on clear height, doors, power, yard and access. Photograph those plainly, state size and date, give each unit its own link.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Industrial tenants lease on numbers and access: clear height, the number and size of the loading doors, power supply, yard depth and how a truck gets in and out. Photograph those things plainly, state the square footage and availability date first, and give each unit its own page and link so the right prospect gets the right space.

A warehouse or workshop is leased on five things a tenant can check from a photo and two they cannot. From a photo: clear height, loading doors, floor condition, yard and the truck route in; from the page: square footage and availability date. Shoot the five plainly with the lights on, put the two numbers in the details and the voiceover, and give each unit its own link.

The tenant who tours after that already knows the space fits their forklift and their delivery schedule.

Industrial tenants are not browsing

A logistics company needs 20,000 square feet with dock doors and a 30-foot clear height within 60 days. A cabinet maker needs 4,000 square feet with three-phase power and a roller door wide enough for a sheet delivery. A car restorer wants a workshop with a pit or room for a lift and a yard for four vehicles. None of them are moved by a photo of the office mezzanine. They scan for the numbers and then look for proof in the photos. The listing's job is to make the proof easy to find.

The shot list for a warehouse or workshop

  1. The unit from the yard, with the doors visible. Dock or grade-level, how many, how wide. Doors open if you can.
  2. The interior from the main door, wide, lights on. Shows depth and the column grid.
  3. The interior from the back wall looking at the doors. Shows clear height against the door frame and the lighting.
  4. A frame that shows the clear height. A column with the roof structure visible, or a person standing by the door for scale.
  5. The floor. Close enough to see the condition. Sealed, cracked, painted.
  6. The electrical panel and any three-phase supply. Panel door open if it is safe and permitted.
  7. The yard from the gate, with the truck route visible. Turning space, surface, security fence.
  8. Office and restrooms, briefly. From the door. Tenants ask, but they do not decide on it.

Shoot on a dry day with the roller doors open and every light on. Sweep the floor first. Leave the previous tenant's racking out of frame unless it stays. Skip AI edits on industrial photos; a tidy edit that removes a stain or a crack misleads a tenant who is choosing the unit for its floor. If any edit is used, keep the disclosure and compare it with the original.

Worked example: a 6,500 square foot workshop unit with a yard

A broker has a 6,500 square foot unit on a small industrial estate: one grade-level roller door, 22-foot clear height, three-phase power, a sealed floor, a 40-foot yard and a 400 square foot office. He shoots nine landscape photos at 10am with the roller door open: the unit from the yard, the interior from the door, the interior from the back, a column with the roof structure for height, the floor, the panel, the yard from the gate with the estate road behind, the office, and the restroom. The listing video runs forty seconds from the estate gate to the back wall. The voiceover states the square footage, the clear height, the door size, the power, and the availability date. The page shows the details typed in and the gallery; the enquiry form asks for the date the visitor wants. A joinery business books Thursday and brings a tape measure for the door, which is the right reaction.

Numbers first, then photos

Square footage and availability date first, in the details and in the first line of the voiceover. Clear height, door count and power in the voiceover and in the details so they are findable without watching. If rent is quoted, keep it current. For a unit with a yard, state the yard depth; it is the number that decides whether an articulated truck can turn.

One link per unit on the estate

An estate with six vacancies gets six pages. The joinery business does not need the 20,000 square foot unit's video and the logistics company does not need the 4,000. If the listing service you post to wants a plain tour link without your details, that is what the unbranded link is for; check the service's current rules. Enquiries from all six units land in one inbox so a busy week does not lose a tenant.

Where industrial listings go wrong

  • Office photos first. The tenant is leasing a shed, not a meeting room.
  • No clear height stated. The first thing a racking supplier asks. State it.
  • Doors closed in every shot. Open them. The door is the unit.
  • Previous tenant's mess. Sweep the floor and move the pallets, or shoot after the clear-out.
  • No yard or route photo. If a truck cannot get in, nothing else matters.
  • A PDF brochure for the whole estate. One page per unit, one link per prospect.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What do industrial tenants look for first in a listing?

Square footage, availability date, clear height, loading doors and power. Then they look at the photos for proof: the doors open, the floor, the yard and the truck route in.

How do I show clear height in a photo?

Shoot a column with the roof structure visible from the floor, or shoot the interior from the back wall with the open door frame in view for comparison. A person standing by the door gives scale. State the number in the details too.

Should I clean up the floor with an AI edit?

No. A tenant is choosing the unit partly for its floor, and an edit that hides a crack or a stain is misleading. Sweep it and shoot it as it is. If any edit is used, keep the disclosure and compare it with the original.
