Coworking space marketing: desks, rooms and the walk to coffee

Show the three things a coworking member buys: a desk, a bookable room and the walk to the coffee. One page per location, a short video, a date to visit.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A coworking space sells three things: a desk someone wants to sit at, a room they can book for a call, and the walk between them that passes the coffee. Show those three in a short video, state the membership options plainly, and let the visitor pick a date to come in. Everything else on the page is secondary.

A coworking space sells three things and the marketing should show exactly those three: a desk the prospect wants to sit at, a room they can book for a client call, and the walk between them that passes the coffee machine. Put that in a short video on a page with the membership options typed in plainly and a form that asks what day they want to visit. Lounges, phone booths and the rooftop are supporting detail; desk, room and coffee are the decision.

What a prospect is actually buying

Nobody joins a coworking space for the beanbags. A freelancer joins because working from the kitchen table has stopped working. A four-person startup joins because they need a door that closes for investor calls. A remote employee joins because the company pays and the commute is ten minutes. All three want to know the same things: will I get a desk I like, can I take a call without the whole floor hearing, and is the coffee any good. The page should answer those before it says anything about community.

The shot list for a coworking floor

  1. Entrance and reception. The door, the sign, the person at the desk if they agree to be photographed.
  2. The open floor, wide, from the entry, mid-morning. Enough people that it looks alive, not so many that it looks full. Faces turned away or permission obtained.
  3. A hot desk, close enough to see the chair and the monitor arm. This is the product for most members.
  4. A dedicated desk or a private office, from the door. For the team buyer.
  5. Each bookable meeting room, from the door, screen on. Name the capacity in the caption or the voiceover.
  6. A phone booth with the door open. People ask about them more than any other feature.
  7. The kitchen and the coffee. The walk from the desk to the machine, if you are recording a walkthrough; the counter and the machine, if photos.
  8. One shot of what is outside. The street, the park, the station. Location is half the membership.

Worked example: a 90-desk space over two floors

A coworking space with 60 hot desks, 30 dedicated desks, six meeting rooms and four phone booths shoots at 10:30 on a Wednesday. The listing video opens on the street door, walks to reception, shows the main floor wide, holds on a window desk, moves to the six-person room with the screen on, passes a phone booth, and ends at the kitchen with the coffee machine and the terrace door open. Fifty seconds. The voiceover names the membership options in one sentence each and the hours. The page shows hours, membership, the gallery, the video on request and a form that asks for the date the visitor wants to come in. A prospect who has just watched the walk to the coffee books Thursday.

Membership on the page: plain words, no asterisks

State the options the way you would say them at the desk: hot desk by the month, dedicated desk by the month, private office for teams of two to eight, meeting rooms by the hour for members. Skip the tier names your website uses. A prospect comparing three spaces wants to put your numbers in the same row as the others, and invented tier names make that harder.

The walk to the coffee is not a joke

Members spend eight hours a day in the space. The route they walk ten times a day is the thing they will remember from the tour, and it is the thing a video can show that a photo cannot. If you record a handheld walkthrough, start it at a desk and end it at the machine. Slow down at the turns so the viewer can see the rooms along the way. Forty seconds is plenty.

Where coworking pages go wrong

  • Community language before the product. Show the desk first. The community comes through in the photos of people working.
  • Empty floor at 7am. It looks like a space nobody uses. Shoot mid-morning with a few members in frame, with permission.
  • No room capacities. A six-person room and a twelve-person room look similar in a photo. Say the number.
  • Phone booths hidden. If you have them, show one. If you do not, say so rather than letting the tour imply privacy that is not there.
  • One page for five locations. A prospect wants the one near them. One page per location, with its own link.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a coworking space video show first?

The open floor wide from the entrance, then a desk close enough to see the chair and the monitor arm. The desk is the product for most members. Rooms, phone booths and the kitchen follow.

Should the space be busy or empty in the photos?

About two-thirds full, mid-morning, with members' permission for anyone in frame. An empty floor looks unused; a packed one looks like a fight for a desk.

How should memberships be described on the page?

In plain words, one sentence each, without tier names. Hot desk by the month, dedicated desk by the month, private office for small teams, rooms by the hour. Prospects are comparing you with two other spaces and need numbers they can line up.

Which Rendprop mode fits a coworking space?

It depends on what you sell. The gyms and studios mode asks for details such as membership and hours; the other businesses mode fits a space that leases offices. Both produce the same page structure with a form that asks for the visit date.
