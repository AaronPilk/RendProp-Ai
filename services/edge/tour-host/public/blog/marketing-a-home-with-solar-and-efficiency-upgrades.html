Marketing a home with solar and efficiency upgrades

Solar and efficiency upgrades sell on paperwork: owned or leased, system size, install year and the seller's real bills. Show the equipment, no guesses.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Solar and efficiency upgrades sell on documents, not adjectives: whether the solar is owned or under a lease or agreement, the system size and install year from the paperwork, the seller's actual utility bills for a stated period, and the year and installer of each other upgrade. Photograph the panels, the inverter, the battery, the charger and the heat pump so a buyer can see they exist, and put the figures on the page without predicting what the buyer will save.

A home with solar and efficiency upgrades sells on paperwork. The buyer wants to know whether the panels are owned outright or under a lease or power agreement, how big the system is and when it went in, what the seller's bills actually were over a stated period, and the year and installer of every other upgrade: heat pump, insulation, windows, water heater, charger. State those from the documents, photograph the equipment so a buyer can see it is real, and resist predicting what anyone will save. The buyer's usage is not the seller's.

Start with the documents, because the page is built from them

  • Ownership. Owned and paid off, financed, leased, or under a power purchase agreement, in the words of the contract. If it is not owned, say that the agreement's terms are available on request and that the buyer should review them with their own advisor. Do not describe how a lease transfers; ask your broker how to word it.
  • System facts from the installation paperwork: size in kilowatts, panel count, inverter type, battery capacity if there is one, install year, installer, and any warranty as the installer's document states it.
  • Production, if the seller has a monitoring history and agrees to share it: the figure for a stated twelve months, labelled as the seller's monitoring data.
  • Bills. The seller's electric bills for a stated period, with the seller's permission, described as the seller's bills for that household. Not a projection.
  • Other upgrades, each with a year and a document: heat pump or furnace, attic and wall insulation, windows, water heater, air sealing, EV charger with its circuit, smart thermostat, roof age under the panels.
  • Any energy rating or audit, only if a report exists, with its date.
  • Incentives. Do not state what credits or rebates a buyer may receive; rules change and depend on the buyer. If the seller received one, that is the seller's business and not a selling point.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in the details fields, as for any listing.

Shooting the upgrades so they are visible

  1. The house from the street with the panels visible, at an hour when the roof is lit and the panels are not a glare. If the array is on the back roof, a hired drone still shows it; typical U.S. rates as of August 2026 are $150 to $350 for a session.
  2. The inverter and the monitoring display, with the production figure readable if the seller agrees.
  3. The battery, with its label.
  4. The EV charger, with the garage or driveway context.
  5. The heat pump outdoor unit and the indoor head or air handler, and the thermostat.
  6. The water heater, with its label and date.
  7. Attic insulation, from the hatch, with the depth visible, and a new window close enough to see the frame.
  8. The roof under the panels, if a photo from the install exists and the seller allows its use, labelled with its date.
  9. The house, in the usual order.

In the listing video these are two or three scenes near the end, after the rooms, unless the buyer pool is specifically looking for low running costs, in which case bring the panel exterior forward. The equipment does not carry a vertical social video; the house does, with a caption that names the upgrades.

Claims that get solar listings in trouble

  • Savings predictions. A percentage or a dollar figure the buyer will save is a guess about a household you have not met. Give the seller's bills and stop.
  • Owned when it is leased. Check the contract. The difference changes the sale.
  • Rounded system size. Use the figure on the paperwork.
  • Incentive promises. You do not know the buyer's situation or next year's rules.
  • Panels cleaned up with an edit. A tidy or sky edit can change the array's shape or remove a panel. Compare against the original and keep the disclosure; the real roof is what the inspector sees.
  • No roof age. A buyer who knows solar asks how old the roof under the array is.

Worked example: a 4-bed with an owned 7.2 kW system

A 4-bed, 2.5-bath with a 7.2-kilowatt, 18-panel system installed in 2021 by a named installer, owned and paid off, a 13-kilowatt-hour battery added in 2023, a heat pump replacing the furnace in 2022, a Level 2 charger on a 50-amp circuit in the garage, and attic insulation topped up in 2020 with a receipt. The seller exports twelve months of monitoring data and twelve months of bills and agrees to both being summarized. The agent shoots the house from the street at 9 a.m. when the south roof is lit, then the inverter and display, the battery, the charger, the heat pump inside and out, the water heater label, the attic from the hatch, and the house. The page reads: solar owned outright, 7.2 kW, 18 panels, installed 2021, installer named, battery 13 kWh 2023, production for the stated twelve months per the seller's monitoring, electric bills for the same period per the seller, heat pump 2022, charger on a 50-amp circuit, attic insulation 2020, roof 2019 under the array. No savings figure anywhere. The street frame with the panels visible is the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a listing say about solar panels?

Whether they are owned or under a lease or agreement, in the contract's words; the system size, panel count, install year and installer from the paperwork; and the seller's bills or production for a stated period, labelled as the seller's. No savings predictions.

Can I say how much a buyer will save?

No. The buyer's usage, rates and situation are not the seller's. Give the seller's actual bills for a stated period and let the buyer draw their own conclusion.

Should I mention tax credits or rebates?

No. Incentive rules change and depend on the buyer. If the seller received one, it is not a selling point for the next owner.

Which photos prove the upgrades are real?

The panels from the street or a drone still, the inverter and monitoring display, the battery, the charger, the heat pump, the water heater label and the attic insulation from the hatch. Buyers and inspectors look for exactly these.
