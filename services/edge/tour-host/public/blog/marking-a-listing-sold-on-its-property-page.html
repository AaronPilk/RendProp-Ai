Marking a listing sold on its property page

Mark the page sold the day the sale closes and keep the link live, so old texts, posts and signs tell late visitors the home is gone.

Mark the property page sold on the day the sale closes, and keep the link alive. Every text, post, QR code and MLS tour link that pointed at the page still exists after closing, so the page has to tell a late visitor that the home has sold instead of offering them a showing. Do not delete it: a sold page is a quiet record of your work, and a seller researching agents next spring may find it.

The mistake is not forgetting to mark it sold. It is thinking the page stopped mattering when the deal closed.

Why the link outlives the listing

Count where the link went during the listing. A text to the seller, who forwarded it to family. A post on Instagram and Facebook. The QR code on the sign rider. The email to your database. The MLS tour field, syndicated to every portal. A buyer who saved it in March may open it in June. A neighbour who scanned the sign while walking the dog may send it to a friend in August.

If the page has vanished, each of those people gets an error and assumes you are gone too. If the page is unchanged, each of them fills in the form for a home that sold, and you have a conversation that starts with disappointment. If the page says sold, each of them learns the truth in one glance and, if they are looking, now knows who sold a home on that street.

What the sold page should do

  • Say sold, clearly, near the top. Not in a footnote. The visitor should not have to scroll to find out.
  • Keep the photos and the video. They show what you can do. A buyer who missed this home may want the next one like it.
  • Keep your contact card. This is the one place where a late visitor turning into a lead is pure upside.
  • Stop inviting a showing. A form that asks "what date would you like to view?" on a sold home is a trap for the visitor and a waste of your inbox. The sold state should change what the page asks for, or remove the ask.
  • Leave the sold price off unless your rules and the seller allow it. Some sellers do not want the closing price displayed, and your MLS may have its own position on it, so check. The page can say sold without saying for how much.

A worked example: the townhouse that closed on a Friday

A townhouse listed in April, went under contract in May, closed on a Friday in June. Friday afternoon, you mark the listing sold. The page now reads sold at the top. The sign comes down the following Tuesday; in between, two people scan it and see a sold home with your name on it. One of them is thinking of listing her own townhouse in the same complex. She taps your number.

In September, the buyer's mother opens the link she was sent in May to show a friend what her son bought. The page is still there, says sold, shows the home at its best. The friend asks who the agent was.

Neither of those would happen if the page had been deleted, and both would have gone worse if the page had still been asking for a showing date.

Under contract is a separate state

Between contract and closing, the page should not say sold, because the deal can fall through and you do not want to re-launch a page that announced a sale. Many agents leave the page unchanged during this period and keep collecting backup enquiries, which is reasonable. If your MLS shows the listing as pending, the page saying nothing is not misleading; it is simply not yet updated. Decide your own rule and apply it to every listing.

Sold-page mistakes that cost you later

  • Deleting the page. Every old link breaks. A sign scanned the week after closing shows an error.
  • Never marking it. The page keeps producing showing requests for a home that closed, and each one is an awkward reply.
  • Marking it sold at contract. The deal falls through in week three and you now have a sold page for an active listing.
  • Cancelling the hosting. If the page lives on a separate monthly tool, cancelling to save money kills the link. Weigh what the link is still worth before you do.
  • Forgetting the MLS tour link. The MLS keeps the listing visible as sold, with the tour link attached. That link should land on a page that says sold too.

The closing-day routine

Add one line to your closing checklist, right after the keys: mark the page sold. It takes less than a minute and it is easy to forget in the rush. If you run a team, assign it to whoever handles the MLS status change so the two happen together.

The next step: look at any listing you closed in the last six months and open its page. If it still asks for a showing date, mark it sold today. FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I delete the property page after the home sells?

No. Links to it exist in texts, posts, signs and the MLS, and they do not disappear with the sale. Mark the page sold and keep it live so late visitors see the truth and your name rather than an error.

Should the sold page show the closing price?

Only if the seller is comfortable, and after checking your MLS's current rules. The page can say sold without a figure. When in doubt, leave the price off the sold page.

When should I mark the page sold: at contract or at closing?

At closing. Deals fall through, and a page that announced a sale in week two of a contract is awkward to reverse. During the contract period, leave the page as it is and keep collecting backup enquiries.

What happens to enquiries that come in after the home sells?

Reply, briefly, with the truth: the home has sold, and you are happy to help them find something similar. A sold page should reduce these by telling visitors up front, but the ones that still arrive are warm leads.
