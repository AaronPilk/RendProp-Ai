Copy for a gym page: memberships, classes and the first visit

A gym page tells a stranger what memberships exist, what classes run and when, and exactly what happens on the first visit. That last paragraph converts.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A gym page should tell a stranger three things: what memberships exist and roughly what each includes, what classes run and when, and exactly what happens on their first visit (who meets them, what to bring, how long it takes). The first-visit paragraph is the one that turns a reader into a person at the front desk, because fear of the first visit is what keeps most people from coming.

A gym page should tell a stranger three things: what memberships exist and roughly what each includes, what classes run and when, and exactly what happens on their first visit. “Walk in any weekday before 8 pm, ask for the front desk, and someone will show you the floor and set you up for a first session; bring shoes and a water bottle, allow 40 minutes.” That paragraph does more than any photo, because the thing keeping most people from a gym is not price. It is not knowing what will happen when they walk in.

Gym copy tends to be written for people who already belong. Class names with no explanation, membership tiers with no prices, and a tone that assumes the reader is confident. The reader who needs the page most is the one who has not been inside a gym in five years and is deciding whether to risk it. Write for that person and the regulars will not mind.

Memberships, in plain terms

Name each option, say what it includes, say the commitment, and state the join fee if there is one. If you do not publish prices, say how pricing works and where to get it in one step. “Open gym: all equipment and the locker rooms, month to month, no join fee. Classes: open gym plus unlimited classes, month to month. Personal training: by session or in blocks of ten; ask at the desk.” Avoid tier names that mean nothing to a stranger; “Gold” and “Elite” make the reader work out which one is the normal one.

Say how to cancel. A sentence like “cancel any time with 30 days' notice, in person or by email” removes the single biggest fear about joining a gym, and it costs you the members who would have left angry anyway.

Classes as a schedule, with levels

Put the timetable on the page as text, by day, with the class name, the time, the length and the level. Then give each class a one-line description a beginner can understand: “Strength basics, 45 minutes, all levels: barbell and dumbbell work with a coach, groups of eight.” Say whether classes need booking and how. If a class is hard, say so plainly, and say which one to start with.

The first visit, scripted

This paragraph is worth more than the rest of the page combined. Write it as a sequence of what will happen, in the order it happens.

  • When to come and who to ask for: “Any weekday before 8 pm; ask for whoever is at the front desk.”
  • What happens first: “They will walk you round the floor, show you the locker rooms and ask what you want to do.”
  • What to bring: “Indoor shoes, a water bottle, a towel if you want to shower.”
  • How long: “Allow 40 minutes, or an hour if you want to try a class.”
  • What it costs: “The first visit is free” only if that is true and current; otherwise state the day rate.
  • How to book it if booking helps: “Pick a date on the form below and someone will be expecting you.”

The room, briefly

After the three blocks, describe what is there: the floor space, the equipment in categories, the hours, parking, showers, the quiet hours and the busy ones. “Six racks, a 20-metre turf strip, cardio along the windows; quietest between 10 and 2, busiest 5 to 7.” The post on photographing a gym when it is busy and when it is empty covers how the photos should match that.

One gym page, before and after

Before: “Join the Elite family and become the best version of you. Flexible membership tiers to fit every goal. Over 40 classes a week. Results guaranteed. Contact us for pricing.”

After: “Open gym is month to month with no join fee and includes all equipment and the locker rooms; add unlimited classes for a second tier; personal training is by session. Cancel with 30 days' notice. Classes run from 6 am to 8 pm, timetable below with levels. First visit: come any weekday before 8 pm, ask at the front desk, bring indoor shoes and water, allow 40 minutes. Pick a date on the form if you want someone expecting you.”

Where gym copy puts people off

  • “Contact us for pricing” on everything. The reader assumes it is expensive and that joining involves a sales pitch.
  • Promised results. You cannot guarantee what someone else's body does, and the claim invites a complaint.
  • Intimidating language. “No excuses”, “beast mode”, “earn it”. The person who needs the gym most reads that and closes the page.
  • Class names with no descriptions. A beginner cannot tell a conditioning class from a mobility class by its name.
  • No cancellation terms. Silence here is read as a trap.
  • Describing the member instead of the gym. “For serious athletes only” or “perfect for busy mums” describes people. Describe the floor, the classes and the hours, and let readers decide.
  • Photos that are not your gym. Members notice at the first visit.

Write the first-visit paragraph tonight

Before anything else on the page changes, write the six-line first-visit sequence and put it above the class schedule. It is the paragraph the nervous reader is looking for, and most gym pages do not have it.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a gym publish membership prices on its page?

If you can, yes, in plain terms with what each option includes and the commitment. If pricing varies too much to publish, say how it works and where to get a number in one step. Contact us for pricing on every option makes readers assume the worst and expect a sales pitch.

What should the first-visit paragraph include?

When to come, who to ask for, what will happen in order, what to bring, how long it takes, what it costs, and how to book if booking helps. Written as a sequence, it removes the uncertainty that keeps most people from walking in.

How should classes be described for beginners?

With the time, the length, the level and one plain line on what happens in the room: the equipment, the group size, whether there is a coach. Say which class to start with. Class names alone tell a beginner nothing.

Is it a mistake to say results are guaranteed?

Yes. You cannot promise what someone else's body will do, and the claim invites complaints and undermines the rest of the page. Describe the coaching, the equipment and the schedule, and let the first visit do the convincing.
