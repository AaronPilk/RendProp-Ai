Video versus photos: what to do when the seller wants both

When a seller wants video and photos, give both from one shoot: photos as the base, the video built from them, a walkthrough only if flow is the story.

When a seller wants both video and photos, give them both, but from one shoot: the photos are the base asset, and the listing video is built from them. That removes the second appointment, the second invoice and the mismatch between a video shot on Thursday and photos shot on Tuesday. A separately recorded walkthrough is a third thing, worth adding only when the flow of the home is the story and stills cannot tell it.

Why the seller asks for both

Usually one of three reasons. They saw a neighbour's listing with a video and assume it is the standard. They worry that photos alone make the house look smaller than it is. Or they want to see that you are doing more than the last agent did. None of these is a request for a film crew. They are a request for the home to be shown moving, and for proof of effort. Both are satisfied by a listing video that exists on the page and a reel that exists on social, whatever it was built from.

What photos do that video cannot

Photos are what buyers scroll, compare, save and send. They sit on the portals, in the MLS, in the email to your database and on the flyer. A buyer deciding between three homes on a Sunday night opens three galleries, not three videos. Photos also survive bad connections and silent phones. If the photo set is weak, no video rescues the listing.

What video does that photos cannot

Video shows scale and sequence. A 1,400-square-foot ranch can look like 1,100 or 1,800 in stills depending on the lens; a video that moves from the entry through the living room to the kitchen settles it. Video also carries a voice. Thirty seconds of you saying what the house is and who it suits does what twelve captions cannot. And on social, video is what gets shown; a still of a kitchen rarely leaves your followers.

The conversation: agreeing what both means

Say this to the seller, in roughly these words. There will be a full photo set, about thirty photos, shot by a photographer on one morning. From those photos there will be a listing video on the property page and a short vertical video for social, with a voiceover and captions. If the house's layout needs it, there will also be a recorded walkthrough on a later visit. Then show them a past listing's page on your phone with the video playing. Most sellers say yes before you finish the sentence, because what they wanted was the video, not the videographer.

A 4-bed with a tricky layout

A 4-bed colonial where the kitchen is reached through the dining room and the family room is a half-level down. In stills the house reads as choppy. The photographer shoots the full set at 9 a.m., including two frames from each doorway looking into the next room. The listing video built from those photos carries a buyer through the house, front to entry to dining to kitchen and down to the family room, then bedrooms and the yard, because the frames exist to do it. The voiceover says the half-level is a few steps, not a staircase. The seller watches it and stops asking for a film crew. Two weeks later, after buyer feedback that the layout is still unclear from the video, you record a handheld walkthrough on the next visit and add it to the page. That is both, done in the right order.

When to add a recorded walkthrough

  • Feedback from showings says the layout surprised people, in either direction.
  • The home has long sightlines or a view that opens as you walk, which stills flatten.
  • The seller is out of state and buyers are too, and the page is doing the job of a first showing.

A walkthrough is handheld footage, separate from the photo-based video, and viewers can watch it or scroll it. It is not a replacement for the photo set and it is not drone footage.

Where the both conversation goes wrong

  • Booking a videographer because the seller said the word video. Ask what they want the video to show first.
  • A video built from a different edit of the photos than the gallery, so the kitchen is warm in one and cool in the other.
  • A long cinematic video on the page and nothing vertical for social, or the reverse. Each channel needs its format.
  • Promising the seller a drone shot when the lot is a quarter acre between two neighbours.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Does a listing video built from photos look like a slideshow?

Not when scene order and camera motion are planned for the photos rather than applied as a generic pan. The difference is in the pacing and the voiceover. A thirty-second cut with a clear script and captions reads as a listing video, not a slideshow.

When is a filmed video worth the extra cost?

When the flow of the home is the selling point and stills flatten it, or when buyers are remote and the page is acting as a first showing. In those cases record a handheld walkthrough on a second visit and add it beside the photo-based video.

What should I tell a seller who insists on a drone shot?

Stand in the street together and look up. If the lot is the story, book an aerial session at $150–350 at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026. If the roof and two neighbours are the story, explain that and spend the money on twilight exteriors instead.

Should the video and the photos use the same edits?

Yes. If the kitchen has a tidy-the-room edit in the gallery, the video should use the same version, and both should carry the same disclosure. Buyers notice when the same room looks different in two places.
