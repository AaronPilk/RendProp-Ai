Marketing a condo in a high-rise

A high-rise condo sells on floor, exposure and the view, then the walk from the lobby to the door. Shoot the view first and show the building honestly.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A high-rise condo sells on the floor number, the exposure and what the window actually shows, then on the walk from the lobby to the front door and the amenities along the way. Shoot the view first at the hour the sun is not in the glass, state the floor and exposure in the first line, and show the lobby, elevator and hallway as they are so the buyer arrives without surprises.

A high-rise condo sells on three numbers and one walk: the floor, the exposure, the view from the main window, and the route from the lobby to the front door. Shoot the view first, at an hour when the sun is not pointing into the glass, put the floor and exposure in the first line of the page, and show the lobby, elevator bank and hallway as they are. Buyers in a tower are comparing units in the same building and the one next door; precise beats pretty.

Shooting a unit in a tower

  1. The view, from the main window, at the right hour. A south exposure is easiest mid-morning or mid-afternoon; east wants afternoon, west wants morning, so the sun is behind the building and not in the lens. Window pull exposure so the skyline reads.
  2. The living room toward the window. Lights on, blinds fully up, reflections checked. Stand so the camera does not appear in the glass.
  3. The balcony or terrace, out and back. One frame looking out over the rail, one from the rail back into the unit.
  4. Kitchen, dining, primary bedroom, baths, second bedroom or den. Any room with the view gets shot toward it.
  5. Storage and parking. The numbered space, the storage cage, the bike room. Not pretty, often decisive.
  6. The walk. With the building manager's go-ahead: the lobby from the entrance, the elevator bank, the hallway toward the unit door. Residents out of frame.
  7. Amenities, empty. Gym, pool, roof deck, lounge, package room. Early morning is usually quietest.
  8. The building from across the street, with the unit's position described in the text.

The listing video opens on the view, moves through the unit, then steps out to the amenities and finishes at the lobby or the street. Portrait photos of the view, the balcony and the kitchen make the vertical social video, which for condos travels further than the landscape listing video because the audience scrolls on a phone on the train.

Worked example: a 2-bed on the 22nd floor

A 2-bed, 2-bath on the 22nd floor of a 34-storey building, south exposure, a 60-square-foot balcony, one deeded parking space and a storage cage. The photographer arrives at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday, shoots the view with the sun behind the building, then the living room, balcony out and in, kitchen, primary bedroom toward the window, both baths, the second bedroom set up as an office, the parking space with its number visible, the cage, and, after a word with the concierge, the lobby, elevator bank and hallway with nobody in them, then the gym and roof deck at 10:40 before anyone is up there. Fourteen landscape frames make the listing video; the view is the main photo. The page's first line reads: 22nd floor, south-facing, direct skyline view, one deeded space and a storage cage. The monthly fee is stated as the association's current figure. Four portrait frames make the social video.

What the page needs to state

  • Floor, exposure, and an honest view description: direct, partial, over a lower roof, across to the next tower.
  • The monthly fee as the association currently states it, and where a buyer can read what it covers. Do not summarize the association's documents yourself; point to them.
  • Parking and storage, deeded or assigned, as the documents say.
  • Pet, rental and move-in policies described as the building's current rules, with a note to confirm with management. Never state them as settled fact in your own words.
  • Amenities actually in the building, named plainly.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet belong in the details fields, not in the text.

Tower listings that fall flat

  • The view shot into the sun. A white window and a dark room. Come back at a different hour.
  • Reflections. The photographer, the tripod, a lamp, all floating in the skyline. Turn off lights behind the camera for the view frame and shoot at an angle.
  • A blue sky edit that rebuilds the skyline. AI edits can move or remove buildings. Compare against the original before using one and keep the disclosure on it.
  • Hiding the crane. If a tower is going up in front of the window, the buyer will see it at the showing. Show it and say what you know about it, and nothing you do not.
  • People in the gym and the pool. Residents did not agree to be in your listing. Shoot early and empty.
  • Skipping the walk. A long hallway or a slow elevator is part of the product. Show it.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should the first photo of a high-rise condo be?

The view from the main window, shot at an hour when the sun is behind the building. It is the thing that differs most between units in the same tower and the first thing a buyer compares.

Can I photograph the lobby, gym and pool?

Ask the building manager first, then shoot early when they are empty. Residents did not agree to appear in a listing, and empty amenity photos read better anyway.

How do I describe the monthly fee and building rules?

State the fee as the association's current figure and point buyers to the association's documents for what it covers and for pet, rental and move-in rules. Do not restate the rules in your own words.

Should a condo listing have a vertical video as well as a landscape listing video?

Yes. Condo buyers scroll on phones, and a vertical social video made from portrait photos of the view, the balcony and the kitchen is the version that gets shared.
