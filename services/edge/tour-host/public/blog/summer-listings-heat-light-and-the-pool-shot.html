Summer listings: heat, light and the pool shot

Shoot summer listings at the edges of the day, keep the house cool and the blinds managed, and get the pool shot when the water is still and lit.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Summer listings are shot at the edges of the day: early morning for the front and the interiors, late evening for the pool and the back yard. Midday sun flattens exteriors and heats the house for showings. The pool shot needs clean water, no cover, no pool toys, and the hour when the sun is low and the water is still.

Shoot a summer listing at the edges of the day: the front of the house and the interiors in the early morning, the pool and the back yard in the last hour of light. Midday sun burns out the sky, throws hard shadows across the facade and heats the house up for anyone touring it. The pool shot is a separate job with its own hour, and it is usually the photo that gets clicked, so it gets its own paragraph in the shoot plan.

Why midday is the wrong time, even though it is convenient

A photographer with four shoots on a July Saturday wants to do yours at one in the afternoon. Push back. At midday the sun is overhead, so the roofline shades the front wall, the windows go black, and a white sky replaces any detail above the house. Inside, the rooms facing the sun are blown out at the windows and the rooms facing away are dim by comparison. Every one of those problems is cheaper to avoid with a better hour than to fix later.

Early morning gives you soft light on an east-facing front and cooler rooms, which matters because an air conditioner working hard shows up as closed blinds and whirring fans in the photos. Evening gives you warm light on a west-facing front, a lit pool and a yard that looks like a place to sit rather than a place to squint.

The pool shot, step by step

  1. Service the pool two days before. Clear water with the pump running the day before, not the morning of. Cloudy water does not photograph as clean no matter what you do afterwards.
  2. Remove everything that floats. Toys, the robot cleaner, the skimmer basket lid, the cover and the cover reel if it can be moved. One pool noodle in the corner reads as a rental.
  3. Turn the pump off before the shot. Still water reflects the house and the sky. Moving water shows ripples and the return jets.
  4. Wait for the low sun. The hour before sunset, with the sun behind or beside the camera, lights the water without a white glare across it. If the pool has lights, shoot again once they come on.
  5. Frame the pool with the house. A photo of water alone could be any pool. Show the patio, the back of the house and the seating, so the buyer sees the life around it.
  6. Shoot a portrait version. A vertical frame of the pool with the house behind it is the strongest opening for a summer social video.

Interiors in heat: blinds, glare and the thermostat

Set the thermostat low the night before so the house is comfortable without every ceiling fan running during the shoot; spinning fans blur in photos and still fans look fine. Open blinds in rooms that face away from the sun, and angle rather than close them in rooms that face into it. A photographer can balance a bright window with a bracketed exposure; a closed blind cannot be opened later.

Fresh flowers wilt by noon in a hot house. If the sellers want them, bring them in the morning of the shoot and shoot those rooms first.

A ranch with a pool on Juniper Way: the two-visit shoot

A three-bed ranch with a kidney-shaped pool, front facing east, back facing west, listing planned for the second Thursday in July. The photographer booked two short visits instead of one long one. Monday at seven in the morning: the front exterior in soft light, then every interior room with landscape photos for the listing video and portrait frames of the kitchen and the sliding door out to the pool. The house was cool, the blinds were managed room by room, and the whole visit took under an hour.

Monday evening, back at the house an hour before sunset. Pump off, cover off, the sellers had taken the kids' floats to the garage. Pool and patio from three angles, the back of the house lit by the low sun, and two frames after the pool lights came on. The evening pool photo became the main photo. The video opened on it and finished on the lit pool at dusk. The seller's one request, that the pool look like it did on a weekend evening, was answered by shooting it on a weekday evening.

Summer mistakes that show up in the gallery

  • Brown patches from the heat. A green lawn edit on a lawn that is dormant from drought is a conversation with the buyer at the showing. If the grass will not be green when they visit, show it as it is and let the price and the pool do the work.
  • A white sky in every exterior. Shooting at the right hour fixes this; a blue sky edit afterwards is a fallback, carries a disclosure, and should not add clouds the day did not have.
  • The hose, the sprinkler, the trash cans. Summer yards fill up with equipment. Walk the yard before the first frame.
  • Showings in a hot house. Ask the sellers to pre-cool before every showing. Buyers remember how a house felt.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What time of day should a pool be photographed?

The last hour before sunset, with the pump off so the water is still. The low sun lights the water without a hard glare, and if the pool has lights a second frame after they come on often becomes the main photo. Midday pool photos are mostly reflection and white sky.

Should the pool cover be on or off in the photos?

Off, and moved out of frame if it can be. A covered pool reads as a closed pool. Take the cover off the morning of the shoot, clear floating toys and the robot cleaner, and run the pump the day before rather than the hour before.

Can I fix a summer-burned lawn with an edit?

Only if the lawn will be green when buyers visit, and the edit carries a disclosure. If the yard is brown from drought and will stay that way through the listing, show it honestly. A buyer who finds a different yard than the one in the photos stops trusting the rest of the listing.

Do I need a separate evening visit for a summer listing?

For a home with a pool or a west-facing back yard, yes. A short morning visit for the front and the interiors and a short evening visit for the pool produce better photos than one long midday session, and most photographers will schedule it that way if you ask early.
