High interest rates and listing marketing: what changes

When rates are high, buyers compare harder and visit less. Marketing shifts to winning the comparison on the page and earning every showing.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. When rates are high, buyers compare harder, visit fewer homes and think in monthly cost rather than list price, so the marketing has to win a comparison it never sees. That means a page that answers the questions buyers are using to rank listings against each other, a video that earns the showing before the buyer commits a Saturday to it, and facts about running costs that let the buyer do their own arithmetic without guessing.

When rates are high, buyers compare harder, visit fewer homes and think in monthly cost rather than list price, and the marketing has to change with them. The listing has to win a comparison it never sees, earn every showing before it happens, and give the buyer the facts to do their own sums. Nothing about the house changes; what changes is how much of the decision happens on the page.

Each shift has a reason. Buyers compare harder because the cost of being wrong is larger. They visit fewer homes because each showing is a commitment they are less sure they can follow through on. And because they think in monthly cost, the facts that feed that arithmetic (taxes, association fees, utilities, what has been replaced) matter more than they did.

How a buyer behaves when borrowing is expensive

They shortlist longer and visit shorter. A buyer who might once have seen eight homes on a Saturday sees three, and chooses those three from the page, not from the drive-by. They re-open the same listing several times over a fortnight, waiting to see whether it moves. They forward it to someone whose opinion they trust before they commit a weekend. They ask the agent fewer questions by phone and more by form, because a form does not commit them to anything. And when they do visit, they arrive already knowing what they think and looking for the reason to confirm or abandon it.

None of this is a reason to market less. It is a reason to put more of the showing on the page, because that is where the decision to visit is being made.

Two listings, one Saturday, one page that answered

Two similar three-beds on neighbouring streets. One has a gallery and a paragraph. The other has a video that walks the house in order, a page with the roof and furnace ages, the tax figure as a matter of record, the association fee and what it covers, and a short list of what was replaced in the last five years. A buyer who can afford one Saturday showing opens both on Thursday night, watches the video twice, reads the facts list, forwards the second page to a parent, and fills in the enquiry form for Saturday morning. The first listing gets a drive-by on the way to the second. At the showing the buyer asks about the windows, which the page had not covered, and nothing else, because the page had covered the rest.

Neither house was better. One of them had done the comparison work for the buyer, and the buyer, who was being careful, rewarded that.

What to change in your marketing

  • Put the running-cost facts on the page. Taxes as recorded, association fees and what they cover, utility figures if the seller will share them, the ages of the systems that cost money to replace. Do not advise; present. The arithmetic is the buyer's and their lender's.
  • Make the video do the first showing. Walking order, calm camera, captions for watching on mute, a voiceover that says in thirty seconds what the house is. This is what wins the Saturday slot.
  • Answer the comparison questions. Which way it faces, the parking, the laundry, the yard size, the drive times. The buyer is ranking listings on a spreadsheet, literally or in their head. Give them the cells.
  • Reply by form, fast. A cautious buyer who filled in a form at 10pm wants an answer at 8am, with a showing time, not a phone call.
  • Keep the photos honest. A careful buyer who visits a home that looked better online does not negotiate. They leave and tell the parent they forwarded it to.
  • Refresh on a schedule. The buyer is re-opening the page over two weeks. Give them something new to see in week two: a different video opening, a new main photo.

What goes wrong when buyers are cautious

  • Marketing as if buyers are in a hurry. Scarcity language, just listed urgency, a launch with no plan for week three. The careful buyer is not moved by urgency; they are moved by information.
  • Leaving the costs out. If the taxes and fees are not on the page, the buyer either guesses high or asks, and asking is the step a cautious buyer avoids.
  • Selling with the price alone. A reduction announcement with nothing else changed tells the buyer to wait for the next one. Pair any price change with a refreshed video and a reason.
  • Overpromising in the photos. This buyer visits once and compares hard. Compare every edit with the original before publishing, keep the disclosure, and keep the wide lens honest.
  • Making financial claims. Statements about what a buyer can afford, what rates will do or what a payment will be belong to the lender. Keep them off the page.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How does listing marketing change when interest rates are high?

Buyers compare more and visit less, so the page has to do the first showing. Put running-cost facts and the system ages on the page, make the video walk the home in order, answer the comparison questions buyers rank listings on, and reply to form enquiries quickly with a showing time.

Should the listing page talk about monthly payments or financing?

No. Present the facts that feed the buyer's arithmetic, such as taxes as recorded and association fees, and leave the payment, the rate and what the buyer can afford to the buyer and their lender. Financial claims on a listing page are a liability, not a selling point.

Does urgency language work with cautious buyers?

Rarely. A buyer who is being careful with an expensive loan responds to information, not pressure. A complete page and an honest video move them; just-listed urgency and scarcity phrases make them wait.

Why do honest photos matter more when buyers are cautious?

Because the buyer visits once, compares hard and forwards the listing to someone they trust. A home that looked better online loses that buyer and the person they forwarded it to. Compare every edit with the original and keep the wide shots truthful about scale.
