Marketing a listing when inventory is low

When few homes are for sale, attention is not the problem; handling it is. Pre-show with the page and video, batch the showings, stay honest.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

When inventory is low, attention is not the problem. Every listing gets opened, every open house gets a line, and the enquiries arrive in a bunch on launch night. The problem is handling it without burning out the seller, missing a serious buyer in the pile or letting the pressure push the marketing toward exaggeration. Attention is not the problem

In a thin market the scarce resource is the seller's tolerance and your Saturday, not buyer interest. Thirty showing requests for a home that can take twelve means eighteen conversations that start with no. A line of forty at an open house means a seller who will not do a second one. And a bidding situation built on photos that flattered the house means a buyer who renegotiates after the inspection, or walks, and a listing that goes back on the market with a story attached.

So the marketing optimises for different things. Not reach, but clarity: a buyer who has watched the video and read the page knows whether this is their house before they book. Not urgency, which the market already supplies, but order: showings grouped, replies templated, the seller's week protected. And not polish, but accuracy: the listing that wins in a thin market is the one whose offers close.

Twenty-two showing requests on a Thursday night

A three-bed in a neighbourhood with almost nothing else for sale. The page goes live at 2pm with the video, the facts and the enquiry form. By 10pm there are twenty-two enquiries in the app, each with a requested date. The agent sorts them by date, groups Saturday into four slots of forty minutes and Sunday into three, and sends every enquirer the same reply: the link to the page again, the video, a note that showings are grouped, and the two slots nearest their requested date. Six people reply that they have watched the video and will pass, which is six slots freed for buyers who are serious. Four ask for a second slot after their first. The seller is out of the house for two half-days instead of a week of evenings.

Every reply is the same. Every buyer gets the same information and the same slots offered in the same way, which is both fair and simpler. The offers that arrive on Monday are from buyers who have seen the house twice, once on a screen and once in person, and the inspection changes nothing because the photos did not promise anything the house did not have.

Use the page to filter

  • Put the dealbreakers on the page. The busy road, the one bathroom, the sloped yard, the shared driveway. In a thin market buyers will come anyway; the ones who come knowing are the ones who offer.
  • Make the video a real walk. Walking order, honest scale, no effects. A buyer who has walked it on a phone and still books is a buyer worth a slot.
  • Ask for a date. An enquiry form that collects the day the buyer wants turns a pile of messages into a schedule.
  • Reply to everyone the same way. Same information, same slots, same tone. It protects you and it is quicker.
  • Keep the seller's calendar. Group the showings, cap the open house, tell the seller the plan before launch so they do not learn it from the doorbell.

What goes wrong under pressure

  • Exaggerating because you can. A thin market tempts the wide lens, the greener lawn, the staged room that is larger than life. The offers come in high and the inspection brings them down, or the buyer walks and the relisting carries the story. Compare every edit with its original, keep the disclosure, and keep the room honest.
  • Treating enquiries differently. Fast replies to some and silence to others is unfair, and it looks it. One template, everyone.
  • Letting the open house run the launch. A line around the block is a photo for your feed and a bad afternoon for the seller. Use the page to pre-show and the slots to control the flow.
  • Skipping the page because the MLS is enough. It is enough for attention. It is not enough for qualifying, batching or replying, and those are the jobs now.
  • Forgetting the second-best buyer. When the first deal falls through, the listing that replied to everyone has a list to go back to.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should listing marketing focus on when there are few homes for sale?

Qualifying rather than attracting. A video and a page that do the first showing so unready buyers screen themselves out, an enquiry form that collects requested dates so showings can be grouped, one consistent reply to everyone, and photos honest enough that offers survive the inspection.

How do I handle dozens of showing requests at once?

Sort them by the date each buyer asked for, group showings into slots the seller can live with, and send every enquirer the same reply with the page link, the video and the two nearest slots. Some will watch the video and pass, which frees slots for serious buyers.

Is it a problem to exaggerate photos in a sellers market?

Yes, more than usual. Offers made on flattering photos get renegotiated after the inspection or fall through, and the relisting carries the story. Compare every edit with its original, keep the disclosure, and keep the wide shots honest about scale.

Should I still build a property page if the MLS alone brings enough buyers?

Yes. The MLS brings attention; the page does the pre-showing, collects requested dates and gives you one link to put in every reply. Those are the jobs that matter when attention is not the problem.
