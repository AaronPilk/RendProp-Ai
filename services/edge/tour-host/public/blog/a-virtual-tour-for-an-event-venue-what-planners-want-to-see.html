A virtual tour for an event venue: what planners want to see

Planners want the room empty and set, a capacity they can trust, the load-in route and a quick way to ask about a date. One page with a video covers it.

Planners want four things from a virtual tour of an event venue: the main room shown both empty and set for an event, a capacity figure they can trust for seated and standing layouts, the practical route from the parking lot through load-in to the room, and a quick way to ask whether a specific date is open. A photo-based video on a single page, with capacity and hours typed in and an enquiry form that asks for the date, covers all four before anyone drives out for a site visit.

The planner is comparing you against five open tabs

A corporate planner booking a 120-person dinner has your venue open in one tab and four others beside it. She is not looking for atmosphere yet. She is checking whether the room physically works: can it seat 120 at rounds with a dance floor, is there a separate space for cocktails, where does the band go, and is there a kitchen or a staging area for the caterer. If the tour cannot answer those in the first minute, she closes the tab and moves to one that can.

That is why the empty room shot matters more than the beautifully dressed one. Dressed photos show what a past client paid for. The empty room shows the planner what she has to work with.

What to photograph, in order

  1. The main room, empty, from the entrance. One wide shot from where guests walk in. This photo sets the scale.
  2. The main room, empty, from the far corner looking back. Shows the ceiling height, the windows, the columns and anything else that shapes a layout.
  3. The same room set for an event. Rounds of ten, a head table, a dance floor. If you have a recent event's photos with permission, use them; otherwise set a few tables yourself.
  4. The overflow space. A terrace, a lawn, a second room. Planners need to know where cocktails happen while the main room is flipped.
  5. Arrival. The drive, the entrance, the signage, the coat check. Guests experience this first and planners know it.
  6. Load-in. The service door, the corridor, the lift if there is one. This one photo saves three emails with the caterer and the AV company.
  7. Restrooms and the green room. Unglamorous and always asked about.

Shoot the empty room at the time of day an event would start. A ballroom at 11am with daylight through the windows looks different from the same ballroom at 7pm with the uplights on, and a planner booking an evening wedding wants the second one.

A worked example: a 200-seat converted hall

Say you run a converted hall with exposed beams that seats 200 at rounds or holds 300 standing. The listing video opens on the exterior at dusk, moves to the empty hall from the doors, then the same hall set for a wedding, then the terrace, then the catering corridor, then back to the exterior. Six to eight scenes, under a minute. Capacity on the page reads seated 200, standing 300. Hours show the earliest vendor access and the music cut-off. The enquiry form asks for the date. A planner who watches that video and fills in the form has told you the two things you need to quote: the date, and in the message, the headcount.

The capacity number has to be true

Venues get into trouble by listing the fire-code maximum as the capacity. A room that holds 300 standing shoulder to shoulder does not seat 300 at rounds with a buffet line and a stage. Give the seated number for the layout people actually book, and give the standing number separately. If you quote 250 seated and the planner's own layout shows 180, you have lost the booking and the referral that would have followed it.

Where venue tours go wrong

  • Only dressed photos. The planner cannot tell what is included and what the last client rented.
  • No sense of scale. Tight shots of centrepieces and chandeliers say nothing about whether 150 people fit.
  • Daytime photos for an evening venue. Shoot the room in the light your clients will use it in.
  • A contact page instead of a date question. A planner who has to write a paragraph to ask about availability writes it to the venue with the simpler form.
  • Heavy AI edits. Adding furniture or changing the room with an edit, then having the planner walk in and see something else, costs trust. If you use an AI edit on a venue photo, keep the disclosure and compare it against the original before it goes on the page.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a venue tour show the room empty or set up for an event?

Both, and empty first. The empty room tells a planner the real dimensions, ceiling height and where the columns are. The dressed version shows what is possible, but make clear which items are included and which were rented by a past client.

Is a photo-based video enough, or do planners need a recorded walkthrough?

A photo-based video answers the first-screen questions: scale, light, layout and arrival. A recorded handheld walkthrough is a separate, optional addition that lets a planner scroll the route from the door to the room at their own pace. Start with the photo video and add the walkthrough if planners keep asking about flow.

How should capacity be stated on the page?

Give the seated number for the layout people actually book and the standing number separately. Do not use the fire-code maximum as the headline figure. A planner whose own layout disagrees with your number will not come back.

What should the enquiry form ask a planner?

The date. That is the one question that decides whether the conversation continues. Rendprop's enquiry form asks for the date the visitor wants, and planners usually add the headcount in the message without being asked.
