Neighborhood content that supports a listing

Neighborhood posts help a listing when they answer the buyer's next question, show places not people, use facts you measured, and link back to the page.

Neighborhood content supports a listing when it answers the question a buyer asks right after seeing the home: how long is the walk to the station, what is the park actually like, is there somewhere to get coffee on a Saturday. Shoot it on your own phone, state only the distances and facts you measured yourself, show places rather than people, and send every post back to the property page. Describe the amenities and never the residents, and ask your broker about fair housing language before anything that sounds like a description of who lives there.

The next question, not the general area

A general area guide is content about you. A post that answers the specific next question is content about the listing. The difference is in where you start. Start with the home, imagine the buyer who just watched the reel, and write down the first three things they would ask if you were standing next to them. Those three questions are the three posts.

For a condo near a hospital, the questions are about parking, noise and the walk to work. For a three-bed near a rail line, they are about the train, the school crossing and whether you can hear the line from the yard. For a farmhouse, they are about the drive to the grocery store and what the road is like in winter. The content writes itself once the question is specific.

Three posts for a three-bed near the rail line

A three-bed on a quiet street four blocks from a commuter rail station, with a park at the end of the block and a Saturday market in the station square.

  1. The walk to the station. Walk it yourself with the phone recording, from the front door to the platform, and time it. The post is a 20-second cut of that walk with your voice: Front door to platform, eleven minutes at a normal pace, two crossings, both with lights. The caption gives the time and the link. Not the schedule, not the fare, not how long the commute is to anywhere; those change and they are the buyer's to check.
  2. The park. Shoot it on a weekday morning and a Saturday afternoon. Show the paths, the playground, the field, the benches. The caption says what is there and when you were there. If you heard the train from the park, say so.
  3. The Saturday market. Shoot the stalls, the square, the produce. Ask a stall holder before filming their stall up close. The caption gives the hours as posted on the market's own sign the day you were there, with the word currently, and the link to the listing.

Three posts over the first two weeks of the listing, each with a photo or clip you made, each ending with the listing's address area and the property page link. A buyer who watches all three has walked to the train, sat in the park and shopped the market before the first showing.

Facts you can state and facts you cannot

  • State: the walk you timed, the distance you drove, the hours posted on the door the day you went, the name of the park, what you saw.
  • Do not state: school ratings or rankings, crime figures, commute times you did not measure, property value trends, or anything about who lives in the area. Some of those are fair housing questions; some are just facts you do not have. Point buyers to the official sources and ask your broker how to phrase it.

Where neighborhood content goes wrong

  • Describing the people. Young families, professionals, a tight-knit community. Describe the park, the station and the market. Ask your broker before any sentence that characterises residents.
  • Stock photos. A photo of a different street in a different town shows the buyer somewhere they are not moving to. Shoot it yourself.
  • Filming people and businesses without asking. Faces, children in the playground, a shop interior. Ask, or shoot the exterior and the street.
  • No link to the listing. Neighborhood content with nowhere to go is a travel post. Every post ends at the property page.
  • Facts from memory. About a ten-minute walk, when it is eighteen. Time it.
  • Posting it instead of the listing content. Neighborhood posts fill the gaps between listing moments, as described in how often to post listing reels. They do not replace the launch reel or the open house story.

The same discipline applies to the written description; see describing a neighborhood without overpromising.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What neighborhood content actually helps a listing?

Posts that answer the buyer's next question after seeing the home: the timed walk to the station, what the park is like, where the Saturday market is. Specific, measured and linked back to the property page.

Can I talk about schools in neighborhood content?

Name the school the home is zoned for only if you have confirmed it with the district, and point buyers to the official source for anything else. Do not quote rankings or ratings. Ask your broker about fair housing language.

Should I use stock photos of the area?

No. Shoot it yourself, on the street the home is on and at the places you are describing. A stock photo of a different street misrepresents the area and buyers notice when they visit.

How many neighborhood posts should one listing get?

Two or three, spread across the first weeks of the listing in the gaps between the launch, the open house and any status change. Each one answers a different question and ends at the same property page link.
