Marketing an investment property to investors

Investors buy a return, not a kitchen. Lead with current rent, documented expenses, lease dates and system ages, and give the figures without a cap rate.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. An investor is buying a return and a set of risks, so the listing leads with current rent, lease end dates, documented expenses, vacancy history and the ages of the roof, heating and water heater, and gives those figures without computing a return for the buyer. Photos are fewer and plainer than for an owner-occupier, condition is shown rather than styled, and tenants are respected in both the photos and the showing schedule.

An investor is buying a return and a set of risks, and the listing should be written for the spreadsheet they will build. Lead with the current rent, the lease end dates, the expenses the seller can document, the vacancy history and the ages of the roof, heating, water heater and electrical. Give the figures and let the buyer compute their own return; do not do it for them. The photos are fewer and plainer than for a family home: condition shown, nothing styled, tenants out of frame. The buyer will inspect everything, so the listing's job is to make the inspection confirm the page.

Who is reading and where they find it

Small local investors who own a handful of doors and read listings at night; out-of-area investors who shop from a phone and depend on the page; and agents for both. They find listings in the MLS, on investor channels and through their networks, and they forward links to each other. The post on LinkedIn for investment listings covers one channel; the more general point is that a page with the numbers on it travels between investors better than a lifestyle video, and the branded link is the one to send, because it carries the enquiry form.

The page is the offering memorandum, in one screen

  • Current rent per unit or for the house, as the lease states it, and the lease end date. Whether the rent is below market is the seller's opinion; label it that way or leave it out.
  • Who pays which utilities, as the leases say.
  • Expenses the seller documents for a stated period: taxes as billed, insurance as paid, management, maintenance, utilities paid by the owner, association fees if any. Say which of those are in the figures and which are not.
  • Vacancy and turnover history as the seller knows it.
  • Capital items with ages: roof, heating and cooling, water heater, electrical service, plumbing material, windows, appliances that convey.
  • Tenant status: leased to a date, month to month, or vacant. Point to the leases for everything else and tell the buyer to review them with their own advisor; ask your broker how to word anything about tenant rights, notice or rent rules.
  • Deferred maintenance the seller knows about, stated plainly. Investors price it either way; the honest version builds trust for the rest of the page.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in the details fields; the figures above in the text.

No projections, no return percentages, no phrases about upside. The figures are the pitch.

A lean, honest shot list

  1. Exterior from the street, both sides if it is on a corner, with the roof visible.
  2. The parking, the yard and the trash area, as they are.
  3. The mechanicals: furnace or boiler with its plate, water heater with its date, panel open, meter bank if there are several.
  4. The interior of a vacant unit or the house, every room, lights on, nothing staged.
  5. Occupied space only with the tenant's agreement and proper notice, the rooms they allow, their belongings respected. If they decline, say so on the page and describe the unit.
  6. Basement, attic, crawlspace if accessible, showing condition.
  7. The roof, from the ground or a drone still from a hired session if its age is a question.
  8. The street, one or two frames, without neighborhood claims you cannot support.

A short landscape listing video of those frames is enough; an investor watches it once to confirm the photos were not selective. Skip the vertical social video unless you have an investor audience on that channel, and keep occupied units out of it. No music; a plain recorded voiceover reading the key figures is more useful.

Worked example: a tenant-occupied 3-bed rental

A 3-bed, 1-bath single-family rental, occupied by a tenant on a lease ending in seven months at a stated rent, tenant pays electric and gas, owner pays water and trash. The seller supplies the lease, the past year's tax bill, the insurance declaration, twelve months of water and trash bills, and invoices showing a 2017 roof, a 2021 furnace and a 2016 water heater. The tenant agrees to photos of the living room, kitchen and exterior on a Saturday morning with a week's notice, bedrooms excluded. The agent shoots the exterior from the street, the detached garage, the back yard, the living room and kitchen as the tenant left them, the basement with the furnace plate, water heater date and panel, and the street. The page reads: leased through the stated month at the stated rent, tenant pays electric and gas, owner-paid water and trash for the past twelve months per bills, taxes per the current bill, insurance per the current declaration, roof 2017, furnace 2021, water heater 2016, bedrooms not photographed at the tenant's request, lease available to qualified buyers through the listing agent. Nine landscape frames make a forty-second listing video with a voiceover reading the figures. The exterior is the main photo.

What goes wrong when marketing to investors

  • Styling the listing like a family home. Twilight edits and staged living rooms signal that the seller is hiding the numbers behind the photos.
  • Computing the return. A cap rate on the page is a claim the buyer will test; give the inputs instead.
  • Market rent claims without a source. If the seller believes the rent is low, say the seller believes it.
  • Tidy edits on condition. Removing a stain, a crack or a broken fixture in an edit shows up at inspection. Compare against the original, or leave the frame honest.
  • Photographing tenants' belongings without agreement, or scheduling showings without the notice the lease and your broker call for.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should an investment property listing lead with?

Current rent and lease end date, who pays which utilities, documented expenses for a stated period, vacancy history and the ages of the roof, heating, water heater and electrical. Photos come after the figures.

Should I put a cap rate or return on the page?

No. Give the inputs, the rent and the documented expenses, and let the buyer compute their own return. A return figure is a claim the buyer will test and distrust.

How many photos does an investor listing need?

Fewer than a family home: exterior, parking and yard, mechanicals with their plates, every room of the vacant space, occupied rooms only with the tenant's agreement, and the roof. Condition shown, nothing staged.

Can I describe the tenant's lease terms and rights?

State the rent, the end date and who pays utilities as the lease says, and point qualified buyers to the lease itself. Ask your broker how to word anything about notice, tenant rights or rent rules.
