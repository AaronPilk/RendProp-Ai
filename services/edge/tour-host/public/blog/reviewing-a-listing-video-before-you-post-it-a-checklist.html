Reviewing a listing video before you post it: a checklist

Watch it muted, watch it with sound, then check the facts, the edits, the first three seconds and the last three. Ten minutes that catch what buyers see.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Watch the video twice, once muted and once with sound. Then check the facts against the listing, confirm every AI-edited photo carries its disclosure and matches the original, look hard at the first three seconds and the last three, read the captions for spelling, and confirm the ask and the link. Ten minutes, and it catches almost everything a buyer or a seller would notice.

Review a listing video in two watches and five checks. Watch it muted, then with sound. Then check the facts against the listing sheet, confirm every AI-edited photo carries its disclosure and matches the original, look hard at the first three seconds and the last three, read every caption for spelling, and make sure the ask is one action and the link goes where it should. It takes ten minutes and it catches the things a buyer, a seller or a compliance officer would catch for you later.

The two watches

Muted first. Phone in hand, sound off, as most of the audience will see it. Can you tell where the home is, what it is and what to do next from the captions and photos alone. Is there a frame where you wanted to scroll. Note the second, do not fix anything yet.

Then with sound. Does each sentence land on the photo it describes. Is the voice clearly on top of the music. Is there a hum, a click or a breath that a quiet room would have avoided. Does the pace feel like a person talking about a house they have been inside.

Check one: the facts

  • Beds and baths match the listing sheet. Half baths counted the way your MLS counts them.
  • The price or price point spoken or captioned matches the listing. If the price may change before the video is posted, leave it out of the video and keep it on the page.
  • Street, neighbourhood and town spelled and pronounced the local way.
  • Open house date and time, if included, are the confirmed ones.
  • Nothing is claimed that the listing does not support: no new roof unless there is a new roof.

Check two: the edits

Every AI-edited photo in the video is still an AI-edited photo. If the living room was virtually staged, the video is showing virtual staging and needs the same label the photo carries. If a sky was replaced or a lawn was greened, that disclosure travels too.

  • Open each edited frame beside its original. Confirm the edit changed only what you asked. AI can add or remove details, and a window that gained a view or a wall that lost an outlet is a problem.
  • Confirm the staged frames are labelled and that the label is visible wherever the video plays.
  • Check your own MLS rules on altered images before the video goes anywhere near the MLS link.

Check three: the first three seconds and the last three

The opening frame is the whole decision for a scrolling viewer. Is it the strongest photo. Does the first spoken or captioned line say something a buyer cares about. Is the caption sitting on the feature or beside it.

The closing frame is what the viewer remembers. Is it the yard, the view or the best room. Is the ask a single action: the open house, the link, a message. Does the branded property page link go where you expect when you tap it.

Check four: the captions

Read every word on screen, slowly. Street names, the neighbourhood, the word its versus it's. If you changed the script after recording, make sure the captions and the voice still say the same thing. One typo is the comment everyone reads.

Check five: the format

The 9:16 version is going to Reels, TikTok and Shorts. The 16:9 version is going to the property page and YouTube. Neither is going to the other place. The MLS gets the unbranded page link, not a file.

A worked example: the Tuesday review

A reel for a three-bed, two-bath with a virtually staged living room, open house Saturday. Muted watch: works, but the fourth frame is a hall bath and the eye drifts. With sound: clean, voice on top. Facts: listing sheet says two full baths, script says two and a half. Edits: staged living room is labelled, but the original shows a thermostat on the wall and the staged version does not; the edit is swapped for a different saved version that kept the wall as it was. First three seconds: kitchen island, good. Last three: back yard with the date, good. Captions: the street is spelled Larkin on the sign and Larken in the caption. Fixes: bath count, staged frame, spelling, hall bath removed. Twelve minutes. Posted.

What people skip and regret

  • The muted watch. If it only works with sound, half the audience gets a silent slideshow.
  • Comparing edits against originals. The one time an edit invents a window is the one time a buyer brings a printout to the showing.
  • Tapping the link. A link to the wrong listing or a page that is not yet published is an easy miss.
  • Reviewing on a laptop only. The reel will be watched on a phone. Review it on one.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should I check first in a listing video?

Watch it muted on a phone. Most of the audience will see it that way, and the muted watch shows whether the captions and photos carry the message on their own. Then watch it with sound to check the voice, the music level and the match between sentences and frames.

Do AI-edited photos in a video need a disclosure?

Yes. A virtually staged photo is still virtually staged when it is a frame in a video, and the label travels with it. Compare each edited frame against the original to confirm nothing was added or removed beyond what you asked, and check your MLS rules on altered images.

How long does a proper review take?

About ten minutes for a reel and a little more for a 90-second page video. Two watches, then facts, edits, opening and closing seconds, captions and format. Skipping it usually costs more than ten minutes later.

Should I include the price in the video?

Only if it will not change before the video stops being used. A price in a caption or voiceover is hard to update later. Keeping the price on the property page, where it can be edited, avoids a stale number in a reel that is still circulating.
