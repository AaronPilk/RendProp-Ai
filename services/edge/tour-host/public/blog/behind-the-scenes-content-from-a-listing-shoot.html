Behind the scenes content from a listing shoot

Behind-the-scenes posts work when they show the craft, not the chaos: the photographer at work, the prep, a labelled before and after. Ask the seller.

Behind-the-scenes content from a listing shoot earns its place when it shows the craft and not the chaos: the photographer setting up a frame, the seller's prep paying off, the room before and after a real edit with the edit named. It tells followers that your photos are made with care and that you are not hiding how. Ask the seller before you film anything, keep their belongings and their security out of frame, and post it after the listing is live rather than the morning of the shoot.

What behind the scenes is for

Buyers have learned to distrust listing photos. Showing how yours are made is a quiet way to answer that: a real photographer, a real tripod, a real room, and a before and after that says exactly what changed. For future sellers it does something else: it shows what a shoot day looks like with you, which is a question every seller has and few ask.

It also fills the gap between listing reels without being another listing reel, which matters if you are trying to post listings without wearing followers out.

A Thursday morning at a four-bed, five frames

The photographer arrives at nine at a four-bed colonial. Here is what to capture, on your own phone, in the gaps.

  1. The setup. The photographer's tripod in the living room doorway, the photographer at the camera, the room ahead. Shot from behind, so the frame they are composing is visible and their face is not the point. Caption line: Living room, nine fifteen, waiting for the sun to clear the neighbor's roof.
  2. The prep. The kitchen counter after the seller cleared it, with one bowl of lemons left. Caption line: The seller did this before the photographer arrived. It is the single most useful thing a seller can do.
  3. The wait. The photographer standing at a window checking the light, or the lens being wiped. Something that shows time being spent on a small thing.
  4. The test frame. The back of the camera or the phone showing the exterior as it came out, compared with the sky that was actually there. If the sky will be replaced in the final listing, say so here and say so again on the listing. Honest in both places.
  5. The pack-down. The gear bag by the door, the house back to normal, the seller's dog returned from the garage. Caption line: Forty frames, two hours, one dog. Listing goes live Tuesday.

Five frames, posted as a story set on the Tuesday the listing launches, or stitched into a short vertical video with your own voice over it. Not the morning of the shoot, when the listing is not live and the seller has not seen the photos.

The before and after, done properly

A before and after is the most useful behind-the-scenes post and the easiest to get wrong. Done well, it shows the original frame next to the finished one with the edit named in the caption: Sky replaced, nothing else changed. Or: Living room virtually staged; the room is empty in person. Done badly, it shows the finished photo alone with the word after, and the viewer is left to guess what the before looked like and whether the room is real.

Keep the original. The comparison is only honest if the untouched frame is the one on the left. The disclosure line is the same one that goes in any caption with an edited photo.

What should stay out of frame

  • The seller's belongings in detail. Family photos, mail on the counter, a child's name on a bedroom door, medication on a nightstand. Pan past or do not shoot.
  • Security. Alarm panels, the key box code, the garage keypad, a door propped open. A behind-the-scenes clip is a map of the home for anyone who wants one.
  • The mess. A behind-the-scenes post about how messy the house was before you fixed it is a post about the seller's housekeeping, and the seller will see it.
  • The photographer's face, if they did not agree. Ask. Most will say yes and appreciate the credit.
  • Anything before the seller has said yes. Some sellers do not want their home's preparation on anyone's feed. Ask before the shoot, in writing, and respect a no.

Mistakes that undo the point

  • Posting the morning of the shoot. The listing is not live, the photos are not reviewed, and the seller sees their kitchen on your story before they see the finished photos.
  • A before and after with no label. It reads as a trick instead of a disclosure.
  • Making it about you. The photographer and the seller did the work in these frames. Credit them.
  • Showing the address. The street number on the door in a story posted before launch is an invitation to visit a home that is not ready.

One line to the seller

Before your next shoot, send the seller one line: Would you be comfortable with a few behind-the-scenes frames from the shoot going on my social accounts after the listing is live, with nothing personal in them. Their answer decides whether the five frames get shot.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I post behind-the-scenes content on the day of the shoot?

No. The listing is not live and the seller has not seen the finished photos. Capture the frames on the day and post them on launch day or after.

Do I need the seller's permission for behind-the-scenes posts?

Yes, in writing, before the shoot. Some sellers are happy; some do not want their home's preparation shown. If they say no, shoot nothing for social that day.

How do I post a before and after of an AI edit honestly?

Show the untouched original next to the edited version and name the edit in the caption: sky replaced, lawn edited, living room virtually staged. Never post the after alone with no label.

What should never appear in a behind-the-scenes clip?

The seller's personal items, children's names, mail, medication, alarm panels, key box codes, and the house number before launch. Keep the frames about the craft, not the household.
