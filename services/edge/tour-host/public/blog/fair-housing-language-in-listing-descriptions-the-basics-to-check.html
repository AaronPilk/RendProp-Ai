Fair housing language in listing copy: the basics to check

Not legal advice. The care point: describe the property, not the people you imagine in it. A reading pass for phrases to question, and where to get help.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. This is not legal advice. The general care point is to describe the property, not the people you imagine living in it or near it. Before publishing, reread the remarks, the story section, the voiceover script and the captions for any phrase about who the home is perfect for, any reference to nearby religious or community institutions as a selling point, and any word about ability, family status or background. This post is not legal advice, and it does not tell you what the law requires; your broker, your local association and your MLS's compliance desk are where that guidance comes from. What it offers is the general care point that experienced agents work from: describe the property, not the people you imagine living in it or near it. Before publishing, reread every piece of listing text for phrases about who the home suits, references to nearby religious or community institutions as selling points, and any word about ability, family status or background. Where a phrase feels borderline, ask before you publish.

The reason to treat this as a reading pass rather than a word list is that the problem is rarely a single forbidden word. It is a habit of thought: writing about the buyer instead of the house. “Perfect for a growing family” is not a problem because of any one word in it. It is a problem because it describes a household rather than a bedroom count. Fix the habit and the word list mostly takes care of itself.

The one principle

Every sentence in a listing can be sorted into one of two piles: sentences about the property and sentences about people. Square feet, the year of the roof, the exposure of the windows, the distance on foot to the park, the number of steps at the front door: property. Who would love it, who lives nearby, what kind of household it suits, how it feels to a particular group: people. Keep the first pile. Move the second pile out, and replace each sentence with the property fact that was behind it.

The reading pass, with swaps

Read the text once, slowly, looking only for people. For each phrase you find, write the property fact instead. These are illustrations, not a complete list, and not a ruling on any phrase.

  • “Perfect for a young family” → “Three bedrooms on one floor and a fenced back yard.”
  • “Ideal for empty nesters” → “Single-level living with the primary bedroom and laundry on the main floor.”
  • “Great starter home for a young couple” → “A two-bedroom with a 2020 roof and a furnace from 2021.”
  • “Walking distance to St. Anne's and the community centre” → “Six minutes on foot to Main Street and the park.” Naming religious or community institutions as a selling point is a phrase to check with your broker; describing general amenities and distances is the property fact.
  • “Family-friendly, safe neighbourhood” → “A dead-end street with no through traffic.” Safety is a judgment you cannot verify; the street layout is a fact.
  • “Not suitable for wheelchairs” or “no stairs to worry about” → “Four steps to the front entry” or “a level entry from the drive and a single-level plan.” Describe the physical features and let the reader judge suitability.
  • “Exclusive enclave” → “A private road of twelve homes.”
  • “Master bedroom” → many agents now write “primary bedroom”; check your MLS's current rules and your brokerage's style.
  • “Quiet neighbours” or any description of who lives nearby → remove. Describe the lot, the fence and the setback.

It is not just the remarks

The reading pass has to cover every piece of text the listing produces, because the habit shows up everywhere. The MLS remarks, yes, but also the story section on the property page, the social captions, the email to your database, the printed flyer, and the voiceover script for the listing video, where a phrase like “imagine your kids in this yard” slips in easily because it sounds friendly out loud. On-screen captions that follow the voiceover word by word put every one of those words on the screen. Review the script with the same eye as the remarks.

Photos and staging deserve a look too. A virtually staged nursery or a prayer room makes a statement about the household the home is for without a single word. The post on AI photo edits and fair housing covers that side.

One paragraph through the pass

Before: “A wonderful family home in a safe, exclusive neighbourhood, perfect for young professionals or a growing family. Walking distance to St. Anne's and the best schools in the district. No stairs to worry about.”

After: “A 1998 four-bedroom on a private road of twelve homes, with a dead-end street and no through traffic. Level entry from the drive and a single-level plan. Six minutes on foot to Main Street and the park. The assigned schools are listed in the details; confirm current boundaries with the district.”

The second version says more about the house and nothing about the people.

Where to get real guidance

  • Your broker, before publishing, for any phrase you are unsure about. That is what the broker relationship is for.
  • Your local association, which usually publishes advertising guidance and runs training.
  • Your MLS's compliance desk, for its current rules on remarks, fields and photos.
  • An attorney, for anything that has gone beyond a wording question.

The habit to build is asking early. A text to your broker with the phrase in question takes a minute; the alternative conversations take much longer.

Where agents get caught out

  • Pasting the seller's words. Sellers describe who they hope buys the house. Thank them and translate to property facts.
  • Reviewing only the MLS remarks. The caption, the script and the flyer carry the same risk.
  • Treating a word list as the whole job. New phrases appear; the principle is what catches them.
  • Writing about the neighbours. Any description of who lives nearby describes people.
  • Assuming friendly means fine. The warmest phrases are often the ones about people.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Is there a list of words that are banned in listing descriptions?

Lists exist from associations and MLSs, and they are worth reading, but no list is complete and this post is not one. The more reliable habit is a principle: describe the property, not the people. For any specific phrase, ask your broker and check your MLS's current guidance. This is not legal advice.

Can I mention schools in a listing?

Many agents name the district and the assigned schools in the fields the MLS provides and tell buyers to confirm boundaries with the district, while avoiding ratings and phrases like best schools. Check your MLS's current rules on school information and ask your broker about wording.

Does fair housing review apply to video scripts and captions too?

The same care point applies to every piece of text the listing produces: remarks, page, captions, emails, flyers and the voiceover script. Friendly spoken phrases about who would love the home slip into scripts easily. Review the script with the same eye, and remember that word-by-word captions put every word on screen.

What should I do if I am unsure about a phrase?

Ask your broker before publishing, with the exact phrase and the context. Your local association and your MLS's compliance desk are the other sources for current guidance. Asking early takes a minute; the alternative conversations take far longer. This post is not legal advice.
