The hook: first lines that keep a listing reel playing

A hook is one specific fact a buyer cares about, in under ten words, landing on the best frame. Twelve first lines that work, six that fail, and why.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

A hook is the first spoken and captioned line of a listing reel, and it works when it delivers one specific fact a buyer cares about in under ten words while the strongest photo is on screen. The fact is usually a price, a place, a feature or a contrast between two of those. It fails when it is about you, when it is an adjective, when it is a question nobody was asking, or when it arrives after four seconds of music. The hook does not have to be clever. It has to be specific.

What a hook is actually doing

Someone is scrolling. Your reel appears. In the time it takes their thumb to move again, the first line has to give them a reason to let it stay. A reason is a fact that matches something they want: a budget, a neighbourhood, a yard, a view, a commute. Give them that fact and they stay for the second line. Give them a greeting and they are gone.

The hook also sets the frame. If the line says kitchen, the kitchen is on screen. If it says the view, the view. Line and frame arrive together or the hook is wasted.

Twelve first lines that work

  • Three bedrooms under $450,000, a twelve-minute drive to downtown. Price and place.
  • This is the kitchen in a $325,000 townhouse. Contrast between frame and price.
  • Two beds, a block from the river path, and this is the view. Place and feature.
  • The back yard is bigger than the house. One surprising fact.
  • Corner lot on the quietest street in Oak Hill. Place and a quality a buyer cannot see.
  • Renovated last year, listed this week. Timing.
  • You can walk to three schools from this front door. Specific to a buyer type.
  • First floor main bedroom, no stairs, half an acre. Three facts for a buyer who wants exactly that.
  • Thirty acres, one neighbour, forty minutes from the airport. For the estate, plain and slow.
  • Under $300,000 and the mortgage is close to the rent next door. Contrast for the first-time buyer. Check the numbers before you say them.
  • Open Saturday, one to three, the one with the blue door. For the open house cut.
  • Sold in nine days on Weller Street. For the sold video, aimed at the neighbour.

Every one is a fact. None has an adjective doing the work.

Six first lines that fail

  • Hi everyone, it's me with another listing. About you, not the home. Scrolled.
  • Welcome to this gorgeous home. An adjective with no fact behind it, and a word that makes buyers distrust the rest.
  • Are you looking for your dream home. A question nobody was asking, answered by scrolling.
  • Let me show you around. Slow, and it promises a tour instead of a reason.
  • 1,850 square feet, built 1994, lot 0.21 acres. Facts, but the wrong ones. Numbers from the listing sheet are for the caption and the page.
  • Four seconds of music, then any line at all. The viewer left during the music.

Matching the line to the frame

Pick the strongest photo first, then write the line about it. If the best frame is the kitchen island, the line mentions the kitchen or the price. If it is the view from the balcony, the line is the view and the train. The two have to agree. A great line over the wrong photo is a reel that opens on a contradiction.

There is one exception: the contrast hook, where the frame is the surprise. This is the kitchen in a $325,000 townhouse works because the kitchen looks like more. The line and frame still agree; they just agree on the surprise.

A worked example: three hooks for one listing

A three-bed, two-bath on a corner lot, renovated kitchen, fenced yard, $462,000, open house Sunday.

The just-listed reel opens on the kitchen: Three bedrooms under $475,000, and the kitchen was redone last year. The open house cut opens on the front: Open Sunday, one to three, the corner lot with the red maple. The sold video opens on the exterior at dusk: Sold in nine days on Weller Street. Same home, three audiences, three hooks, each a fact.

Writing the hook last

Write the rest of the script first: the three things a buyer cannot see, the ask. Then look at what you have and pull the single strongest fact to the front. Hooks written first tend to be slogans. Hooks pulled from a finished script tend to be facts. The script method is in writing a 30-second voiceover script, and the three-second test is in what a listing reel should do in three seconds.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long should the hook on a listing reel be?

Under ten words, spoken and captioned, landing while the first photo is on screen. One fact: price, place, a feature or a contrast between two of them. If the line needs a second sentence to make sense, it is not the hook.

Should the hook mention the price?

Often, yes. Price is the fact most viewers want first, and a price or a bracket keeps the right buyers and lets the wrong ones scroll. If the price might change while the reel is still circulating, use a bracket and keep the exact figure on the property page.

Is it a mistake to start with a greeting?

Yes. A greeting is about you, and the viewer has not decided to care about you yet. Lead with the home and a fact. Your name and contact card belong on the property page and, if you want, at the end of the reel.

Should I write the hook before the rest of the script?

Write it last. Draft the three things a buyer cannot see and the ask first, then pull the single strongest fact to the front. Hooks written first tend to come out as slogans; hooks pulled from a finished script tend to be specific.
