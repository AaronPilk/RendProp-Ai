Words to avoid in listing copy

Avoid words a buyer cannot check: charming, cozy, must see, nestled, boasts, gem. Replace each with the fact behind it; describe the home, not the buyer.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Avoid three families of words: filler adjectives a buyer cannot check (charming, cozy, gorgeous, spacious), manufactured urgency (must see, won't last, rare opportunity) and anything that describes the buyer instead of the home (perfect for families, ideal for professionals). Avoid three kinds of words in listing copy: filler adjectives a buyer cannot check, manufactured urgency, and anything that describes the buyer instead of the home. “Charming” becomes “built in 1926 with the original oak floors and a brick fireplace”. “Won't last” comes out. “Perfect for a young family” becomes “three bedrooms on one floor and a fenced yard”. In every case the replacement is the fact that made you reach for the word in the first place. The third family is also where fair-housing problems start; this is not legal advice, and when a phrase feels borderline, ask your broker.

These words do not just fail to help. They cost you. Buyers have read “cozy” on enough small rooms and “charming” on enough old kitchens to decode both on sight, and once they have decoded one word they read the rest of the description as code too. A description with no code words gets read as a plain account of the house, which is the only thing a description can be that a buyer still trusts.

Family one: adjectives that carry no information

The test is simple. Could a buyer standing in the room confirm or deny it. “Oak floors” passes. “Beautiful floors” does not. Here are the usual suspects, with what to write instead.

  • Charming → the year, the material, the original feature: “1926, original oak floors, brick fireplace”.
  • Cozy → the dimensions and what fits: “the second bedroom is 9 by 10 and fits a double bed and a desk”.
  • Spacious → the square feet or the room size: “the living room runs 14 by 24”.
  • Gorgeous, beautiful, lovely → delete, and let the photo and the noun do it.
  • Boasts, features, offers → has, is, gets. “The kitchen has a gas range” not “the kitchen boasts”.
  • Nestled → where it actually sits: “at the end of a dead-end street, backing onto the creek”.
  • Gem, oasis, retreat, sanctuary → the thing that made you think it: “a fenced quarter-acre with no rear neighbour”.
  • Immaculate, meticulously maintained → the maintenance record: “roof 2021, furnace 2019, serviced each spring”.
  • Updated, renovated on their own → what and when: “kitchen and both baths, 2022”.
  • Entertainer's delight → the deck size and the layout: “a 16 by 20 deck off the kitchen”.
  • Sun-drenched, light-filled → the exposure: “south-facing windows across the back”.

Family two: urgency the buyer did not ask for

“Must see”, “won't last”, “rare opportunity”, “priced to sell”, “motivated seller”, “bring all offers”. None of these describe the house. The first four have been used on so many ordinary homes that buyers skip them. The last two give away the seller's position to every buyer's agent who reads the listing, which is not yours to give without the seller's agreement. If the home is unusual, say what is unusual about it, and the reader will supply the urgency.

Family three: words about people instead of the property

This is the family that matters most, and the one to be strictest with. Any phrase that describes who should buy or live in the home, who lives nearby, or what kind of household the home suits is describing people. “Perfect for a growing family”, “ideal for a young professional”, “great for empty nesters”, “bachelor pad”, “family-friendly neighbourhood”, “exclusive”, “safe”, and references to nearby places of worship or community institutions as selling points all fall here. Some are merely vague; some can create fair-housing problems. The fix is the same for all of them: describe the property. Three bedrooms on one floor, a fenced yard, a single-level layout with no steps at the entry, six minutes on foot to the park. The buyer decides who it suits. This is not legal advice; the post on fair housing language in listing descriptions covers a reading pass, and your broker and local association are where real guidance comes from.

Words that are fine when they are true

Not every short word is a problem. “Quiet” is fine when it describes a physical fact: a dead-end street, no through traffic. “Large” is fine next to a measure. “New” is fine next to a year. “Move-in ready” is fine if the systems are recent and nothing needs doing before the buyer sleeps there, and dangerous if it is a hope. The rule is not to ban adjectives; it is to make every one of them answerable.

One description with the words taken out

Before: “Charming and cozy 3-bedroom gem nestled in a sought-after, family-friendly neighbourhood. Boasts a spacious, light-filled living room and an entertainer's back yard. Meticulously maintained. Must see, won't last.”

After: “A 1926 three-bedroom with its original oak floors and a brick fireplace, at the end of a dead-end street. The living room runs 14 by 24 with south-facing windows. The fenced back yard holds a 16 by 20 deck off the kitchen. Roof 2021, furnace 2019. Six minutes on foot to the park and the elementary school.”

Every sentence in the second version can be checked at the showing, and none of it describes the buyer.

What goes wrong when you cut the words

The common failure is to delete the adjective and leave nothing. “A 3-bedroom home in a neighbourhood with a living room and a back yard” is worse than the original. The adjective was pointing at something; find the something and write it down. If you cannot, the sentence had nothing in it, and it should go too.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Why avoid words like charming and cozy in a listing?

Buyers have learned to translate them: cozy means small, charming means old. Once they decode one word they read the whole description as code. Replacing each with the fact behind it, such as the year, the size or the original feature, gives the buyer something to check and keeps the rest of the description credible.

Is it wrong to say a home is perfect for families?

It describes people rather than the property, which at best is vague and at worst creates fair-housing problems. Describe what made you think it: three bedrooms on one floor, a fenced yard, a park six minutes away. This is not legal advice; ask your broker about specific phrases.

Can I use any adjectives at all in listing copy?

Yes, when each one is answerable at the showing. Quiet next to a dead-end street, large next to a measure, new next to a year. The goal is not to ban adjectives but to make sure every one points at something a buyer can confirm.

What should replace must see and won't last?

Nothing, usually. If the home has something genuinely unusual, say what it is and the reader will supply the urgency. Phrases like motivated seller and priced to sell also reveal the seller's position to every buyer's agent, which is not yours to do without the seller's agreement.
