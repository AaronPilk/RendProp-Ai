Marketing a listing in a tech hub

Tech-hub buyers research everything and expect a complete page. Give them the office, the provider, the commute and the facts, stated plainly.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. In a tech hub the buyer researches everything before they contact anyone, shops on a phone at odd hours, and treats an incomplete page as a reason to move on. Many are relocating on a start date and many work from home at least part of the week, so the home office, the internet provider, the commute to a named campus or transit line and the practical facts (EV charging, systems, association) belong on the page in that order, stated plainly and without pitch.

In a tech hub the buyer has done the research before they contact anyone. They have read the listing, the neighbourhood threads, the permit history and the flood map, usually on a phone, usually late at night, and they treat an incomplete page as a reason to move on rather than a reason to ask. Many are relocating on a start date; many work from home part of the week; most expect the page to answer questions that other markets leave to the showing. Market the listing by giving them the home office, the internet provider, the commute to a named campus or transit line and the practical facts, in that order, stated plainly and without a pitch they will see through.

A buyer who reads everything

This buyer will find out whatever you leave off the page, and they will hold the omission against you more than the fact. If the house is near a flight path, they will have checked; say it. If the roof is twenty years old, the permit record says so; say it. If the internet is one provider only, they will look it up; name the provider and let them check the plans themselves. The service you provide is not persuasion; it is saving them the three browser tabs by putting the answers in one place, accurately.

They also shop in bulk and compare on specifics. A page with the square footage of the office, the number of dedicated circuits in the garage and the distance to the light rail stop in minutes wins a comparison against a page with a paragraph about natural light. They value their time, they are used to products that work, and a listing page that behaves like one earns a kind of trust that the copy cannot.

A buyer with a start date in five weeks finds it at midnight, reads the whole page, checks the provider and the rail timetable in two other tabs, and fills in the enquiry form for the weekend she flies in. She watches the walk-to-the-platform video on the flight. At the showing she measures nothing, asks two questions the page could not answer (noise from the neighbours, how the roof deck drains), and offers that evening. The page had already done the first showing and most of the second.

The home office and the connection

  • Shoot the office as an office. A desk, a chair, the window, the door closed. If it is a bedroom that could be an office, say so and give the dimensions; do not stage it as something it is not.
  • Name the internet provider. State who serves the address and leave the speeds and plans to the buyer to check. Do not promise performance.
  • Show the second office if there is one. Two people working from home is common; a second quiet room with a door is worth a frame.
  • State the charging situation. A dedicated circuit in the garage, or none, is a fact the buyer wants on the page.
  • Give the commute both ways. By transit and by car, at a named hour, to named destinations. Minutes, not miles.

What goes wrong with listings in a tech hub

  • Pitch instead of facts. Smart home, modern living, tech-forward. This buyer writes software for a living and reads marketing copy as noise. State what is installed.
  • Leaving out what they will find anyway. The flight path, the roof age, the single provider. The omission costs more than the fact.
  • A video that is all effects. Fast cuts, heavy motion, music over everything. The buyer wants to see the rooms. Calm camera, walking order, captions.
  • Edits that do not survive scrutiny. This buyer zooms in. A tidy-the-room edit that removed an outlet or a vent, a staged office in what is really a closet, a greener lawn than the one on the street view. Compare every edit with its original, keep the disclosure, and expect the comparison to be made.
  • Slow, phone-first replies. They filled in a form at 1am and want a written answer with a time, not a voicemail.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What do tech-hub buyers want on a listing page?

The home office shown as an office with its dimensions, the internet provider named, and the charging situation in the garage. The commute by transit and by car to named destinations at a named hour. The ages of the roof and systems, the association fee and what it covers, and anything they would find in the permit record anyway.

Should I describe the home as a smart home or tech-forward?

No. State what is installed, by name, and let the buyer judge. This buyer reads marketing adjectives as noise and specifics as credibility.

Can I promise internet speeds on the listing?

Name the provider or providers that serve the address and leave the plans and speeds for the buyer to check. Promising performance you do not control is a claim you cannot back.

Why do AI photo edits carry more risk with this buyer?

Because they zoom in, compare the photos with street view and the permit record, and visit once. An edit that removed an outlet, staged a closet as an office or greened the lawn gets found. Compare each edit with the original and keep the disclosure on every version.
