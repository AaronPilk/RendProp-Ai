Relaunching a listing after a failed contract

Back on market means buyers assume a problem. Answer that first, refresh only what is stale, announce it plainly, and go back to the people who missed out.

Answer the question before it is asked

Back on market is a phrase buyers read as a warning. The ones who were interested before want to know what went wrong; the ones seeing it for the first time see the days on market and the status change and assume the worst.

The honest reasons are usually mundane: the buyer's financing fell through, the buyer's own sale collapsed, the buyer got cold feet, or the inspection turned up something the seller has since fixed. Whichever it is, ask your broker what you can say and how, then say it in the remarks and in the first sentence of every reply. If the inspection did find something, say what it was and what was done about it, and have the receipt or the report ready.

What to refresh and what to keep

The temptation is to treat the relaunch as a brand-new listing with new everything. Usually that wastes money and signals panic. Go through the media with one question: is this still true?

  • Keep photos that still show the house as it is. If the contract lasted a month and the season has not changed, the gallery is fine.
  • Reshoot the exterior if the season has turned. A listing that went under contract with leaves on the trees and comes back with bare branches needs a new front exterior and a new main photo, or it looks like it has been sitting since autumn.
  • Replace any photo of something that changed during the contract: a repair, a room repainted, furniture removed if the sellers started packing.
  • Re-cut the video if you replaced the main exterior, so the opening scene matches the gallery. If nothing changed, the same video is still the right video.

If the page itself has gone stale or the listing was taken down entirely, the mechanics of bringing it back are in expired listing, new link: relaunching a property page.

The announcement: plain and short

Do not run a just-listed campaign for a house that was listed six weeks ago. The people on your list remember it. One line does the job: back on the market through no fault of the house, buyer's financing fell through, here is the link, showings this weekend. The same line goes in the email, the social post and the text to the agents who showed it.

Order matters. First the buyers who made offers or came to second showings, by phone, before anything is posted. Then the agents who brought buyers. Then the email list. Then the social post. The buyers who already wanted the house should hear it from you, not from the status change, and a day's head start is a courtesy they remember. The social version of the three-stage story is in just listed, under contract, sold: three videos from one photo set; a relaunch is a fourth, shorter one.

The colonial back on market on a Friday

A four-bed colonial, under contract for just over three weeks in October, buyer's mortgage declined in the final week. The sellers heard on a Wednesday afternoon. The agent's first call, within the hour, was to her broker to confirm what she could say; the answer was that the financing reason could be stated plainly. Her second and third calls were to the two buyers who had made backup-level offers in the first round. One asked for a showing Saturday.

Thursday she walked the house. The maples out front had dropped in the three weeks, so the main photo, taken in full color, now looked dated. The photographer came Thursday at four for a new front exterior and stayed for a twilight. Inside, nothing had changed; the gallery stayed. She swapped the main photo for the twilight, re-cut the video so it opened on the new exterior, and read the page once on her phone: price back to list, details unchanged, form tested.

Friday morning the listing went back to active with one line in the remarks about the financing, and the same line went to the four agents who had shown it, then the email list, then one social post with the twilight. Saturday had three showings: the backup buyer from Wednesday's call, one agent's client from the first round, and one new. The backup buyer's offer came Sunday evening.

Relaunch mistakes

Saying nothing. Buyers fill the silence with an inspection horror story.

A full just-listed campaign. Everyone on your list knows it is not new. It reads as either confusion or desperation.

Reshooting everything. Money spent to replace photos that were still true.

Leaving the autumn main photo on a bare-tree street. The one photo that most needs replacing is the one agents most often leave.

Cutting the price in the same breath. A failed contract is not evidence the price was wrong; the first buyer agreed to it. Relaunch at the list price and decide about price separately, after the first weekend. If a reduction is coming anyway, plan it properly: see marketing a listing with an upcoming price reduction.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I say why the contract fell through?

Yes, within what your broker tells you can be shared. Buyers assume the inspection found something unless told otherwise. State the reason plainly in the remarks and in the first sentence of every reply, and if it was an inspection issue, say what was found and what was done about it, with the receipt ready.

Do I need new photos when a listing comes back on market?

Only where the old ones are no longer true. If the season has turned, reshoot the front exterior and swap the main photo, and re-cut the video to match. Interiors that have not changed stay. Reshooting everything wastes money and signals panic.

Who should I tell first when a listing is back on market?

The buyers who made offers or came to second showings, by phone, before anything is posted. Then the agents who brought buyers, then your email list, then social. The second buyer for a house is often someone who already wanted it.

Should I reduce the price when I relaunch?

Not automatically. A failed contract is not evidence the price was wrong, since the first buyer agreed to it. Relaunch at the list price, give it a weekend, and treat any reduction as a separate decision with its own plan.
