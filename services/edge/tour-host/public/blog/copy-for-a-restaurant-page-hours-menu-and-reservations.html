Copy for a restaurant page: hours, menu and reservations

Restaurant page copy is current hours, a menu as text with prices, and one clear way to reserve or a plain note that you do not take them. Then the room.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Restaurant page copy is three things done plainly: hours that are current and readable at a glance, a menu as text with prices rather than a photographed PDF, and one clear way to reserve, or a plain sentence saying you do not take reservations. After those, two sentences on what the room is like and who it suits on a given night. A person deciding where to eat at 6:40 on a Thursday reads nothing else.

Most restaurant pages get the order backwards. A paragraph about the chef's philosophy, a line about locally sourced ingredients, and then, somewhere below, hours that were right last spring. The reader wants to know if you are open, what it costs, and whether they can get a table. Answer those and the philosophy paragraph becomes a pleasant extra rather than an obstacle.

Hours that read in one glance

Write hours as a short list by day, not a sentence. Split kitchen and bar if they differ, because “open until 11” means something different to someone who wants dinner at 10. Say what happens on holidays, or that the page is updated when they change. Date the hours if they are seasonal.

  • Tuesday to Thursday: kitchen 5 to 10, bar until 11.
  • Friday and Saturday: kitchen 5 to 11, bar until midnight.
  • Sunday: brunch 10 to 2, dinner 5 to 9.
  • Closed Monday.

“Hours may vary” is not hours. If they vary, say by what: “Kitchen closes earlier on quiet nights; call after 9.”

The menu as text, with prices

A photographed PDF cannot be read on a phone, searched, or found by someone looking for the dish you are known for. Type the menu. Put the dishes you are known for first in each section, give the price, and add the dietary notes that get asked about: vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free options, nuts. Keep descriptions to the ingredients and the method: “Roast chicken, half, with potatoes and salsa verde, $28.” Adjectives on every dish make the whole menu read the same. Date the menu or say how often it changes, and take the stale specials down the day they end. The post on hours, menu and capacity on a page that is not a PDF goes into the format.

Reservations: yes, no, or how

Pick one of three sentences and put it near the hours.

  • “Reservations through the link below; walk-ins welcome at the bar.”
  • “No reservations; the wait on Friday and Saturday is usually 30 to 45 minutes after 7.”
  • “Tables of six or more by reservation; smaller parties walk in.”

Say how large parties and private dining work and who to ask. “Private dining for up to 24 in the back room; ask about a date on the form below.” A page that says “reservations recommended” with no way to make one has sent the reader to a search engine.

The room, in two sentences

After the practical block, say what it is like to be there and when it suits whom. “The front room is loud and bright with ten bar seats and the open kitchen; the back room is quieter and takes groups. Kids are welcome before 7.” That is enough. The gallery and the video show the rest; the post on restaurant marketing with a walkthrough covers what to show.

A restaurant page, rewritten

Before: “A culinary journey awaits at our neighbourhood gem, where farm-fresh ingredients meet passionate cooking. Open for dinner most evenings. Reservations recommended. See our menu (PDF).”

After: “Tuesday to Thursday, kitchen 5 to 10 and bar until 11; Friday and Saturday until 11 and midnight; Sunday brunch 10 to 2. Closed Monday. Reservations through the link below; walk-ins at the bar; parties of six or more by reservation. Menu below with prices, updated for autumn; the roast chicken and the clam toast are the dishes people come back for. The front room is loud and bright; the back room is quieter and takes groups.”

Where restaurant copy goes stale or vague

  • Hours from last season. The most common and the most damaging; it produces a locked door and a one-star review.
  • The PDF menu. Unreadable on a phone and invisible to search.
  • “Reservations recommended” with no link.
  • Adjectives on every dish. “Succulent”, “decadent”, “mouthwatering”; the ingredients say more.
  • Specials from three weeks ago. Take them down the day they end.
  • No word on groups, kids or accessibility. The three questions that arrive by phone during service.
  • Edited photos without a note. If a room photo has been altered, say so as you would anywhere else.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a restaurant publish menu prices online?

Yes. A reader deciding between two places at 6:40 wants the number, and a menu without prices reads as expensive. Type the menu as text with prices, put the dishes you are known for first, and date it or say how often it changes.

What if the restaurant does not take reservations?

Say so in one plain sentence near the hours, and give the reader something useful in its place: the typical wait on busy nights, whether the bar is first come, how large parties are handled. No reservations is a policy; silence about reservations is a gap.

How often should the hours on the page be updated?

Every time they change, the same day. Stale hours are the most common and most damaging error on a restaurant page because they produce a locked door. If hours are seasonal, date them; if the kitchen closes early on quiet nights, say so.

How much should the page say about the food philosophy?

A sentence or two after the practical block, if it is specific: where the fish comes from on Fridays, that the bread is baked at 6 each morning. General phrases about passion and freshness appear on every restaurant page and tell the reader nothing.
