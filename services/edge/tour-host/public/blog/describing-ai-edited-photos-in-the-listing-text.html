Describing AI-edited photos in the listing text

One plain line near the photos: which were changed, how, and that originals are available. Then describe the home as it is, not as the edit shows it.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. When the photos include AI edits, say so in the text in one plain line near the first mention of the photos: which photos were changed, what was changed, and that the originals are available. Then describe the home as it is, not as the edited photo shows it. A staged living room is empty; a twilight sky is a photo choice, not evening light the buyer will find.

When a listing's photos include AI edits, say so in the text in one plain line, placed where the photos are first mentioned or at the top of the remarks: which photos were changed, what was changed, and that the originals are available. “Photos 3 and 7 are virtually staged; the sky in photo 1 was edited. Original photos available on request.” Then write the rest of the description about the home as it is, not as the edit shows it.

The disclosure and the description are two separate jobs, and the second is the one most people miss. Agents who remember to add a virtual staging note will still write “beautifully furnished living room” two sentences later, because the staged photo is the one they were looking at. The text has to describe the empty room, because that is what the buyer will walk into.

The one line, and where it goes

The line has three parts: which photos, what kind of edit, and where the real ones are. Keep it to one or two sentences and use the same wording everywhere the photos appear: the MLS remarks, the property page, the social caption, the printed flyer. Check your MLS's current rules on where disclosures must sit and whether specific wording is required, and ask your broker about office policy and whether the note belongs on the photo itself as well as in the text.

Placement matters. A disclosure at the end of 900 characters of remarks, after “call today”, is technically present and practically invisible. Put it where the buyer's eye is when they are looking at the photos: the first or second sentence of the remarks, or the top of the story section on the page.

What to write for each kind of edit

  • Virtual staging. “Photos 3, 4 and 7 are virtually staged. The home is vacant and the furniture shown is not included.” Then describe the room by its size, its windows and its floor, never by the furniture.
  • Twilight edit. “Photo 1 is a twilight edit of a daytime photo.” Do not write “evening light pours across the front” as if it were a feature. Write about the west-facing front and let the buyer visit at dusk.
  • Blue sky replacement. “The sky in the exterior photos was edited.” The house, the roof and the yard are unchanged, and the description can say so.
  • Green lawn edit. “The lawn in photo 2 was edited; it was dormant when photographed.” Then say what the yard is: fenced, a quarter-acre, south-facing.
  • Decluttering. “Personal items were removed from photos 5 and 6.” Describe the room, not its tidiness.
  • Added furniture to a furnished room, or any custom edit: name what was added or changed. If an edit changed something structural by mistake, do not publish that version at all.

Describing the home, not the edit

A simple test: for every sentence, ask whether a buyer standing in the room on a grey Tuesday would agree with it. “The living room runs 14 by 22 with windows on two sides and oak floors” passes in an empty room and in a staged photo. “A warm, beautifully furnished living space” passes only in the photo. Write the sentences that pass in the room.

The same goes for the voiceover and the captions on any video built from those photos. If a staged photo is in the reel, the script should not say “imagine coming home to this”, and the caption on the post should carry the same one-line note.

The same room, two descriptions

Before: “Step into this beautifully furnished, sun-drenched living room with evening light pouring across the manicured front lawn. Move-in ready. (Some photos virtually staged.)”

After: “Photos 1 and 4 are virtually staged and photo 1's sky was edited; the home is vacant and originals are available on request. The living room is 14 by 22 with windows on the south and west walls and oak floors. The front faces west over a quarter-acre lot.”

The second version tells the buyer what was done, then tells them what is there.

Where the text still misleads

  • The buried note. At the end of the remarks, in parentheses, after the call to action.
  • “Some photos” with no numbers. The buyer cannot tell which room is real.
  • “Professionally photographed” as the disclosure. It is not one.
  • The note in the text but not on the photo, or on the photo but not in the text. Both places, same wording. The post on what to disclose and where covers the photo side.
  • Describing furniture that is not there. The most common one, and the one that produces the awkward silence at the showing.
  • Different notes on different channels. One line, written once, pasted everywhere.

How this works in Rendprop

In Rendprop's photo studio, every AI edit is saved as a separate version with a disclosure: staging is labelled “Virtually staged”, and other edits carry an AI-alteration disclosure. The untouched original stays in the listing's photo history, which is what makes “originals available on request” true. AI can change or invent details, so compare each edited version against the original before you choose which to publish. The property page shows the details you typed, the story section and your contact card; the one-line note in the story section is yours to write, since Rendprop does not write listing text, and it should match the labels on the photos.

Write the line before the description

Make the list of which photos are edited and how, write the one line from it, and put it at the top of the remarks and the story section. Then write the description with that list beside you, so every room gets described as it is.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Where in the listing text should an AI edit disclosure go?

Near the top, where the buyer's eye is while they look at the photos: the first or second sentence of the remarks or the top of the story section on the property page. A note at the end after the call to action is easy to miss. Check your MLS's current rules on placement and wording, and ask your broker about office policy.

Do I need to say which photos were edited, or is a general note enough?

Name them by number. A general note like some photos virtually staged leaves the buyer guessing which rooms are real, which defeats the point. Listing the numbers and the type of edit takes one sentence and removes the guessing.

Can the description mention the furniture in a virtually staged photo?

No. The furniture is not there and is not included, and describing it sets up the showing to disappoint. Describe the room by its size, windows, floor and light, which are true in the photo and in the empty room.

Should the same disclosure appear on social posts and the property page?

Yes, the same line, everywhere the edited photos appear: remarks, page, caption, flyer. One wording written once and pasted is easier to keep consistent and easier to defend if anyone asks later.
