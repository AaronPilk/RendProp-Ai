Marketing a multifamily building

A multifamily building sells on unit mix, rents, expenses and system ages. Put the numbers first, shoot vacant units and common areas, respect tenants.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A multifamily building is bought on numbers: unit mix, current rents, expenses, vacancy and the age of the roof, boiler and electrical. Put those on the page first, as the seller documents them, photograph the exterior, the common areas, the mechanicals and whichever units are vacant or whose tenants agree, and keep the photos honest about condition because the buyer will inspect every unit.

A multifamily building sells on numbers before pictures: how many units of what size, what each rents for now, what the building costs to run, how many are vacant and how old the roof, boiler, electrical and plumbing are. Put those on the page first, sourced to the seller's documents. Then photograph the exterior, the common areas, the mechanicals and the units you can enter without intruding on tenants. A buyer who gets the numbers up front books an inspection; one who gets a kitchen photo and no rent roll calls three other listings first.

The page is a one-screen summary of the deal

Write it the way the buyer's spreadsheet is laid out.

  • Unit mix. Count of studios, one-beds, two-beds, with approximate square feet per type.
  • Current rents by unit, as the seller's rent roll states them, with lease end dates where the seller permits. Say whether any unit is below market in the seller's view, and mark it as the seller's view.
  • Expenses the seller documents: taxes, insurance, utilities paid by the owner, management, maintenance. State what is included and what is not.
  • Vacancy now and which units are empty.
  • Systems with ages: roof, heating, water heaters, electrical service, plumbing material, windows.
  • Metering: separately metered or master-metered, for each utility.
  • Parking, laundry, storage, and whether laundry brings income.
  • Price and total building square feet in the details fields. If a single beds or baths figure would mislead, leave those fields empty and put the unit mix in the text; the page hides empty sections.

Do not restate lease terms, rent regulation or notice rules in your own words. Point to the leases and tell the buyer to review them with their own advisor; ask your broker how to describe anything you are unsure of.

Shot list for a building with tenants in it

  1. Exterior from across the street, both ends if it is on a corner, with the number of entrances visible.
  2. Entry, mailboxes, hallways, stairwells. Clean, lit, nothing stored in them.
  3. Mechanical room. The boiler with its date plate, the water heaters, the electrical panels with the meter bank. A buyer reads more from these than from any kitchen.
  4. Roof, from a safe vantage, or a drone still from a hired session if the roof is a selling point or a question.
  5. Laundry, storage, bike room, parking with space count visible.
  6. Every vacant unit, fully: living, kitchen, bedroom, bath. Vacant units are where the buyer sees the finish level.
  7. Occupied units only with the tenant's agreement and proper notice, and only the rooms they allow, with their belongings respected. If a tenant declines, say the unit is occupied and similar to a photographed one.
  8. Yard, trash area, exterior stairs, fire escapes. Condition, honestly.

The listing video runs exterior, common areas, one vacant unit, mechanicals, back to the exterior. Keep tenants, their doors and their names out of every frame. For investor audiences the landscape video is the one that matters; a vertical social video is optional and should not show inside occupied units at all.

Where multifamily listings lose the buyer

  • No rent roll on the page. The buyer assumes the rents are bad.
  • Photos of one renovated unit presented as the building. Say which units are renovated and which are not.
  • Tidy edits on common areas. Removing a hallway stain or a damaged stair in an edit shows up on inspection day. Compare with the original and keep the disclosure, or leave the frame alone.
  • Tenants in frame. Their mail, their doors, their faces. None of it.
  • Summarizing leases or regulations. Point to the documents instead.

Tenants in place change how you show the building as well as how you shoot it; the post on marketing a listing with tenants in place covers notice and scheduling.

Worked example: a brick fourplex with one vacancy

A two-storey brick fourplex, four two-bed units of about 850 square feet each, three occupied and one vacant after a turnover with a new kitchen. The seller supplies a rent roll, twelve months of expenses, a 2018 roof invoice and a boiler date plate reading 2015. Two tenants agree to a Tuesday morning photo of their living room only; one declines. The agent shoots the exterior from both corners, the entry and hallway, the stairwell, the mechanical room with the boiler plate and the four meters, the laundry, the four-space lot, the vacant unit in six frames, the two living rooms with permission, and the rear yard with the trash enclosure. Fourteen landscape frames make the listing video, opening on the exterior, with the vacant unit in six frames. The page's first screen reads: four 2-bed units, rents per the seller's roll with lease end dates, seller-documented expenses for the last twelve months, one vacancy, roof 2018, boiler 2015, electric separately metered, gas master-metered, four parking spaces, coin laundry income stated by the seller. The exterior frame is the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a multifamily listing page show first?

Unit mix, current rents from the seller's rent roll, documented expenses, vacancy, and the ages of the roof, heating, water heaters and electrical. Photos come after the numbers for this buyer.

Can I photograph occupied units?

Only with the tenant's agreement and proper notice, and only the rooms they allow. If a tenant declines, say the unit is occupied and similar to a photographed one. Keep tenants, their doors and their names out of every frame.

How do I handle beds and baths for a building with several units?

Put the unit mix in the text. If a single beds or baths number would mislead, leave those fields empty; the page hides sections whose fields are empty.

Should I describe the leases and tenant rules on the page?

No. Point to the leases and tell the buyer to review them with their own advisor. Ask your broker how to word anything about rent rules or notice.
