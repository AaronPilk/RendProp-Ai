Marketing a vacation home

A vacation home is bought by people who are not there. The page is the first visit: the setting, the walk to the water, what conveys, real rental history.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A vacation home is bought by people who live somewhere else and shop from a phone, so the property page has to do the work of a first visit: the setting and the distance to the beach, lake, lifts or town, the house in season, what furniture and gear convey, how the owner gets there from the airport, and the rental history as the seller's documents show it. Shoot the walk from the door to the reason people come, and state everything a buyer would otherwise fly in to check.

A vacation home is bought by someone who is not there. They are scrolling on a phone three states away, deciding whether to spend a weekend flying in, so the property page has to be the first visit: the setting, the walk from the door to the water or the lifts or the square, the house in the season people use it, what conveys, how long the drive is from the airport, and the rental numbers as the seller's documents show them. Show the walk. State the facts a buyer would otherwise come to check. The house itself is often the third thing they look at.

The buyer is far away and that changes the page

Local buyers fill gaps with a drive-by; remote buyers cannot, and they rarely ask. No distance to the beach reads as far. No summer photo reads as grey all year. No word on the furniture reads as unfurnished. Every missing fact is a reason not to book the flight. The posts on second-home buyers who shop from far away and out-of-town buyers cover the follow-up; this one is about the page and the photos.

What the page must say

  • The setting in one line: the lake, the beach, the mountain, the town, and the distance from the front door in minutes on foot or in the car.
  • Travel: the nearest airport and the drive time, year-round road access or seasonal, and whether a four-wheel drive is needed in winter.
  • Season: when the area is used, when the photos were taken, what the house is like in the off-season.
  • Turnkey or not: furniture, linens, kitchenware, boat, kayaks, skis, golf cart, each listed as conveying or not.
  • Rental history, if any, as the seller documents it: gross for a stated period, occupancy if known, the platform or manager, labelled as the seller's figures. No projections.
  • Rental permission: whether the home is currently permitted or registered for short-term rental, per the seller's documents. Rules vary and change; tell the buyer to verify locally and ask your broker how to word it.
  • Management: who looks after snow, lawn, pool and cleaning now, and whether that can continue.
  • An owner's closet or locked storage if the home is rented.
  • Utilities and internet as they exist; remote buyers plan to work from the house some weeks.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in the details fields, and sleeping capacity in the text if the home is rented.

Shooting the reason people come, then the house

  1. The walk. From the front door to the sand, the dock, the lift line or the main street, in three or four frames with distance readable. If it is a drive, the destination through the windshield and the time it took.
  2. The destination itself, on a good day: the beach, the lake, the slope, the square.
  3. The house from outside, in season, with whatever gear belongs there: chairs on the deck, the boat at the dock, skis by the door.
  4. The main living space toward the view or the deck.
  5. The deck or porch looking out, with the furniture that conveys on it.
  6. Kitchen, stocked enough to show it works for a group.
  7. Every bedroom, with the bed sizes visible, because sleeping capacity is a rental question.
  8. Baths, laundry, the bunk room, the game room if there is one.
  9. Gear storage, the owner's closet, the garage, and the hot tub or fire pit.
  10. The town, two frames of the main street, if it is part of the appeal.

The listing video opens on the destination or the walk, not the front door. The vertical social video from portrait frames of the walk and the deck is the version sent from one phone to another, which is how vacation homes are often found. A recorded voiceover saying the distances helps a remote viewer more than music.

Worked example: a 3-bed cabin six minutes from the lake

A 3-bed, 2-bath cabin on a wooded lot a six-minute walk from a public lake beach and a ten-minute drive from a ski town, rented in summer and winter through a local manager, sold furnished with the kayaks and the hot tub, an hour and forty minutes from the nearest airport on a paved county road. The agent shoots in July at 5 p.m.: the trail from the door to the beach in four frames, the beach, the cabin with the deck furniture out, the great room toward the deck, the deck with the hot tub, the kitchen, three bedrooms, both baths, the mudroom with the gear wall, the owner's closet, and two frames of the town's main street. The page says: six-minute walk to the beach, ten-minute drive to town, airport one hour forty by paved road, year-round access, furnished with listed exceptions, kayaks and hot tub convey, sleeps eight, rented through a named manager with the seller's gross for twelve months, current short-term rental registration per the seller's documents with a note to verify, fibre internet. Sixteen landscape frames make the listing video, which opens on the trail. The deck with the hot tub is the main photo.

What goes wrong with vacation home listings

  • Projected income. A figure the buyer might earn is a guess. Only the seller's actual figures for a stated period belong on the page.
  • Stating rental rules. Say what the seller's permit or registration shows and tell the buyer to verify.
  • Off-season photos with no note. A grey lake in November with no mention of July costs the listing its reason to exist.
  • No distances. Near the beach means nothing from three states away.
  • A blue sky edit on every frame. One real good day beats twelve identical skies; if you edit, disclose and compare against the original.
  • Furniture in the photos that leaves with the seller. List what conveys.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should the first line of a vacation home listing say?

The setting and the distance to it: the lake, beach, lifts or town, in minutes on foot or by car from the front door. Remote buyers cannot check it themselves, and a missing distance reads as a long one.

Can I state rental income on the page?

Only the seller's actual figures for a stated period, labelled as the seller's, with the manager or platform named. Do not project what a buyer might earn, and do not state short-term rental rules; tell the buyer to verify them.

Should the photos be taken in season?

Yes, or at least include dated in-season photos from the seller alongside current ones, with the season stated on the page. A vacation home photographed in its quiet months with no note loses its reason to exist.

What should the listing say about furniture and gear?

List what conveys and what does not, item by item for anything in the photos. Remote buyers assume furnished means everything they see, and the disagreement surfaces at contract.
