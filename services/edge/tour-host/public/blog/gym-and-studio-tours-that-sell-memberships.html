Gym and studio tours that sell memberships

A gym tour sells when it shows the floor, the equipment a prospect cares about, the changing rooms and membership terms, with a way to book a visit.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A gym or studio tour sells a membership when it removes the reason someone has not walked in yet: not knowing what the place is like. Show the floor wide, the specific equipment or class room they care about, the changing rooms, and the membership terms, all on one page with a form to book a first visit.

A gym tour sells memberships when it removes the one thing stopping a prospect from walking in: not knowing what it is like inside. Show the training floor wide so they can judge the space, show the specific equipment or class room they care about, show the changing rooms because everyone wonders, and put the membership terms and a way to book a first visit on the same page. That is the whole job.

Who is watching and what they fear

Most people looking at a gym page are not comparing squat racks. They are deciding whether they would feel comfortable there. A 45-year-old coming back to training after ten years is worried about being the odd one out on a floor full of twenty-somethings. A powerlifter moving to a new city wants to see a platform and a bar that is not bent. A parent looking at a pilates studio wants to know if there is parking and whether a 6am class exists.

The tour should answer those fears in the first fifteen seconds. Wide shots of the floor at a normal hour. A clear look at the equipment that defines your gym. A changing room that is clean and bright. Faces of staff, if they agree to be in it, because a coach's face is half the reason people join.

The shot list for a gym

  • Entrance and front desk. The first thing a new member sees. Keep the counter clear.
  • Training floor, wide, from the entrance. Shoot at 0.5x if the floor is big, but step back from the nearest machine so it does not stretch.
  • Free weight area. Racks, platforms, dumbbell range. Lifters zoom in on this one.
  • Cardio and the view from it. Nobody loves a treadmill, but a treadmill facing a window is a different thing.
  • Class studio, empty, lights on. Mats or bikes set out in rows show capacity better than a crowded class.
  • Changing rooms, showers, lockers. Empty, clean, lit. This photo closes more memberships than the equipment shots.
  • Something that is yours. The sauna, the turf strip, the coffee bar, the kids' corner. Whatever people mention when they recommend you.

Busy or empty

Shoot the floor empty for the wide shots so the space reads clearly, then add two or three photos with members training, with their permission, so the gym looks alive. An entirely empty gym looks closed. An entirely full one looks like a wait for every machine. See photographing a gym when it is busy and when it is empty for the timing.

Worked example: a strength gym with two class rooms

A 6,000 square foot strength gym with a turf lane and two class rooms shoots fourteen landscape photos at 1pm on a Tuesday when the floor is quiet. The listing video opens on the exterior, then the floor from the door, the racks, the turf, class room one set for a circuit session, class room two empty with mats out, the changing room, and a final wide from the mezzanine. A 25-second voiceover names the opening hours and the fact that every membership starts with a coached first session. The page shows hours, the membership options typed in plain words, the gallery, the video and a form. The form asks the visitor which date they want to come in. That date is the first visit, and the first visit is where memberships are sold.

The membership line that actually matters

People do not sign up from a page. They sign up after a visit. So the page has one job: get the visit booked. State the membership options in a sentence each, no asterisks, and make the enquiry form the easiest thing on the page to use. If someone has to call during business hours to ask about a trial, half of them will not.

What goes wrong with gym tours

  • Showing only the equipment. It tells a nervous beginner nothing about how it feels to be there.
  • Shooting at peak hour. Faces you do not have permission to publish, and a floor that looks like a queue.
  • Dark changing rooms. If you cannot light it, do not include it; a dark locker room reads as dirty.
  • Vertical social clips as the main video. Fine for a reel, wrong for the page. The page video should be in landscape orientation so the floor reads wide.
  • AI edits that add equipment. If an edit puts a rack where there is none, the first visit is the moment you lose them. Compare any edited photo with the original and keep the disclosure.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a gym tour video show first?

The training floor, wide, from the entrance. It tells a prospect the scale, the light and the crowd in one shot. Equipment close-ups come after, and the changing rooms should always be included.

Should the gym be busy or empty in the tour?

Mostly empty for the wide shots so the space reads, with a few photos of members training, taken with their permission, so it looks alive. Avoid peak hour; you will not be able to publish the faces and the floor looks like a wait.

How do I get memberships from a page?

You do not; you get first visits from a page and memberships from the visit. State the membership options plainly and make the form that books a visit the easiest thing on the page to use.

Which Rendprop mode is right for a gym or studio?

The gyms and studios mode. The page asks for details such as hours and membership instead of price and beds, and the enquiry form asks for the date the visitor wants to come in.
