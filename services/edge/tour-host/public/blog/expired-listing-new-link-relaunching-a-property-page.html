Expired listing, new link: relaunching a property page

When an expired listing comes back, decide whether the old page helps or hurts. Usually it hurts: relaunch with a fresh page, new main photo and new price.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. When an expired or withdrawn listing comes back to market, the question is whether the old page helps or hurts. If buyers saw it at the old price for months, it hurts, and you should relaunch with a fresh page, a different main photo, the new price and a video that opens differently. If the home was off the market briefly and few people saw it, keep the page and update it. Either way, the old links out in the world should land somewhere sensible.

When an expired or withdrawn listing comes back, the first decision is whether the old page is an asset or a liability. If the home sat for months at the old price and the page was shared widely, it is a liability, because buyers remember it, so relaunch with a fresh page, a different main photo, the new price and a video that opens on a different room; if the home was off the market for a few weeks and few people saw it, keep the page, update the price and the status, and move on. In both cases, make sure the old links still land somewhere that does not embarrass you.

How to tell which case you are in

Ask three questions. How long was it listed before. Did the price drop more than once. Did the previous agent share the page broadly, with a sign, portals and social. Two yeses means buyers have a memory of this home at a price it did not sell for, and the marketing has to look like a different listing. One yes or none means a quick update is enough.

If you are the new agent after another agent's expiry, assume the fresh-page case. You want the seller and the buyers to see a change, and you want your own link, your own form and your own inbox.

What a relaunch page changes

  • The main photo. If the old listing led with the front exterior in summer, lead with the kitchen, or a new exterior in different light. A buyer scrolling past should not recognise it at a glance.
  • The price. The new price, matching the MLS, visible at the top. A relaunch at the old price is a re-run.
  • The gallery order. Reorder so the first five are different from before. Add any new photos: a repainted room, a cleared yard, a twilight exterior.
  • The video. A new opening scene and a new first line. Buyers who watched the old one will know in two seconds whether this is new.
  • The link. A new page means a new link. Update the sign rider, the flyer, the MLS tour field and your social bio. Do not reuse the old QR code.

What to do with the old links

The old page was texted and posted. Those links still exist. If the old page was yours, mark it sold or archived so it stops presenting the home at the old price, or if the tool allows, point it at the new page. If the old page belonged to a previous agent, you cannot control it; ask the seller to tell anyone who has it that the home has been relisted with you, and make your new page easy to find from the address.

Do not leave an old page live at the old price with a working enquiry form. Buyers who find it will either enquire about a price that no longer exists or conclude the home has been on the market forever.

A worked example: the colonial that sat through winter

A 4-bed colonial listed in October at $749,000, dropped to $725,000 in December, expired in February. Twenty-two showings, no offers, a page shared on every channel. The seller lists with you in March.

You have the home repainted in the entry and the primary, the yard cleaned, and a fresh shoot done on a bright day, with a twilight exterior. New page: main photo is the twilight exterior, which the old listing never had. Price $699,000. Gallery leads with twilight, then the repainted entry, then the kitchen, then the primary. New video opens on the twilight exterior with a first line about the spring market, not the house. New link on a new sign rider, in the MLS tour field, and in a just-relisted post. The old page is archived.

Buyers who saw it in November see a different home at a different price. The ones who passed in November get a reason to look again.

Relaunch mistakes that give the game away

  • Same photos, new price. Buyers recognise it. The price cut becomes the story instead of the home.
  • Old page left live. Two pages for one home, two prices, two forms. Confusion and lost enquiries.
  • Old QR code on the new sign. It points to the dead page.
  • The relaunch video is the old video with a new title card. Two seconds in, the buyer knows.
  • Relaunching before the home has changed. If the price is the only difference, say so plainly to the seller and do the minimum: update the page, keep the link.

Tell the seller which case you chose and why

Sellers of expired listings are tired and suspicious. Explain the decision: "Buyers saw this at $725,000 for four months. We are going to make it look new, and here is what new looks like." Then show them the new page on your phone next to the old one. The difference should be obvious to them before it is obvious to a buyer.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I keep the same property page when I relist?

Only if the home was off the market briefly and few people saw the old page. If it sat for months at a price it did not sell for, a fresh page with a new main photo, new price and new video gives buyers a reason to look again.

What do I do with the old page's links that are already out there?

If the old page is yours, mark it sold or archived so it stops presenting the old price with a working form. If it belonged to a previous agent, you cannot control it; make your new page easy to find and ask the seller to redirect anyone who has the old link.

Do I need new photos to relaunch?

Not always, but a different main photo and a reordered gallery help a lot. If the seller has made changes, even small ones like fresh paint or a cleared yard, a short reshoot of those rooms and a twilight exterior give you new material without a full shoot.

Can I reuse the old QR code on the new sign rider?

Not if the link has changed. A new page means a new code. Reprint the rider and check the code from the curb before the sign goes back in.
