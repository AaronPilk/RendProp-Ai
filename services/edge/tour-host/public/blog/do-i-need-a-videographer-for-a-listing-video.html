Do I need a videographer for a listing video

Not for most listings. A photo-based video built from the listing photos covers a standard home. Hire a videographer when the home must be felt in motion.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Not for most listings. A photo-based video built from the photos you already have covers a standard home well, posts the same day and costs a fraction of a shoot. Hire a videographer when the property has to be experienced in motion: a large or unusual layout, a luxury home where the film is part of the pitch, or a seller who expects it.

Hire a videographer when the home has to be experienced in motion: a large or unusual layout, a luxury property where the film is part of the pitch, or a seller who has made it a condition of the listing.

At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, a filmed listing video runs $250–600, and a social cutdown edited from that footage adds $75–150. On a mid-priced home that line item often decides whether the listing gets any video at all. A photo-based video removes that decision.

What a videographer adds, honestly

A good videographer brings three things a photo set cannot: continuous movement through a space, a person in the frame where that helps, and a point of view that was chosen on site with the light in front of them. On a 6,000 square foot home with a double-height entry and a kitchen that opens onto a terrace, that continuous movement is the point. The buyer needs to feel the walk from the front door to the pool.

On a three-bed with a conventional plan, the walk is not the point. The rooms are. A buyer wants to see the kitchen, the living room, the primary bedroom, the bathroom and the garden, in that order, with enough time on each to judge it. A sequence of good photos with gentle camera motion, a short voiceover and captions does that job, and does it in a format the buyer can watch with the sound off on a train.

A Tuesday listing, two ways

A three-bed in a cul-de-sac is photographed Monday afternoon: 28 landscape photos and 8 portrait photos shot for social. The listing goes live Tuesday morning.

With a videographer, the earliest realistic film date is the same Monday if you booked both crews together, with the edit back later in the week. The video reaches social and the property page after the first wave of buyers has already seen the listing.

With a photo-based video, Monday evening you choose 14 of the 28 photos, record a 35-second voiceover, add captions and export a landscape video for the property page and a vertical version for Instagram. Both are live when the listing goes live Tuesday. The first buyers see the video, not just the photos. For a standard home that timing matters more than the extra polish of a filmed piece.

When to book the videographer anyway

  • The home is large, layered or architecturally unusual and the plan itself is the selling point.
  • The price point supports it and the competing listings in that bracket all have filmed video.
  • The seller expects it, and the relationship is worth more than the fee.
  • You want footage with people, lifestyle sequences or a presenter on camera.
  • The exterior or grounds need motion to be understood, in which case a drone operator may be the better hire.

Even then, make the photo-based video as well. It is ready first, it reuses the same photo set, and it gives you a second cut for the platforms the filmed video does not fit. The post on photo-based video versus a walkthrough goes further into which format wins where.

What goes wrong when agents skip the videographer badly

The failure is rarely the decision to skip; it is what replaces the film. A shaky phone walkthrough shot at a jog, with the agent breathing into the microphone and the toilet lid up, is worse than no video. A slideshow with a hard cut every two seconds and a stock track is not much better.

The other failure is reusing poor photos. A photo-based video is only as good as the stills it is built from. Dark rooms, tilted verticals and clutter get bigger and slower on video. Fix the photos first, then make the video.

What to do on the next listing

Decide per listing, not per year. For the next listing, write down the price point, the layout and what the seller expects. If none of those three calls for motion, make the photo-based video from the shoot you already paid for and put the videographer budget toward the twilight exterior or the staging.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How much does a real estate videographer cost?

At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, a filmed listing video runs $250–600, and a social cutdown or reel edit from the same footage adds $75–150. Larger homes and longer edits sit at the top of those ranges or above them.

Is a photo-based video good enough for a luxury listing?

It can be the first video a luxury listing gets, because it is ready the same day as the photos. For the main film on a high-value home, buyers in that bracket often expect filmed movement, so plan a videographer as well and use the photo-based video for social and early marketing.

Can I film the walkthrough myself instead of hiring someone?

Yes, if you take it seriously: slow, level, wide lens, a tidy home and a quiet moment. A rushed handheld walkthrough does more harm than a photo-based video. Treat it as a separate asset, not a replacement for the photo video.

What do I need to make a photo-based listing video?

A set of good landscape photos for the listing video, and portrait photos if you want a vertical social version. Add a short script of around 30 to 40 seconds and a few minutes to review the result before you post it.
