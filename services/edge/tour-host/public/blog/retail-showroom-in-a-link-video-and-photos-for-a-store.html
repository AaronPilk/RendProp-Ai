Retail showroom in a link: video and photos for a store

Put the store in one link: a short floor video, a gallery of the displays, hours and a contact card, sent to anyone asking whether the trip is worth it.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A retail showroom in a link is a single page with a short video of the floor, a gallery of the displays, the hours and a contact card, that you can text or post to anyone who asks what you stock. It is not an online shop. It answers the question that gets someone through the door: is it worth the drive.

A showroom in a link is one page that shows a shopper the store before they drive to it: a short video walking the floor, a gallery of the displays, the hours, and a way to ask a question. It is not an online shop and it does not need a cart. Its job is to answer the question a shopper asks before leaving the house: is this place worth the trip.

For a furniture showroom, a lighting store, a bike shop or a boutique, that question decides the visit.

The difference between a store page and a shop

An ecommerce site sells individual products. A showroom page sells the store. The shopper who finds a sofa online has a hundred options. The shopper who sees your showroom, with the sofa in a room setting next to the lamp and the rug, has a reason to come and sit on it. The page should feel like walking the floor with the owner, not scrolling a catalogue.

This matters most for anything people want to touch before buying: furniture, mattresses, kitchens, bikes, instruments, bridal, tile. For those stores the showroom is the product.

What to photograph on a retail floor

  1. The storefront from the street, with the sign readable. People use it to recognise the place when they arrive.
  2. The floor from the door. Wide and level, so the shopper gets the scale and the layout in one frame.
  3. Each display zone as a room. A furniture store might have six: living, dining, bedroom, outdoor, lighting, rugs. One wide photo per zone, shot from where a shopper would stand.
  4. Three or four hero pieces. The things that make people stop. Not product shots on white; the piece in context on your floor.
  5. The practical corner. The parking, the loading door, the lift if you are upstairs. For big purchases this is a real question.

Shoot when the store is tidy and the lights are all on, before opening or after close. Clear the counters. Straighten the price tags or turn them away from the lens; a page full of readable prices goes stale the day you run a sale.

The video: a one-minute walk of the floor

From the landscape photos, a listing video opens on the storefront, walks through the zones in the order a shopper would, lingers on the hero pieces and ends back at the door. Under a minute. Record a plain voiceover: who you are, what you carry, when you are open, where to park. Do not read a product list. The vertical cut of the same photos, from portrait shots of the hero pieces, is the version for Instagram and TikTok, with captions so it works with the sound off.

Worked example: a lighting showroom upstairs on a side street

A lighting showroom on the first floor of a building with no street-level window struggles with walk-in traffic. The owner shoots twelve landscape photos: the street door and the sign, the stairs, the main floor from the top of the stairs with every fixture lit, the pendant wall, the table lamp section, the outdoor lighting corner, three hero pieces, and the goods lift. The video runs fifty seconds. The page shows the hours, including the Thursday late opening, a gallery and a contact card with the direct line. The link goes on the Instagram bio, on the flyer in the interior designers' trade pack, and in every email reply to someone asking what they carry. The lift photo alone has answered the question every designer used to call about.

Where store pages go wrong

  • Trying to be a shop. Prices, SKUs and a cart turn the page into a worse version of your real ecommerce site. Keep it to the showroom.
  • Stock that has sold. If the hero piece in the video went out the door in March, update the photos. A shopper who drives in for it will not come back.
  • Cluttered counters and open boxes. Tidy the room before you shoot rather than editing the clutter out afterwards; if you do use an AI tidy edit, keep the disclosure and compare it with the original.
  • Hours missing. The single most common reason a store page fails. Type them in by day.
  • A link nobody can find. The page is only useful if it is in the bio, the sign, the email signature and the reply to every enquiry.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Is a showroom page the same as an online store?

No. An online store sells individual products with a cart. A showroom page sells the visit: it shows the floor, the displays, the hours and how to reach you. Keep prices and SKUs on your ecommerce site if you have one.

What is the most important photo of a retail store?

The floor from the door, wide and level with every light on. It gives the shopper the scale and layout in one frame and tells them whether the trip is worth it. The storefront with a readable sign comes second, because people use it to find you.

Should prices be visible in the photos?

Turn tags away from the lens or straighten them so they are not readable. Prices change with every sale and a page full of old prices looks neglected within weeks.

Which Rendprop mode is right for a store?

The retail mode. The page asks for store details such as hours, and the sections you do not fill in stay hidden. For a showroom that also hosts events, the event venues mode asks for details such as capacity instead.
