How much does a listing video cost

A filmed listing video runs $250–600 at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, plus $75–150 for a social cutdown. A photo-based video costs far less.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, a filmed listing video from a videographer costs $250–600, and a social cutdown or reel edited from it adds $75–150. A photo-based video made from the listing photos you already have costs whatever the app's plan covers, with no videographer fee on top of the shoot. Those numbers are national ranges. A one-bed condo filmed in twenty minutes sits at the bottom. A 7,000 square foot home with a twilight exterior, a presenter and two revisions sits above the top. Ask for a fixed quote per property rather than an hourly rate.

What the fee is actually paying for

A filmed video has four cost centres, and knowing them makes quotes easier to compare.

  • Time on site. Usually one to two hours for a standard home, longer for large homes and anything with grounds. This is where the stabiliser, the lighting and the second pass through tidied rooms live.
  • The edit. Often more hours than the shoot. Colour, pacing, music licensing, captions, the agent's intro if there is one. Revisions are usually limited; a third round costs extra.
  • Deliverables. One 16:9 master is the base. Vertical cuts, a 15-second teaser, an unbranded version for the MLS and a branded one for social each add time, which is why the cutdown is a separate line.
  • Travel and scheduling. A rural listing an hour away costs more than one across town, and a weekend or twilight slot often carries a premium.

A budget for a $450,000 listing

Take a three-bed at $450,000 with a standard shoot already booked at $150–300 for the photos. Adding a filmed video at $400 and a vertical cutdown at $100 puts the video line at $500 before the agent has posted anything. On a listing that sells in three weeks, that is a reasonable spend. On a listing that sits for four months and is relisted with new photos, it is a sunk cost and the video is now out of date.

The alternative is to build a photo-based video from the 28 landscape photos the photographer already delivered, record a 35-second voiceover, add captions, and export both a landscape video for the property page and a vertical video for Instagram. The per-listing cost is the time it takes, which is under an hour. When the price changes, you update the script and re-export rather than paying for a re-edit.

Most agents end up with a split: photo-based video on every listing, filmed video on the ones where the home or the seller justifies it.

Where the money gets wasted

  1. Paying for a video that is posted once. A $500 video that goes on Instagram once and nowhere else has done a tenth of its job. It belongs on the property page, in the MLS virtual-tour field as an unbranded link, in the email to your database and in the listing presentation for the next seller.
  2. Paying for length. A four-minute film costs more to edit and fewer people finish it. Thirty to sixty seconds for social, under two minutes for the property page.
  3. Paying twice after a price cut. If the video names the price in the voiceover, a reduction means a re-edit. Keep the price in the caption and on the page, not in the audio, unless you can re-export the video yourself.
  4. Paying for footage without a home for it. If there is no property page, the video lives on a social feed where it is buried in a week.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What does a real estate videographer charge per listing?

At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, $250–600 for a filmed listing video, with a social cutdown or reel adding $75–150. Large homes, twilight shoots, presenters on camera and extra revisions push the price above those ranges.

Is a cheaper listing video worth it?

A cheap filmed video is usually a fast shoot with a template edit, and it shows. For most standard homes a well-made photo-based video from a good photo set is a better result than a rushed film, and it is ready the same day.

Who should pay for the listing video, the agent or the seller?

Practice varies by market and brokerage. Many agents treat standard media, including a photo-based video, as part of their service, and discuss a filmed video with the seller as an additional item on higher-value homes.

Does the video price include the vertical version for social?

Often not. The vertical cutdown is usually quoted separately, which is why the $75–150 line exists. Ask for both formats in the quote, or build the vertical version yourself from portrait photos.
