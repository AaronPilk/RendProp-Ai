Photos and video from the open house itself

Shoot at the open house for two uses: a short setup clip before anyone arrives, and a behind-the-scenes shot for your own brand after. Never film visitors.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Shoot at the open house for two things only: a short clip before the doors open, with the house set up and the midday light, and a behind-the-scenes shot afterward for your own social accounts. Do not film visitors, their cars or the sign-in sheet. The listing's real photos and video were made on shoot day and stay on the property page; Sunday's phone footage is social content, not listing content.

Shoot at the open house for two things and no more. Before the doors open, a short clip of the house ready: the table set, the kitchen clear, the light at noon, the sign on the lawn. After the doors close, one shot for your own brand: the empty hall, the stacked flyers, a line about the afternoon. Visitors do not get filmed. Their cars do not get filmed. The sign-in sheet does not get photographed.

And none of Sunday's footage replaces the listing photos. The gallery and the video on the property page were made on shoot day, in planned light, with the house prepared. A phone snap from 12:40 with your reflection in the oven door is not an upgrade.

Rosewood, Sunday, two clips

A bungalow on Rosewood Avenue, open Sunday 1 to 3. You arrive at 12:15.

12:40, the setup clip. The house is ready and empty. You walk, phone in hand, from the gate to the front door, through the hall into the kitchen where the laptop sits on the island with the page open, and out to the deck where the sun is on the table. Twenty seconds, one take, no narration, the real sound of the street. Then three stills: the front with the open house rider, the kitchen island, the deck. Posted as a story at 12:50 with the hours and the page link: “Open until 3. Page with the full gallery and video linked.”

3:10, the closing shot. Everyone has gone. One still of the hall with the flyers half gone and the afternoon light on the floor. One line, posted Monday morning rather than Sunday: “A good afternoon on Rosewood. If you could not make it, the page has the video and a form for a private visit.” No numbers, no “packed,” no claim you would have to defend.

Two posts, both honest, neither showing a single visitor's face.

What to capture before the doors open

  • The approach. Gate to door, because the page's video starts there too, and the story echoes it.
  • The setup. The island with the page on the screen, the table, the open blinds. This is the “ready” shot and it reads as care.
  • The light. Whatever the house does at that hour. If the deck is in sun at 12:40, that is the frame.
  • The sign. The lawn sign with the rider, from the kerb. It places the house for people who know the street.
  • A recorded walkthrough, if you want one. The hour before an open house is the one time the house is prepared and empty with you in it. If the listing could use a handheld walkthrough that viewers can scroll, this is when to film it, not during.

What not to capture

  • Visitors. Faces, backs, children, anyone. People at an open house did not agree to be content.
  • Cars and plates. The drive full of cars is a tempting “busy” shot and it is a list of who was there.
  • The sign-in sheet. Names and phone numbers.
  • Documents on the table. The disclosure folder, the seller's invoices.
  • Anything that contradicts the listing. A snap of the staged-in-photos living room, empty, with no label, posted next to the staged version, raises the question you did not want to raise.

What Sunday footage is for, and what it is not for

It is for your own channels: stories, a behind-the-scenes post, the running record of a working agent. It shows that you turn up, prepare, and care about the details. It is not for the property page, not for the MLS, and not for the listing's main video. Keep the two sets apart. The page carries the considered photos; social carries the Sunday.

If a Sunday shot is genuinely better than a gallery photo, because the light did something the shoot day missed, treat it like any other listing photo: shot on purpose, from the right height, with the room prepared, and added to the gallery deliberately rather than as a replacement for the main photo.

Where it goes wrong

  • A reel with visitors in it. Even blurred, people recognise themselves and their neighbours do too.
  • Replacing the main photo with a phone snap. It is the first thing every buyer sees. It deserves the shoot-day frame.
  • “Packed.” If it was not, the people who came know, and the seller will ask why there are no offers.
  • Filming during. You are supposed to be greeting people and standing where you can see the door.
  • Posting the setup clip after the open house. It is a “come now” piece; it has no job at 4 p.m.
  • Forgetting the link. The setup story needs the page link and the hours or it is just a nice video of a kitchen.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Can I film visitors at an open house for a social post?

No. People who came to look at a house did not agree to appear in your content, and their neighbours will recognise them even blurred. Film the empty house before the doors open and the empty hall after they close. Leave cars, plates, the sign-in sheet and documents out of frame as well.

Should I replace listing photos with photos taken at the open house?

Not as a habit. The gallery and the video on the property page were made on shoot day in planned light with the house prepared. If a Sunday frame is genuinely better, shoot it properly, from the right height with the room ready, and add it to the gallery on purpose rather than swapping the main photo for a phone snap.

When should I post the open house setup clip?

As soon as it is shot, before the doors open, with the hours and the property page link. It is a come-now piece. The closing shot of the empty hall can wait until Monday morning, with one honest line and the link for people who could not make it.

Is the open house a good time to record a walkthrough video?

The hour before it is. The house is prepared, empty and you are already there. Record it then, not while visitors are in the house. A recorded walkthrough is separate from the photo-based listing video and should be reviewed before it goes on the page.
