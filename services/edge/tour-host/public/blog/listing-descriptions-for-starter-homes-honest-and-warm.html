Listing descriptions for starter homes: honest and warm

Starter home copy says what works today, what it costs to live there and what a buyer can do later. Warm means specific; honest means naming ages.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A starter home description should tell the buyer three things quickly: what works today, what it costs to live there, and what they could do later. Warm means specific and human, not cute. Honest means naming the age of the roof and the size of the second bedroom before the showing does it for you.

A starter home description should tell a first-time buyer three things in the first hundred words: what works today, what it costs to live there, and what they could do with it later. Warm comes from being specific and human about a modest house. Honest comes from naming the age of the roof and the size of the second bedroom before the showing does it for you.

The buyer for a $285,000 two-bed is usually comparing it with rent, is nervous about surprises, and has read a dozen descriptions this week that called small rooms “cozy” and old kitchens “charming”. They have learned to translate. A description that skips the code and says what is actually there stands out, and it brings the right people to the door.

Who is reading, and what they are afraid of

First-time buyers are not afraid of small. They are afraid of the thing they did not see: the furnace that dies in January, the roof that fails the insurer, the bedroom that turns out to fit a bed and nothing else. Every sentence that names an age, a size or a date removes one fear. Every sentence that hides one adds to the pile.

They also want permission to like the house. A line about the morning light in the kitchen or the maple over the back fence is not fluff if it is true and specific. It is the warmth. Keep it to one or two lines and put the facts around it.

The three paragraphs

  • What works today. The things a buyer can move in and use: the 2020 roof, the furnace serviced this spring, the fenced yard, the washer and dryer that stay. Write the dates. “Furnace and water heater replaced in 2021” is worth more than any adjective.
  • What it costs to live there. Property taxes, any association fee, whether the utilities are gas or electric, what heating a winter has typically cost if the seller will share it. Buyers moving from rent are doing this math in their heads; do it for them, and check your MLS's rules on where those figures go.
  • What they could do later. Only things that are physically true. “The unfinished basement has 7-foot ceilings and a rough-in for a bath” is a fact. “Easily add a second bath” is a promise you cannot keep for them.

Saying what condition it is in

The honest line is almost always the better line. “Original 1978 kitchen, clean and working, with a newer dishwasher” tells the buyer exactly what to expect and lets them budget for the kitchen they want. “Needs TLC” makes them imagine the worst. “Updated kitchen” when it is not sends them home angry. Write the year and the state, and let the photos show the rest.

Size the same way. A 9 by 10 second bedroom is a 9 by 10 second bedroom. Say “fits a double bed and a desk” if it does. Buyers who measure at the showing and find the number matches start to trust the rest of the page.

The same two-bed, two ways

Before: “Adorable and cozy starter home with tons of potential. Charming kitchen, nice-sized bedrooms and a great yard. Perfect for first-time buyers or investors. Won't last.”

After: “A 1978 two-bed on a fenced quarter-acre, with a 2020 roof and a furnace and water heater from 2021. The kitchen is original, clean and working, with a window over the sink facing the back yard. The second bedroom is 9 by 10 and fits a double bed and a desk. The unfinished basement has 7-foot ceilings and a rough-in for a bath. Taxes were $2,640 last year. Showings from Friday.”

The second version is the one a nervous buyer forwards to a parent.

What goes wrong in starter home copy

  • The code words. “Cozy” for small, “charming” for old, “potential” for unfinished, “investor special” for a house you would not want to describe. Buyers decode them all and assume the worst.
  • Describing the buyer. “Perfect for a young couple” or “ideal for a growing family” describes people rather than the property. Describe the two bedrooms and the fenced yard and let the reader decide. Phrases about who should live there are worth running past your broker.
  • Promising the future. You do not know whether the town will permit a second storey. State what exists.
  • Skipping the running costs. The buyer will find the tax figure anyway. Finding it late feels like a catch.
  • Overdressing the photos. A twilight edit on a two-bed bungalow and a virtually staged living room full of designer furniture can set up a showing that disappoints. If you use edits, keep them modest, label them, and describe the real rooms in the text.

Do this before you publish

Read your draft as the buyer's parent would, looking for the thing nobody mentioned. Add the age of the roof, the furnace and the kitchen, the size of the smallest bedroom and last year's taxes. FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I mention that a starter home needs work?

Yes, specifically. Name the item, its age and its condition: original 1978 kitchen, clean and working. That lets a buyer budget and keeps them from feeling tricked at the showing. Vague phrases like needs TLC make buyers imagine worse than the truth.

Is it a good idea to include property taxes in the description?

Buyers comparing a first home with rent want the figure, and they will find it anyway. State last year's taxes and any association fee, and check your MLS's current rules on which fields those numbers belong in.

Can I say the home is perfect for first-time buyers?

It is better to describe the home and let the buyer place themselves in it. Phrases about who a home is ideal for describe people rather than property, and some of them are the kind of language to check with your broker. Two bedrooms, a fenced yard and a 2020 roof say enough.

How warm is too warm?

One or two true, specific lines are plenty: the morning light in the kitchen, the maple over the back fence. Beyond that, warmth turns into adjectives, and adjectives are what first-time buyers have learned to distrust.
