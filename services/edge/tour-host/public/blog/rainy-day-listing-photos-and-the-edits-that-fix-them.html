Rainy-day listing photos and the edits that fix them

Rain is an interiors day. Shoot inside, shoot the exterior between showers, and use a sky edit only when the sky is the sole change, with its disclosure.

Rain is an interiors day. The soft, even light that comes through a window on a wet morning suits most rooms better than hard sun, so shoot inside and leave the exterior for a gap between showers or another morning. If the schedule leaves you with a wet exterior and no second visit, there is one defensible edit: replace the sky, keep the disclosure, and change nothing else. Wet pavement, dark windows and a flat lawn are the truth of that morning, and a buyer can read them.

What rain does to each part of the shoot

Indoors, rain is close to ideal. No hard shadows across the floor, no blown-out window on one side of the room, and a photographer can balance the grey outside against the lamps inside with a bracketed exposure. Turn on every lamp, open every blind, and shoot in the order that keeps the darkest rooms for whenever the sky is brightest.

Outdoors, rain takes away the three things an exterior photo relies on: a sky with detail, light on the facade, and a dry, defined edge between pavement, lawn and house. Everything flattens to the same grey.

Shooting the exterior in rain anyway

If there is no second visit, get the best wet exterior you can rather than none.

  • Wait for the lightest ten minutes. Rain is rarely constant. Shoot the front during the brightest patch, even if it is still drizzling.
  • Keep the lens dry. Shoot from under an umbrella or from the open door of a car. A drop on the lens is the one thing no edit fixes cleanly.
  • Turn on every front-facing light. Lit windows give the facade some life against the grey, the same trick as a twilight shot.
  • Consider waiting for dusk. A wet twilight with every window lit and the pavement reflecting the house can be a strong photo. The rain becomes atmosphere rather than a problem.

The edits that fix a rainy exterior, and the ones that do not

Blue sky: defensible. The sky is the one part of the frame that is weather rather than property. Replacing a flat white sky with a plain blue one, with the AI-alteration disclosure on the photo, shows the buyer the same house on a different day. Keep it plain; dramatic clouds the day did not have are the version buyers notice. Check the edge against the roofline and any bare trees, because that is where sky edits fail. The practical guide is in blue sky replacement for listing photos.

Brightening and warming: fine, if it is global. Lifting the exposure and warming the colour across the whole frame is the same adjustment any photographer makes.

Drying the pavement or greening the wet lawn: not defensible. The AI has to invent texture that was not photographed, and the result is a house that looks dry under a sky that looks sunny while the gutters are still dripping.

Twilight conversion on a rain frame: usually not. A converted twilight has to light windows and darken a sky that is already flat; on a wet frame it tends to look painted. If you want a twilight, shoot a real one at dusk, rain or not.

The bungalow on Alder: a wet Saturday

A two-bed bungalow with a deep front porch, photographer booked for a Saturday with four shoots and no slack. Rain from the start. She shot the interiors first: living room, kitchen, both bedrooms and the bath, every lamp on, with the dark-stained floors looking rich under the soft light. The back garden was shot through the open kitchen door, dry under the eave, with the rain visible on the patio; it looked like a place to sit with coffee.

For the front she waited for the lightest spell, shot straight on from the pavement under an umbrella with the porch light on, and moved to the next job. That evening the agent reviewed the set. The interiors needed nothing. On the front exterior she tried a blue sky edit, compared it against the original with attention to the porch roofline, kept it as a saved version with its disclosure, and used it as the main photo with the original wet frame two slots further into the gallery. The video opened on the sky-edited front and moved inside. Nothing about the pavement, the porch or the lawn was changed, and the listing read as a house photographed on a grey day with a better sky, which is what it was.

Where rainy-day edits go wrong

Fixing everything. One edit for the sky, another for the lawn, a third to brighten the facade, and the result is a composite nobody shot. One change, disclosed, is honest; four changes is a different house.

Not comparing. A sky edit that also recoloured the roof, smoothed the brick or removed a downspout is a common failure. Put the edit next to the original and look at the edges before you choose it. How to do that quickly is in how to compare an AI-edited listing photo against the original.

Dark interiors from under-lighting. The usual rainy-day failure is not outside; it is a living room shot with the lamps off because someone assumed the window would be enough. It is not on a wet day. For the fix without flash, see fixing dark rooms in listing photos without flash.

Scheduling the second visit and never doing it. If you told the seller the exterior would be reshot on a dry day, reshoot it. A page that still leads with the rain frame three weeks on says more about the agent than the weather.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I cancel a listing shoot because of rain?

Not for the interiors. Soft light on a wet day suits most rooms, so shoot inside as planned with every lamp on. Shoot the exterior during the lightest spell or on another morning, and if you have to use a wet exterior, a sky edit with a disclosure is the only fix worth making.

Is a blue sky edit on a rainy-day exterior honest?

Yes, when the sky is the only thing that changes and the photo carries its AI-alteration disclosure. The sky is weather, not property. Keep it plain, check the roofline edge against the original, and leave the wet pavement, the lawn and the windows as they were shot.

Can an edit dry the pavement or green a wet lawn?

It can try, but the result is invented texture under a sky that does not match it, and buyers notice the mismatch. Those parts of the frame describe the property on that day. Reshoot on a dry morning if the exterior matters that much.

Can I shoot a twilight exterior in the rain?

You can, and it is often the best wet-weather exterior. With every window lit and the wet pavement reflecting the house, rain reads as atmosphere rather than gloom. It needs no edit and beats a converted twilight on a rain frame every time.
