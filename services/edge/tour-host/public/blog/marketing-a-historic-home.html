Marketing a historic home

A historic home sells on what is original and what has been updated, with dates. Shoot the details close and point buyers to the district's own guidelines.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A historic home sells on two lists: what is original and what has been updated, each with dates and sources. Shoot the details close, with a wide frame and a detail frame for every room that has something original in it, put the systems work (roof, wiring, plumbing, heating) on the page with years, and point buyers to the district's own guidelines rather than summarizing them yourself.

A historic home sells on two lists, and the marketing has to carry both. The first is what is original: floors, millwork, windows, fireplaces, hardware, tile. The second is what has been updated, with years: roof, wiring, plumbing, heating, foundation work. Shoot the details as closely as you shoot the rooms, put both lists on the page with their sources, and let the district's own guidelines speak for themselves rather than paraphrasing them. The buyer who wants an old house wants proof on both counts.

A worked example before the rules: a 1912 bungalow

A 3-bed, 1.5-bath Craftsman bungalow, built in 1912 according to the county record, inside a locally designated historic district. Original fir floors, built-in dining room cabinetry with leaded glass, a brick fireplace with the original tile surround, and the front door hardware. Roof replaced in 2021, knob-and-tube wiring removed and the panel upgraded in 2019, a new boiler in 2017, all with invoices in a folder the seller keeps. The agent books the photographer for late afternoon so the porch is in soft light, and briefs her to shoot every room twice: once wide, once on the best original detail. The set comes back as twenty-two frames; fourteen go into the listing video and all twenty-two go in the gallery. The page lists the originals in one paragraph and the updates with years in the next, then says the home sits in the district and names where the district publishes its guidelines. The main photo is the porch at 5 p.m.

How to shoot an old house

  1. Front exterior in soft light. Porches throw hard shadows at noon. Early or late, with the porch furniture arranged and the door open if the hall behind it is good.
  2. Entry hall and staircase. Wide from the door, then the newel post or balusters close.
  3. Living room wide, then the fireplace detail. Mantel, surround, tile, as close as the lens allows without distortion.
  4. Dining room wide, then built-ins or a pocket door.
  5. Kitchen, honestly. If it is updated, show it; if it is original, show it and say so. Do not split the difference with an edit.
  6. Windows and floors. One frame of a window with original glass and the sash hardware; one frame of floor grain in good light.
  7. Bedrooms, baths, a detail from each that has one. Original tile, a clawfoot tub, picture rail.
  8. Basement or attic systems. The new panel, the boiler, the dated roof invoice held up to the camera if the seller agrees. Not glamorous, and often the frames that get a buyer off the fence.
  9. Rear exterior and garden.

The listing video should rest on details more than a new build's would, which means giving it close-up frames to rest on; Rendprop plans the camera motion, so the frames you include are the control you have. A recorded voiceover that tells the house's documented story, in a minute or less, suits this property type better than music alone. For the vertical social video, shoot the three best details in portrait: door hardware, the stair, the fireplace tile.

What the page should say, and in what order

  • Year built and the source (county record, deed, historical society).
  • Style, only if you can support it. If you are not certain whether it is Queen Anne or Folk Victorian, write the year and describe the features.
  • Original features, as a list a buyer can check at the showing.
  • Updates with years and, where the seller allows, that documentation is available.
  • District status, and where the district publishes its guidelines. Do not summarize what the district allows or requires; point to it and suggest the buyer read it before an offer.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in their fields, as on any listing.

Keep adjectives down. The post on fewer adjectives applies to old houses as much as expensive ones; the details do the talking.

Where historic listings go wrong

  • An AI tidy edit that removes a radiator or straightens a window. The radiator is heat, and the wavy glass is original. Compare every edit with the original before you use it and keep the disclosure on it.
  • Virtual staging with the wrong furniture. A glass coffee table in a 1912 parlor reads as wrong even to buyers who cannot say why. If you stage, choose pieces that suit the period and keep the Virtually staged label.
  • Calling it restored when it was renovated. Restored means put back as it was; renovated means changed. Use the word that is true.
  • Noon shadows on the porch. The front of the house is the first frame; do not shoot it at the worst hour.
  • Hiding the systems. A buyer who cannot see the panel assumes the worst. Show the updates.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What matters most when marketing a historic home?

Two lists with dates and sources: what is original and what has been updated. Buyers of old houses want proof of character and proof that the systems have been looked after, and they look for both before they book a showing.

Should I say what the historic district allows?

No. Name the district, say where it publishes its guidelines, and suggest the buyer read them before making an offer. Summarizing rules you did not write is how a listing ends up wrong.

Can I virtually stage a historic home?

Yes, if the furniture suits the period and the photo carries the Virtually staged label. Compare the staged version with the original to make sure the edit has not changed trim, floors or windows.

How should the listing video differ for an old house?

Slower, with more close-ups. Give it detail frames of hardware, tile and millwork, and consider a short recorded voiceover that tells the documented story of the house instead of music alone.
