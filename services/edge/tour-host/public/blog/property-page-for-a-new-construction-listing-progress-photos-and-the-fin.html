Property page for a new build: progress photos to finished home

Publish the page early with the lot, the plan facts and the first framing photos, then replace the gallery as the home takes shape.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. For a new construction listing, publish the property page early, with the lot, the plan facts and the first clear progress photos, and keep the same link from foundation to finish. Replace the gallery at each milestone rather than stacking every stage, and lead with the most finished view you have. Buyers who find the page in week three should see a home taking shape, not a mud pit with a sign.

For a new construction listing, publish the property page as soon as there is something honest to show, usually the lot and the framing, and keep the same link until the home is finished and sold. At each milestone, replace the gallery rather than adding to it, so the page always leads with the most finished view. A buyer who finds the page in week three should see a home taking shape and a clear statement of what it will be, not a stack of mud photos with a rendering on top.

The page for a new build has a different job from a resale page. It is not showing a home; it is showing a promise and the evidence that the promise is being kept.

Four stages, one link

Stage one: lot and plan. The page carries the address or lot number, the price, the planned beds, baths and square feet (labelled as planned), and photos of the lot, the street and any completed homes of the same plan nearby. The main photo is the best of those. The enquiry form asks when the buyer wants to visit, which at this stage means a walk of the lot and a look at a model or a finished neighbour.

Stage two: framed and roofed. Replace the lot photos with exterior progress photos taken on a clear day, from the same angle each time so the change is obvious. Add one or two interior framing shots that show room sizes and window placement. Keep the price and planned facts. Buyers at this stage are choosing finishes; the page should say that the selection window is open.

Stage three: drywall and finishes. Now the interior photos matter. Kitchen with cabinets in, primary bath tiled, flooring down. Replace framing photos with finished-room photos as each one completes. Update the facts to confirmed figures if they have changed.

Stage four: finished. A full professional shoot, a listing video, the final price and facts, and the page becomes a normal listing page. The gallery from earlier stages goes; nobody buying a finished home wants to scroll past the slab.

Why the same link matters more here than on a resale

A new build is marketed for months. The link goes on the site sign in month one, in the builder's email in month two, on social every few weeks, and to the buyer who asked about it in month three and wants to check progress. If the link changes at each stage, every earlier copy breaks and the buyer who saved it in month one has nothing. One link, updated in place, means every copy out there is always current.

A worked example: Lot 14

Lot 14 in a twelve-home subdivision. The builder's plan is a 4-bed, 3-bath, 2,400 square feet, priced at $689,000 with standard finishes. In March the page goes live with the lot cleared, a shot of the street with two finished neighbours, the plan facts marked as planned, and a main photo of the finished neighbour that shares the plan, labelled as such. In May, framing is up: replace the lot photos with framed exterior and three interior framing shots. In July, drywall: kitchen with cabinets, primary bath, the view from the great room windows. In September, finished: a 30-photo shoot, a listing video, confirmed square footage, and the page reads like any other 4-bed for sale.

The buyer who found the page in April and asked about finishes is the same buyer who comes back in September, opens the same link, sees the finished home, and fills in the form for the first weekend.

Mistakes that stall a new-build page

  • Leading with a rendering and never updating. Buyers assume the project has stalled. Progress photos are the proof it has not.
  • Keeping every stage in the gallery. By stage three the buyer is scrolling past forty photos of framing to find the kitchen. Replace, do not stack.
  • Unlabelled planned figures. If the square footage says 2,400 and the finished home measures 2,350, a buyer who was not told the first number was planned feels misled. Label it.
  • Using a neighbour's finished home as the main photo without saying so. Fine as a reference, labelled. Misleading as the hero.
  • A different link at each stage. The sign, the emails and the social posts all break.
  • AI edits that invent finishes. Adding furniture to a framed room or generating a finished kitchen from a drywall photo creates a picture of a home that does not exist yet. If you stage an empty finished room, label it as virtually staged. Do not generate stages the build has not reached.

Before each update

Open the page on your phone after each milestone. Check the main photo is the most finished view, the facts are current and labelled, the gallery has no stale stage, and the form still works. Then send the same link again, with a one-line note: "Lot 14, drywall in, kitchen going in next week."

FAQ

Questions on this topic

When should I publish the page for a new construction listing?

As soon as there is something honest to show, usually the lot with the plan facts and a photo of a finished home on the same plan, labelled as a reference. Waiting for the finished shoot means months of marketing without a page.

Should I keep the progress photos in the gallery once the home is finished?

No. Replace them with the finished shoot. A buyer of a finished home does not want to scroll past the slab and the framing. Keep the progress photos in your own files for the builder's portfolio.

Can I use AI to show what a framed room will look like finished?

It is risky. A generated finish is a picture of a home that does not exist yet, and buyers remember what they saw. If you stage an empty finished room, label it as virtually staged. Do not generate a stage the build has not reached.

What if the final square footage differs from the plan?

Label the early figure as planned on the page from day one, then update to the confirmed figure when it is measured. A buyer who knew the first number was a plan accepts the change; one who did not feels misled.
