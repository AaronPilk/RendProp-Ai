Marketing a fixer-upper honestly

Sell a fixer-upper on the bones, the lot and the price, with photos that show the real condition. Skip edits that hide defects and label any staging.

Sell the bones, show the condition

A fixer-upper has two audiences: the investor who is pricing a renovation and the owner-occupier who wants a house they could not otherwise afford on that street. Both of them are doing arithmetic. The investor needs to know the structure, the roof, the systems and the lot. The owner-occupier needs to know what works today and what does not. Neither is helped by a listing that pretends the kitchen is fine.

So the copy leads with the fixed assets: the lot size, the location, the square feet, the layout, the year built, and anything structural that is sound. Then it states the condition plainly: the kitchen and bathrooms are original, the roof is near the end of its life, the furnace works and is old. Then the price, which is the whole argument.

What to photograph in a fixer-upper

  • The exterior from the street, honestly. Peeling paint and all. Shoot it in good light; a tired house in soft morning light reads as a project, and the same house at noon reads as a problem.
  • The lot. From the back corner looking at the house, and from the house looking out. For a fixer-upper the lot is often the most valuable thing in the frame.
  • Every room, lit, as it is. Lamps on, blinds open, surfaces cleared of debris but not dressed. The buyer needs to see the room, including the water stain on the ceiling.
  • The structure and systems. The basement or crawl space, the panel, the furnace, the roof from the ground, the foundation where it is visible. Investors look for these frames and trust a listing that includes them.
  • Anything original and good. Hardwood under the corner of a lifted carpet, original trim, a brick fireplace. These are what the owner-occupier is buying.

Edits to skip on a fixer

The question for every edit on a fixer-upper is whether it changes what the buyer will find. On this kind of listing, most of them do.

Skip: tidy the room on debris or damage, because the edit has to invent the floor or wall underneath and the buyer will see the real one; green lawn on a yard that is dirt; anything that smooths a cracked wall or brightens a stained ceiling. These are not enhancements, they are alterations of condition, and the line between the two is drawn in the difference between enhancement and alteration.

Acceptable with care: a plain blue sky over a flat grey one, disclosed, because the sky is weather; virtual staging of one or two empty rooms to show scale, labelled Virtually staged, with the empty original beside it in the gallery. The staging shows the room's size and shape; it does not repair anything.

The two-bed on Mill Street, as-is

A 1940s two-bed on a deep lot two blocks from a popular main street, owned by the same family for decades, now sold as-is by the heirs. Original kitchen, one bathroom with a cracked floor, a roof the home inspector had called tired, and oak floors under worn carpet in every room. Price set below anything else on the street by a wide margin.

The shoot was at nine on a clear morning. Exterior from the street with the peeling porch paint in soft light; the lot from the back fence showing the depth and the mature trees. Inside, every room lit and cleared of boxes but otherwise untouched, including the kitchen and the cracked bathroom floor. The agent lifted a corner of the living room carpet and the photographer shot the oak underneath. Basement, panel, furnace and the roof edge from the garden completed the set. One edit was used: a blue sky on the front exterior, compared with the original and disclosed. The living room was virtually staged in one labelled frame to show that the room took a full sofa and dining table, with the empty original beside it.

The video walked the house in order with a recorded voiceover that said what was original, what was worn and what was sound, and spent its last ten seconds on the lot. Nine showings in the first week, five of them investors and four owner-occupiers who had read the condition and come anyway. Three offers, no inspection surprises, because nothing was a surprise. Where fixer-upper listings go wrong

Soft-focus photos of hard problems. Dim rooms, tight crops that hide the damage, no basement frame.

Copy that argues. Cosmetic only, needs TLC, bring your vision. Say what is wrong and what is right, and let the price make the argument.

Staging as repair. A staged room that also fixed the wall is a misrepresentation, and the label does not cover it.

Pricing it like a finished house with a discount. The buyer is adding the renovation cost to your price and comparing the total with the renovated house down the street. Price for that arithmetic.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should fixer-upper photos show the damage?

Yes, in good light. Buyers for a project want to see the real condition, including the basement, the systems and the worn rooms, and a listing that hides them loses the serious ones. Shoot every room lit and cleared of debris, but not dressed.

Can I use AI edits on a fixer-upper listing?

Only edits that do not change condition: a plain disclosed sky replacement, or labelled virtual staging of an empty room to show scale with the empty original beside it. Skip anything that removes damage, smooths walls or greens a dirt yard, because those alter what the buyer will find.

What should the copy for a fixer-upper lead with?

The things that cannot be changed and are being bought: lot size, location, square feet, layout, year built and anything structurally sound. Then plain statements of condition, then the price. Avoid phrases that argue, like cosmetic only or needs TLC.

Who are the buyers for a fixer-upper and how do I reach both?

Investors pricing a renovation and owner-occupiers who want the street at a price they can reach. Both do arithmetic, so give them the inputs: structure and systems frames for the investor, original features and the lot for the owner-occupier, and honest condition for both.
