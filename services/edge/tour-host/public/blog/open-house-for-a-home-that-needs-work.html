Open house for a home that needs work

Run it for the buyer who is coming anyway: honest photos, a video of the whole house, paperwork on the table, problem rooms open rather than hidden.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. An open house for a home that needs work is for the buyer who is coming anyway: the investor, the contractor, the couple who want the street and can live through a renovation. Market it to them with honest photos, a video that shows every room including the 1978 kitchen, a page that lists what has been done and what has not, and a Sunday where the problem rooms are open, lit and documented rather than hidden.

An open house for a home that needs work is not an attempt to hide the work. It is for the buyers who are coming because of it: the investor pricing a flip, the contractor who can see past the kitchen, the couple who want this street and can live through a renovation. Market it to them with honest photos, a video that shows every room, a page that says what has been done and what has not, and a Sunday where the problem rooms are open and lit, with the paperwork on the table.

The temptation is to tidy the photos until the house looks like something it is not, draw a crowd, and watch them leave disappointed. The crowd is not the goal. The right six people are.

Cherry Street, Sunday 12 to 2

A 1950s 3-bed on Cherry Street. Original kitchen, original bathroom, a roof replaced last year, new electrics three years ago, a damp patch in the basement corner that was addressed but still shows. Priced below the street's recent sales to reflect the kitchen and bath.

The page, before Sunday. Photos of every room as they are, in good light, clean, with nothing removed by software. The kitchen is the kitchen. The video walks the whole house in order with a voiceover that says, plainly: “the kitchen and bathroom are original; the roof is one year old, the electrics three; the basement had water in the corner, the gutter that caused it was fixed, the invoice will be on the table.” The description lists what has been done, with years, and what has not. The price sits in the details and makes sense next to the list.

The house on Sunday. Clean, lit, warm. The basement light is on and the stairs are clear, because the basement is where the decision gets made. The kitchen cupboards are empty and open so people can see the carcasses. On the dining table: the roof invoice, the electrical certificate, the gutter repair receipt, a permit history, and a one-page list matching the one on the page. Nothing is hidden behind a strategically placed plant.

What you say. “The price reflects the kitchen and the bath. Everything structural and expensive is recent, and the paperwork is on the table.” Then you let them look. The contractor goes straight to the basement. The couple stands in the kitchen and starts measuring for the one they will put in.

Eleven groups through. Seven leave quickly; they wanted a finished house and the page told them it was not, but they came anyway. Four stay. Two ask for second showings with their own builder. That is a good Sunday for this house.

What goes on the page for a house like this

  • Honest photos. Clean and well lit is fair. Removing the stain, the crack or the dated cupboards is not. If you use any edit at all, it should be labelled, and a labelled edit on a house that needs work tends to raise more questions than it answers.
  • Staging, if any, labelled. A virtually staged living room can help a buyer see the shape of an empty, dated room; the label and a mention at the door keep it honest.
  • The done-and-not-done list. With years. Roof, electrics, boiler, windows, drainage. Then the kitchen, bath, flooring and whatever else is original. Two short lists do more than any paragraph.
  • The video, whole house. Including the worst room. The buyer who watches it and still comes is your buyer.
  • The price, in context. The details show it; the description can say in one sentence what it reflects.

The paperwork table

For a house that needs work, documents are the staging. A roof invoice does more for a contractor than a vase of flowers. Put the originals or clean copies on the dining table with the one-page list on top, and say they are there. Buyers who are doing sums want dates and amounts, and a seller who can produce them is a seller who is easier to trust about everything else.

Where this goes wrong

  • An edit removed what Sunday shows. The stain that was taken out of the basement photo is on the wall at 12:30. The buyer now doubts the roof invoice too.
  • The basement light is off. It reads as hiding. Light it, clear the stairs, let them go down alone.
  • Over-cleaning out the character. Original floors, a fireplace, the proportions: show them. A house that needs work usually has something a new build does not.
  • Defensive about the price. You priced it for the work. Say so once and let the list argue.
  • No list. Buyers assume the worst about anything not stated. The done list is what stops “what else is wrong” from becoming the only question.
  • Drawing the wrong crowd. A page that shows a flattering kitchen angle and no mention of the work brings buyers who want finished and leave within four minutes, and tells the seller nothing useful.

Write the two lists first

Before the shoot, sit with the seller and write what has been done, with years and invoices, and what has not. Put both on the page, in the voiceover and on the dining table. FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I hold an open house for a home that needs a lot of work?

Yes, aimed at the buyers who come because of the work: investors, contractors and people who want the location and can renovate. Market it to them with honest photos, a video that shows every room and a page that lists what has been done and what has not, then let them inspect the problem rooms with the lights on and the paperwork out.

Can I use AI edits on photos of a dated or damaged home?

Cleaning and lighting the room before you shoot is fair. Removing a stain, a crack or dated cupboards with an edit is not, because the buyer will see them on Sunday and then doubt everything else, including the roof invoice. If you stage an empty room virtually, label it and mention it at the door.

What documents should be on the table at the open house?

Invoices and certificates for the expensive recent work: roof, electrics, boiler, drainage, windows, with dates and amounts, plus any permit history and a one-page list that matches the page. For a house that needs work, paperwork is the staging. Contractors and investors read it before they look at the kitchen.

How do I talk about the price at an open house for a fixer?

Once, plainly: the price reflects the kitchen and the bathroom, the structural and expensive items are recent, and the paperwork is on the table. Then stop. The done-and-not-done list does the arguing, and buyers who are doing sums respond to dates and amounts rather than persuasion.
