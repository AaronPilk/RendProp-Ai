Marketing a listing with tenants in place

Tenants did not ask to be marketed. Give proper notice, do one shoot, let the video replace the first wave of showings, and keep their things off the page.

Market a tenant-occupied listing around one well-planned shoot, arranged with whatever notice your lease and local rules call for, so the tenant is disturbed once rather than every Saturday for a month. Then let the listing video and the property page handle the first wave of interest, so only buyers who have already seen the house on a phone come to see it in person. Keep the tenants' belongings, names and routines out of the photos and the copy. Their cooperation is the asset; the plan should protect it.

The tenant did not ask for this

The seller wants to sell. The buyer wants to see the house. The tenant wants to be left alone, and in most cases is entitled to a fair amount of that.

Start by reading the lease, asking the seller what it says about access and sale, and following the notice period it and your local rules set. Do not state a notice period from memory or from another state; ask the seller's attorney or your broker if the lease is unclear. Then meet the tenant, in person if you can, and tell them exactly what will happen: one shoot on an agreed morning, then showings in agreed windows, and a lot less of both than they fear.

One shoot, one notice, fewer showings

  1. Agree the shoot date with the tenant first, then the photographer. A tenant who picked the morning will have the place ready. One who was told the morning will not.
  2. Give them a short prep list and offer help. Clear surfaces, make the beds, put personal photos and mail away, and, if they are willing, let the photographer open the blinds. Offer a cleaner the day before at the seller's expense; it is cheaper than a second shoot.
  3. Shoot everything in one visit. Landscape photos of each room for the listing video, portrait frames of the kitchen, the living room and the exterior for the social cut, and the exterior from the street.
  4. Build the video and the page from that one set. The video is what lets a buyer decide whether to visit at all. A listing with a video on the page gets fewer curious showings and more serious ones, which is exactly what the tenant needs. The logic is laid out in listing video as a pre-showing.
  5. Set showing windows and keep them. Two windows a week, agreed with the tenant, with the notice the lease requires before each one. Group buyers into those windows.

What goes on the page and what stays off

The page carries the facts about the property: price, beds, baths, square feet, the building and the lot. Anything about the tenancy, such as the lease end date, the rent and whether the tenant intends to stay, belongs in the MLS remarks and in the conversation with buyers, in whatever form your broker advises, not in a public page any neighbour can open.

The photos should show rooms, not a household. Names on mail, children's drawings with names, prescription bottles, a work badge on the hook, a tenant's face in a mirror: all of it comes out before the shoot or the frame is reshot. If a surface cannot be cleared, reframe rather than relying on an edit; a tidy the room pass that removes a tenant's belongings is guessing at what is behind them and can invent details the buyer will not find.

The duplex on Second Avenue: one Saturday

A side-by-side duplex, both units tenanted, one tenant cooperative and one annoyed. The seller, a landlord selling to another investor, wanted it listed fast. The agent read both leases, confirmed the notice period with the seller's attorney, and gave written notice to both tenants for a single Saturday morning two weeks out.

She visited both tenants in person that week. The cooperative one asked for a cleaner, which the seller paid for. The annoyed one wanted to know how many showings there would be; the honest answer was that with a video on the page most buyers would never visit, and showings would be grouped into Wednesday evening and Saturday morning windows with notice before each. He agreed to the shoot on that basis.

Saturday at nine the photographer shot unit A, then unit B, then the exterior, the shared yard and the basement laundry, in ninety minutes. Both tenants had cleared surfaces and put personal items away. The video walked through unit A in full and showed unit B's main rooms, and the page carried the property facts only; the rent roll went to buyers who asked, through the seller's broker. Three investors watched the video, two asked for the numbers, one came to the Wednesday window and made the offer. The annoyed tenant was disturbed exactly twice. Where tenant-occupied marketing goes wrong

Showing up with a photographer and a text from last night. The lease and local rules decide the notice, and the tenant decides whether to cooperate. Skip the first and you lose the second.

Publishing the tenancy details. Rent and lease dates on a public page tell every neighbour what the tenant pays. Keep them in the channels your broker advises.

Treating every enquiry as a showing. The video exists so you can qualify first. Send the link, ask what they want to see, then book the window.

Editing the tenant away. A belonging removed by an edit is a guess about the wall behind it. Clear it or reframe it.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How much notice do I need to give a tenant before a photo shoot or showing?

Whatever the lease and your local rules set, which varies. Read the lease, ask the seller's attorney or your broker if it is unclear, and give written notice in that form. Agreeing the shoot date with the tenant before booking the photographer avoids most of the friction.

Should the rent and lease dates go on the property page?

No. A public page can be opened by anyone, including neighbours. Property facts go on the page; tenancy details go in the MLS remarks and to buyers who ask, in whatever form your broker advises.

How do I keep showings to a minimum for the tenant?

Put a listing video on the page so buyers can tour from a phone first, then group in-person visits into two agreed windows a week with proper notice before each. Most casual buyers never visit, and the serious ones fit the windows.

Can I use an AI edit to remove the tenant's belongings from the photos?

Better not. The edit has to guess what is behind the belongings and can invent details the buyer will not find. Ask the tenant to clear surfaces and put personal items away, offer a cleaner at the seller's expense, and reframe any shot that still shows something private.
