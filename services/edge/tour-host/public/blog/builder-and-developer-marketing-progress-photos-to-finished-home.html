Builder marketing: from progress photos to the finished home

Market each new home on one link from slab to handover, refreshing the photos and video at every stage, so early and late buyers end up at the same page.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A builder or developer should market each home on a single link that lives from the first slab to the handover, refreshing the photos and the video as the build progresses. Buyers who found the page at framing come back to the same link at finish, and the enquiries from every stage land in one place.

Market each new home on one link from the day the slab is poured to the day the keys are handed over, and update that same page as the build moves. Progress photos at framing, lock-up and fit-out, then the finished photos and a proper listing video at the end. Buyers who found the page early come back to the same link, and the enquiries from every stage arrive in one inbox.

The alternative, a new post and a new link every month, loses the people you already had.

Why one link beats a monthly post

A spec home takes eight to twelve months. Over that time the buyer pool changes: early interest comes from people planning ahead, late interest from people who need to move soon. If each stage goes out as a separate post or a separate brochure, the early buyers lose track and the late buyers see only the finished shots with no sense of what is behind the walls. One page that grows with the build keeps both groups, and gives a buyer at the end a record of the construction that no finished-home listing can offer.

What to shoot at each stage

  1. Site and slab. The lot from the street, the slab or foundation, the view from where the living room will be. Two or three photos. The main photo at this stage is the lot with the best angle on whatever the view will be.
  2. Framing. The frame from the street, the inside of the main living space looking toward the windows, the upstairs view if there is one. People like seeing the bones.
  3. Lock-up. Roof on, windows in, wrap on. The exterior from the street and the corner. The interior now reads as rooms.
  4. Fit-out. Drywall, kitchen cabinets going in, tiling. One photo of the kitchen as it becomes a kitchen is the most shared progress photo there is.
  5. Finished. The full set: exterior front and angle, every room, the yard. This is when the listing video gets made and the main photo changes to the finished front.

Shoot each stage on a clear day, mid-morning, from the same two or three positions. Repeating the position makes the progress visible; the same corner at slab, frame, lock-up and finish tells the story on its own.

Worked example: a four-bedroom spec home on a corner lot

A builder starts a four-bed on a corner lot in March with a planned handover in November. In March the page goes up with three photos, the lot and the slab, and a short note typed in with the beds, baths and square feet from the plans. Buyers who drive past and scan the sign on the fence land on that page. In May the framing photos replace the main photo and the gallery grows. In August the lock-up exterior becomes the main photo. In October the kitchen fit-out photos go up and the first enquiries asking about the price arrive. In November the finished set replaces the progress set as the gallery lead, the listing video is created from the finished landscape photos, and a vertical cut goes to the builder's social accounts. The same link has been on the fence sign for eight months. The buyer who scanned it in April and the buyer who saw the reel in November both end up at the same form.

The details on the page

Beds, baths and square feet from the plans go in on day one. Price goes in when it is set; a page without a price takes enquiries and that is fine early on. When the price is set, type it. When the home sells, mark it so the page says so rather than taking new enquiries, and keep the link alive; it still shows your work to the next buyer who scans the sign on the lot next door.

Progress photos that hurt

  • Mess in frame. A site is a site, but a frame full of offcuts and a skip reads as careless. Move the skip or move your feet.
  • Rain-day shoots. Mud and grey sky make every stage look stalled. Wait for the dry day.
  • Renderings mixed with photos without a label. A rendering used as the main photo next to progress shots confuses the buyer about what exists. If you show the rendering, say it is one.
  • AI edits on progress shots. Green lawn on a lot that is still mud, or furniture in a room that is still studs, tells the buyer something that is not there yet. Keep AI edits for the finished set, keep the disclosure, and compare each one against the original.
  • A new link every stage. You lose the people who saved the old one.

Start at the slab

For the next home you break ground on, put the page up the week of the slab with three photos and the plan details, and print that link on the fence sign. Add photos at each stage from the same positions, and make the listing video the week the house is finished.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a builder post progress photos or wait for the finished home?

Post progress photos on the same page the finished home will use. Early buyers follow the build, late buyers get a record of what is behind the walls, and everyone ends up at the same link and form.

When should the listing video be made?

At finish, from the finished landscape photos. A video from progress shots reads as a construction diary rather than a home. Short social clips from the fit-out stage are fine for the builder's own accounts.

Can I use AI edits on progress photos?

Better not. A green lawn on a mud lot or furniture in a room that is still studs shows the buyer something that does not exist yet. Save edits for the finished set, keep the disclosure, and compare each edit with the original.

What happens to the page when the home sells?

Mark it sold and the page says so. Keep the link alive; it still shows the quality of your work to the next buyer who scans a sign on your next lot.
