LinkedIn for commercial and investment listings

LinkedIn suits listings where the buyer is a business or an investor. Lead with the figures they ask for, add the video and the page link.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. LinkedIn is the right channel when the buyer of the listing is a business, an investor or someone acting for one. Lead the post with the figures that kind of buyer asks for first, add the video and the property page link, and write in the tone of a person reporting to a colleague rather than selling to a family.

LinkedIn is the channel for a listing when the buyer is a business, an investor, or a person deciding on behalf of one: a six-unit building, an office suite, a warehouse bay, a mixed-use corner. The post leads with the figures that buyer asks for first, carries the video and the property page link, and reads like a colleague reporting a deal rather than an agent selling a dream. Homes rarely belong here; income property and space for a business do.

Who is reading and what they want first

The person who stops on a commercial or investment post is not browsing for a feeling. They are checking whether the numbers are worth a call. So the first line is a number, the second line is the asset, and the third line is the condition of the thing that makes the number real.

For an investment property, that usually means the asking price, the unit count or square footage, and the current income position stated at the level the seller has agreed to share. For a space to lease or buy for a business, it means the size, the location in terms a tenant uses (loading access, parking count, zoning, frontage), and availability.

Do not publish figures the seller has not approved for public release, and do not publish anything that identifies current tenants or their rent. Ask the seller and your broker what can go in the post and what waits for the signed confidentiality agreement.

A six-unit building, posted properly

Take a six-unit brick building on a corner lot near a hospital, fully occupied, with separately metered utilities and a new roof.

The post: Six units, fully occupied, new roof in 2025, separately metered. Corner lot two blocks from the regional hospital, which is the tenant base. Asking $1,150,000. Rent roll and operating statement available on request after a signed NDA. Video and photos at the link.

Then the vertical video or the landscape video, and the branded property page link. That is the whole post. No adjectives, no story about the neighborhood's charm, no description of the kind of person who should buy it. An investor reads it in eight seconds and either messages or does not.

The video for this listing opens on the exterior from the corner, because the corner is the location argument, then shows one representative unit, the mechanical room, and the roof line. The script reads the same figures the post states. Nothing in the video should say something the post does not, and nothing in the post should say something the seller has not cleared.

An office suite is a different post

For a 2,400-square-foot suite on the third floor of a mid-sized building, the buyer is a business owner or an office manager, and the post answers the questions they would ask the leasing agent on the phone. Size, floor, how many private offices and how many open desks, parking, the nearest highway exit, available from when, and whether the furniture stays. The video shows the reception, the conference room, the view, the corridor and the parking, in that order, because that is the order a visitor sees them.

A suite does not need a hook in the first three seconds the way a home reel does. It needs the facts on screen from the first frame and a steady pace. See a page for the suite, not the building for what the property page should carry.

Where LinkedIn listing posts go wrong

  • Posting like Instagram. Pictograms, hashtag walls and a story about the morning light. The reader came for figures.
  • No numbers. A commercial post without a price, a size or an income figure is read as a post with something to hide.
  • Publishing what the seller did not clear. Tenant names, individual rents, a lease expiry that gives a competitor an opening. Confirm every figure with the seller before it goes public.
  • Tagging people to force reach. Tagging twelve investors who did not ask is a fast way to be muted by all twelve.
  • Linking to a PDF. A brochure attachment is fine as a follow-up. The post should link to a page that opens on a phone and has a form.
  • Residential homes. A three-bed in a cul-de-sac is a stretch here unless it is being sold as a rental investment, and then the post should say so with the numbers.

Get the three lines approved

For your current commercial or investment listing, write the three-line opening: the number, the asset, the condition that makes the number real. Get the seller to approve those three lines in writing. Then build the rest of the post around them.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should residential listings go on LinkedIn?

Rarely. LinkedIn works when the buyer is a business or an investor. A home sold as a rental investment can work if the post leads with the income figures. A family home in a cul-de-sac belongs on channels where families browse.

What figures should a commercial listing post include?

The asking price or rent, the size or unit count, and the one condition that makes the number real: occupancy, a new roof, zoning, parking. Only publish figures the seller has approved, and keep tenant names and individual rents for the signed confidentiality agreement.

Which video format works best for a commercial listing on LinkedIn?

Either the 16:9 video or the vertical one can work; test both with your own account. What matters more is that the facts are on screen from the first frame and the script reads the same figures the post states.

Should I tag potential buyers in a listing post?

Only people who asked to be told about listings like this one. Tagging a list of investors who did not ask is treated as spam and gets you muted. Send the link privately to the people you actually know.
