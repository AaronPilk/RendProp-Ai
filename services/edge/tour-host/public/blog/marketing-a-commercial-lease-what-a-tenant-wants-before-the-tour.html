Marketing a commercial lease: what tenants want before the tour

Before a tour, a tenant wants the size, the floor, the availability date, true photos of the space and the loading door. Put those on one page per unit.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Before a tenant books a tour, they want five things: the size, the floor or unit, the date it is available, honest photos of the space as it is now, and the practical access such as parking, loading and power. Give each unit its own page with a short video and those details typed in, and the tour request arrives with the questions already answered.

A tenant deciding whether to tour a commercial space wants five answers first: how big it is, which floor or unit, when it is available, what it looks like right now rather than in a rendering, and the practical access such as parking, loading, ceiling height and power. Give each unit its own page with a short video, those details typed in, and a form that asks when they want to come. The tour request that follows is from someone who already knows the space fits.

The tenant has a brief, not a wish list

A tenant rep working for a dental group has a brief: 1,800 to 2,200 square feet, ground floor, parking for staff, available within ninety days. She is not browsing. She is filtering. Every listing that makes her hunt for the square footage or guess at the availability date costs her time and drops down the list. The listings that state those numbers plainly, with photos that match what she will see on the tour, are the ones she books.

That is the standard for the page: the five answers in the first screen, photos that are true, and one way to request a tour.

Photograph the space as it is

Commercial marketing leans on renderings and old fit-out photos. Tenants have learned to distrust both. Shoot the space in its current condition. If it is in shell, shoot the shell with the lights on and say so. If the previous tenant's fit-out is still there, shoot it and say what stays. A tenant who tours a space that matches the photos trusts the rest of the deal.

  • Exterior and signage. The frontage from across the street, with the unit's door visible.
  • The main space, wide, from the entrance. Lights on, blinds up.
  • The main space from the far end looking back. Shows depth, columns and the ceiling.
  • Ceiling height and services. A frame that shows the grid, the ducting or the clear height; tenants ask.
  • Back of house. Loading door, storage, restrooms, the electrical panel if it is a selling point.
  • Parking. One photo of the lot or the street, with the unit in the background.

Leave AI staging off commercial photos. Adding furniture to a shell space shows a tenant a fit-out they are not getting. If a tidy edit is used to clear a previous tenant's rubbish, keep the disclosure and compare it against the original.

Worked example: a 2,000 square foot ground-floor unit

A broker has a ground-floor unit in a strip of six, formerly a physiotherapy clinic, with the partition walls still in place and parking for twelve in front. He shoots eight landscape photos at 10am: the frontage, the reception area, the main room from the door, the same room from the back, two of the treatment rooms with doors open, the rear corridor with the loading door, and the parking from the kerb. The listing video runs forty seconds from the frontage to the loading door, with a voiceover giving the square footage, the availability date, the fact that the partitions can stay or go, and the parking count. The page shows the details typed in and the gallery; the enquiry form asks for the date the visitor wants to tour. The dental group's rep books Thursday and arrives knowing the partition layout already.

One page per unit, one link per prospect

A building with four vacancies gets four pages. The rep looking for 2,000 square feet gets the link for the 2,000 square foot unit, not a brochure with all four. If your listing service wants a plain tour link without your branding, the unbranded link is for that; check the current rules of the service you post to. Enquiries from all four units land in one place so nothing is missed on a busy week.

Where lease listings lose the tenant

  • Square footage buried in a PDF. The first thing they look for. Type it into the details and say it in the voiceover.
  • No availability date. Second thing they look for. If it is negotiable, say the earliest date.
  • Renderings instead of photos. They assume the worst about what the rendering is hiding.
  • No back-of-house shots. Loading, storage and restrooms decide more leases than the frontage.
  • One video for the whole building. The tenant wants their unit, not the tour of the others.
  • A contact page three clicks away. The form that asks for the tour date should be on the unit's page.

Start with the slowest vacancy

Take the vacancy that has had the fewest tours, shoot it as it is with the lights on, and build its own page with the five answers typed in. Send that link to the three reps whose briefs it matches and compare the response with the brochure.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What does a tenant want to know before touring a commercial space?

Size, floor or unit, availability date, what the space looks like now, and practical access such as parking, loading, ceiling height and power. If those five are on the page, the tour request comes from someone who already knows it fits.

Should I use renderings or old fit-out photos?

No. Shoot the space in its current condition with the lights on and say what it is: shell, or the previous fit-out with a note on what stays. Tenants distrust renderings and trust a listing whose photos match the tour.

Should each unit have its own page?

Yes. A tenant rep with a brief for 2,000 square feet wants the 2,000 square foot unit, not a brochure of four. One page per unit means one link per prospect and no hunting.

What is the unbranded link for on a commercial listing?

It is a version of the page with no name, no contact details and no Rendprop branding, for listing services that want a plain tour link. Check the current rules of whichever service you post to before using it.
