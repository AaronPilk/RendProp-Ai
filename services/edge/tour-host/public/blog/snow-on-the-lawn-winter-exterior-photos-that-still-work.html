Snow on the lawn: winter exterior photos that still work

Fresh snow photographs well; old grey snow does not. Shoot the morning after a snowfall, clear the walk and drive, and do not edit the snow into a lawn.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Snow on the lawn is fine in a listing photo when it is fresh, the walk and the driveway are cleared, and the house is lit. Old grey snow, slush and a half-shovelled path are the problem. Shoot the morning after a snowfall or at twilight, and never replace snow with a green lawn in an edit; it describes a yard the buyer will not find.

Snow on the lawn works in a listing photo when it is fresh, the walk and the driveway are cleared, and the house is lit against it. What does not work is week-old grey snow, slush at the kerb and a path shovelled halfway. Shoot the morning after a snowfall or at twilight, and do not ask an edit to turn the snow into a green lawn: it shows a yard the buyer will not find until April, and it carries a disclosure that invites the question of what else was changed.

Fresh snow helps, old snow hurts

Clean snow does three useful things. It covers a dormant lawn and bare beds. It reflects light up into the facade, so a north-facing front that is normally in shadow reads brighter. And it reads as a season, not a condition; a buyer looking at a snow-covered colonial in January understands exactly what they are seeing.

Old snow does the opposite. It goes grey along the street, it piles in dirty ridges where the plow went past, and it makes the whole frame look like the house has been sitting. If the only snow available is old snow, the twilight shot is your friend: in low light the grey disappears and the lit windows take over.

A shot list for a snowy exterior

  1. Front, straight on, from across the street. The morning after the snowfall, before cars have driven through the drive. Sun behind or beside the camera. The snow on the roof is fine; the snow on the walk is not.
  2. Front, angled, from the cleared walk. Shows the depth of the house and the cleared path to the door, which is the detail buyers want to see: that someone can get in.
  3. Back of the house from the yard. Snow makes the yard look bigger because the eye has nothing to stop on. If there is a deck, clear it, because a deck under a foot of snow does not read as a deck.
  4. A detail that says winter is handled. The garage interior with the snow blower, or the mudroom with the boot tray. These are not decorative; they answer a buyer's practical question.
  5. Twilight. Every window lit, porch light on, the snow glowing blue. This is frequently the best exterior a northern house will ever have and it needs no edit at all.

What to clear and what to leave

Clear the walk edge to edge, the full driveway, the front steps and the deck. Clear the cars out of the driveway entirely. Knock the snow off the shrubs by the door so they do not look crushed. Leave the lawn alone; a lawn with footprints across it looks worse than untouched snow, so ask the sellers and the photographer to stay on the cleared paths.

If the snow has been on the ground long enough to be dirty, ask the sellers to shovel a fresh edge along the walk and the drive the morning of the shoot. A crisp line between cleared pavement and white snow does a lot of work even when the snow itself is a few days old.

The colonial on Birchwood: Thursday morning

A four-bed brick colonial, north-facing front, listing planned for the following Tuesday. Snow was forecast for Wednesday night. The photographer was booked for Thursday at nine with the agreement that if the snow did not come, they would shoot at twilight instead.

It came. The sellers shovelled the walk, the steps and the full drive before eight and moved both cars to the street two doors down. The photographer shot the front from across the road with the low morning sun lighting the snow and bouncing it up onto the brick, which is the best that north-facing front had ever looked. He shot the back from the cleared deck, the mudroom with the boot tray, then went inside for the landscape photos of every room while the sellers kept the lamps on. At four he came back for the twilight: lamps in every front room, porch light on, the drive still clear. The twilight became the main photo and the morning front went second. Nothing was edited. The listing went live Tuesday, snow still on the lawn, and the first buyer through on Saturday said the house looked exactly like the pictures.

Edits that go wrong on snow

Green lawn over snow. The worst version of the green lawn edit. It does not just brighten a dormant lawn; it removes a season, and the AI has to invent the lawn edge, the beds and whatever was under the snow. The result rarely survives comparison with the original, and if it did, it would show a yard that does not exist until spring. The honest use of that edit is covered in green lawn edits: fixing a brown yard honestly.

Blue sky over a snow day. Snow days are often overcast, and the flat white sky bleeds into the white lawn. A blue sky edit here can be defensible with its disclosure, but check the edge where the sky meets the snow-covered roof, because that is where the edit tends to smear.

Removing the snow pile. A tidy the room style edit on the plow ridge at the kerb invents pavement and lawn that were never photographed. Shovel it or reframe the shot.

Twilight conversion when a real one was available. In winter the real one is an hour's wait. Take the wait.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Is it a problem to have snow in listing photos?

Not if the snow is fresh and the walk, steps and driveway are cleared. Clean snow covers a dormant lawn, bounces light onto the house and reads as a season rather than a condition. Old grey snow, slush and a half-cleared path are what buyers read as neglect.

Can I edit the snow out and show a green lawn?

You can, but you should not. The edit has to invent the lawn, the beds and everything under the snow, it carries a disclosure, and it shows the buyer a yard they will not find for months. Shoot at twilight instead, where the lawn is not the subject.

What should the sellers clear before a snow-day shoot?

The walk edge to edge, the full driveway, the front steps and the deck, and both cars out of the drive entirely. Leave the lawn untouched so there are no footprints across it. If the snow is a few days old, shovel a fresh crisp edge along the paths the morning of the shoot.

What time of day is best for a snowy exterior?

The morning after the snowfall, with low sun lighting the snow and bouncing it onto the house, or twilight with every window lit. Twilight is often the stronger of the two in winter and hides old snow that would look grey at midday.
