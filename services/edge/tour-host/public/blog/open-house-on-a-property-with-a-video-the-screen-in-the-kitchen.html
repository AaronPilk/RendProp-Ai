Open house with a video: the screen in the kitchen

Put a screen on the kitchen island with the property page open and the video ready to play on request. Not looping, not loud. People stop in the kitchen.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. At an open house, put a laptop or tablet on the kitchen island with the property page open and the listing video a tap away. Do not loop it with music; let visitors play it when they want to see the rooms they have just walked through in order, or the garden in summer light. The kitchen is where groups stop and talk, so that is where the screen earns its place.

Put a screen in the kitchen. A laptop or a tablet on the island, the property page open, the listing video one tap from playing. Not looping. Not with music filling the room. The kitchen is where visitors stop, lean on the counter and talk to each other, and that is where a two-minute video of the house they are standing in gets watched.

It sounds redundant to show a video of the house inside the house. It is not. The video shows the rooms in order, in the best light the house gets, with the garden in the season it was shot. The visitor who walked through in November drizzle gets to see the deck in September. And the page under the video has the price, the square footage and the form for booking a second visit, which is what the kitchen conversation usually turns into.

The ranch on Cedar Lane, Sunday 1 to 3

A 1960s ranch on Cedar Lane with a long back garden and a kitchen that opens to the dining room. Open house Sunday 1 to 3 in late November, grey outside. A laptop on the island, plugged in, screen brightness up, the property page showing the main photo and the details, the video below it.

A couple comes through. They do the loop: living room, bedrooms, bathroom, back to the kitchen. In the kitchen they stop because the island invites it. One of them notices the screen and taps the video. It opens on the street, moves through the front door, and gets to the garden in August with the maple in full leaf. “Oh, that is what it looks like in summer.” That sentence is the open house doing its job. They scroll down to the details, see the square footage they had been guessing at, and one of them says, “we should come back when it is light.” The form is right there. They do not fill it in while you watch, but one of them photographs the screen, and the enquiry arrives that evening with Tuesday 5 p.m. as the preferred date.

You did not sell the video. You left it where it would be found.

Setting it up so it helps rather than annoys

  • Plugged in. Battery dies at 2:15. Bring the cable and use the outlet behind the island.
  • The property page, not a video file. The page gives them the video and the details and the form. A video file alone ends and leaves them nowhere.
  • Brightness up, auto-lock off. A screen that goes dark looks like a closed laptop someone left behind.
  • Sound at a level for one person. If the video has a voiceover, it should be audible to the person standing at the island and nobody else. Captions do the work for everyone else.
  • A small card beside it. “Tap to play the video. Scroll for details. Form at the bottom if you want to come back.” Nobody wants to touch a stranger's laptop without permission.
  • Not looping. A looping video with music turns the kitchen into a showroom and makes people leave it sooner. Play on request means the visitor chose it, which means they watch it.

The edited photo and the room next to it

This is where the screen can hurt you. If the living room was virtually staged in the photos and the real living room, ten feet away, is empty, the visitor notices. That is fine if the photo carries its label and you say so: “the living room is staged in the photos so you can see the scale; the label is on the page.” It is not fine if the staged photo looks like a claim about the room.

Same with a twilight edit. The visitor at 2 p.m. cannot check it, but the one who drives past at 6 p.m. can. Use edits that would survive the drive past.

What goes wrong with screens at open houses

  • The screen shows the portal. Visitors see your house and the four others the portal recommends. Open your page.
  • The screen is in the hall. People do not stop in the hall; they stop in the kitchen.
  • The video autoplays with music. You have made the house louder and the visitors quieter.
  • A television on the wall playing a slideshow. It reads as advertising. A laptop on the island reads as information.
  • Nobody mentions it. One line as they come in: “there is a video on the island if you want to see the garden in summer.” That line is the difference between watched and ignored.

Before next Sunday

Test the page on the device you will use, in the kitchen, with the cable. Write the card. Decide on the one sentence you will say at the door. If you are not sure whether your edited photos will hold up next to the real rooms, read photo edits buyers notice before you put the screen out.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should the listing video loop during an open house?

No. A looping video with music turns the kitchen into a showroom and moves people out of it. Leave the property page open with the video one tap away and mention it at the door. A visitor who chooses to play it will watch it; a visitor who walks past a loop will not.

Where should the screen go at an open house?

On the kitchen island or counter, plugged in, with the property page open. The kitchen is where groups stop and talk. The hallway is where they pass through, and a television on the wall playing a slideshow reads as advertising rather than information.

Is it odd to show a video of the house inside the house?

It feels that way until you watch a visitor use it. The video shows the rooms in order and the garden in the season it was shot, which a grey November afternoon cannot. The page beneath it has the details and the form for a second visit, which is where the kitchen conversation usually goes.

What if the photos on the screen were virtually staged and the room is empty?

Say so, and let the label on the page do the rest. Staging that is labelled and explained helps visitors see scale. Staging that looks like a claim about the room, ten feet from the empty room, costs you trust at exactly the wrong moment.
