A disclosure policy for a brokerage using AI edits

A one-page brokerage policy for AI photo edits: allowed and banned edits, label wording and placement, originals, review, sign-off and a review date.

Why a policy and not a memo

Without a written policy, every agent draws their own line, and the office's reputation is set by whoever draws it loosest. A policy does three things a memo does not: it tells agents what they may do without asking, it gives the broker something to point at when someone asks “was this allowed,” and it makes the office's standard the same on a $300,000 condo and a $3 million estate.

The eight sections

1. Allowed edits

Name them. For most offices: exposure and colour correction, straightening, lens correction, removal of loose personal items, blue sky, green lawn, twilight conversion, virtual staging of vacant rooms, custom prompts that stay within those categories. Each of the altering ones comes with a label requirement in section three.

2. Banned edits

Name these too, plainly. Removing or changing anything fixed to the property: fixtures, outlets, vents, bars, cracks, stains, damage. Changing materials or finishes. Reshaping rooms or openings. Removing power lines, poles, neighbouring structures or anything about the surroundings. Staging over a seller's existing furniture. Any prompt that describes people rather than furniture.

3. Label wording

Fix the words so they are the same across the office. Something like: “Virtually staged” for staging; “Digitally altered: [sky / lawn / lighting / items removed]” for other alterations. Check your MLS's current rules for any required wording and use that where it exists.

4. Where labels go

On the image or its caption, in the listing remarks, on the property page, and in the first lines of any social post using the photo. All four, not one. The disclosure post explains why each one is needed.

5. Originals

Every listing keeps its untouched originals and every edited version, together, labelled, for a period the broker sets. State where they live and who can reach them. The provenance post describes a simple system.

6. Review before publishing

Whoever accepts an edit compares it with the original first. Name the checks: openings counted, fixed surfaces scanned, materials unchanged, view unchanged, edit did only what was asked. Failed edits are re-run, not captioned.

7. Exceptions and sign-off

An agent wants to remove a crack because the seller is fixing it before closing. Who decides? Name the person, usually the managing broker, and require the decision in writing. Exceptions that are not written down did not happen.

8. Review date

Tools change and MLS rules change. Put a date on the policy and a date to revisit it, every six or twelve months, whichever the office can keep to.

A worked example: the office of twelve

A brokerage with twelve agents and two staff. The managing broker drafts the eight sections in an afternoon, using the categories above and the MLS's current rules as the baseline. She sends it to the office's attorney, who adjusts the wording in two places. She holds a twenty-minute meeting, walks through one real listing from last month against the policy, and finds two photos that would now need labels. The agent adds them that day.

Three months later a buyer's agent emails about a staged photo. The broker replies with the policy, the label, and the original, in one message. The question ends there.

Where policies fail

  • Too long. Nobody reads page four. One page, eight headings.
  • No banned list. “Use good judgement” means twelve different standards.
  • Label wording left to agents. “Enhanced,” “staged,” “edited,” “AI” all appear across the office and none of them match.
  • No one owns review. If the policy says “edits should be reviewed” without saying by whom, they are not.
  • Written once, never revisited. The tool the policy describes was replaced a year ago.
  • Photographers not included. If outside photographers deliver edits, they need the policy too. The photographer's policy post is the counterpart.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long should a brokerage AI edit policy be?

One page. Eight short sections: allowed edits, banned edits, label wording, label placement, originals, review before publishing, exceptions and sign-off, and a review date. Anything longer does not get read.

Who should review the policy before it goes to agents?

An attorney, and whoever at your MLS answers rule questions. Rules differ by MLS and state, and the policy should match your MLS's current rules on altered images and labels. This post is a practical outline, not legal advice.

What edits should a brokerage ban outright?

Removing or changing anything fixed to the property, changing materials or finishes, reshaping rooms or openings, and removing power lines or neighbouring structures. Add staging over a seller's existing furniture and any prompt that describes people rather than furniture. Name each one plainly so nobody has to guess.

Does the policy need to cover outside photographers?

Yes. If photographers deliver edited photos, they need the same allowed list, banned list and label wording. Send them the policy and ask them to deliver originals alongside any edits.
