Property pages for agents who list twenty homes a year

At twenty listings a year, a page per listing has to be a by-product of the listing, not a project. Same routine, one inbox, sold pages that last.

At twenty listings a year, which means a new one every two or three weeks and three or four live at once in spring, a property page cannot be a project. It has to fall out of the work you already do: choose the photos, type the facts, make the video, and the page exists. The routine is identical every time, enquiries from every page land in one inbox, and every sold page stays live as a quiet record of the year; agents who build pages by hand at this volume stop by listing eight, and the rest of the year's listings go without.

The difference between an agent with twenty pages and an agent with eight is not effort. It is whether the page was ever a separate task.

The routine, fixed and repeated

Every listing follows the same six steps on the same schedule, so you never decide what to do, only when the photos arrive.

  1. Photos arrive. Usually the evening of the shoot. Choose the main photo and the gallery order; cut duplicates.
  2. Facts typed. Price, beds, baths, square feet, from the MLS entry, read twice.
  3. Edits, if any. Blue sky on the exterior, staging on the empty living room, each compared against the original and labelled.
  4. Video made. A 16:9 video for the page and the MLS, a vertical one for social, same photos, a short script.
  5. Page published. Two-minute review on your phone. Branded link to the seller for approval.
  6. Links out. Unbranded link into the MLS tour field. Branded link to the sign company, the social posts, the email, the open house stand.

Total time per listing after the photos land: an evening, not a weekend. At twenty listings, that is twenty evenings instead of twenty weekends.

One inbox for all of them

With four listings live, enquiries arrive all day from four pages. If each page has its own form service, you check four places. If all four pages send to one inbox with the listing name on each enquiry, you check one place and sort by what needs a reply first. On a Saturday with two open houses, that is the difference between replying by dinner and replying on Monday.

It also makes the seller conversation simple. "Eleven enquiries this week, four asked for showings, two came." You can say that for any listing because the inbox already knows.

A worked example: the spring with four live

March: a condo and a townhouse list in the same week. April: a 4-bed and a ranch join them. Four pages, four sets of links, one inbox. Each listing took one evening to set up. The condo goes under contract in week two, the townhouse in week four. Both pages are marked sold at closing and stay live. In May, the 4-bed gets a price cut; one edit, the page and the MLS tour show the new price. In June, a past client whose friend saw the sold townhouse page asks for a listing appointment. You bring the townhouse page to the meeting.

Twenty listings a year means twenty of these pages by December, each one an evening's work and each one still reachable.

What goes wrong at volume

  • Pages for the first few, then none. The page was a project and the project got dropped when the calendar filled.
  • Pages on three different tools because each was built when a different tool was handy. Three logins, three forms, three places to mark sold.
  • Subscriptions that stack. A per-site fee at $9 to $150 a month per site at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, times four live, times twelve months, for pages that could have come with the listing.
  • Sold pages never marked. At volume, the closing-day step gets skipped and old pages keep producing enquiries for closed homes.
  • Inconsistent links. One listing's sign points at a portal, another at a page, another at a video. Nobody remembers which.
  • Skipping the two-minute review because it is the fourth listing this month. That is the one with the wrong price.

The test of a routine

A routine that works at twenty listings is one you can hand to an assistant with a one-page note and get the same result. Write the six steps down with the two-minute review and the closing-day step. If a new teammate can run it on their first listing, it will survive your busiest month.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Is a property page worth it for every listing, including the small ones?

Yes, if the page takes minutes rather than an afternoon. The condo at the low end of your range gets the same routine as the estate at the top; the routine is what makes it cheap. Skipping small listings is how the habit breaks.

How do I keep twenty pages' enquiries from getting lost?

Send every page's form to one inbox with the listing name attached to each enquiry. Check it at fixed times each day and reply to anyone who asked for a date first. Separate inboxes per listing is where enquiries go missing.

Should I delete old pages to keep things tidy?

No. Mark them sold and leave them live. Old links keep working, and a sold page with your name on it is something a future seller can find. Tidiness is not worth broken links.

Can an assistant run the page routine for me?

If the routine is written down as fixed steps with a review and a closing-day step, yes. The parts that need you are the main photo choice, the facts check against the MLS and the seller approval. Everything else can be delegated.
