Do I need a drone to market a listing

No. Most homes sell on good ground-level photos, a clear video and a page that answers questions. A drone earns its fee when the setting is the story.

No. A typical home sells on sharp ground-level photos, a short video and a property page that answers the questions buyers actually ask. A drone session earns its fee when the land or the setting is the reason someone would buy: acreage, waterfront, a ridge-line view, a pool and garden you cannot see from the street, or a house set so far back that the front photo shows a driveway and a hedge.

At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, a drone or aerial session runs $150–350 on top of the photo shoot. That is money well spent on a five-acre property. It is money that adds little to a three-bed in a cul-de-sac where the aerial shows the neighbour's trampoline and a lot of roof.

What an aerial photo is for

An aerial answers questions the ground cannot. How big is the lot. What is behind the house. How close is the water, the road, the school, the neighbour. Where does the sun come from. If none of those questions matter to the buyer of this particular home, the aerial is decoration.

For most suburban and urban homes the questions that decide a showing are different: what does the kitchen look like, how is the light in the living room, is the primary bedroom big enough, is the garden usable. Those are ground-level questions, and ground-level photos answer them. Put the budget into getting the interiors right before adding altitude.

Two listings, one decision each

Listing one is a four-bed on 1.5 acres with a pond, a barn and a long tree-lined drive. From the road the house is a roof behind trees. Here the aerial is the main photo. It shows the drive, the pond, the house in its clearing, and it does the work that no ground shot can. Book the drone.

Listing two is a three-bed on a 0.2-acre corner lot in a 1990s subdivision. The front elevation is clear from the sidewalk, the garden is a fenced rectangle, and the selling points are the renovated kitchen and the finished basement. An aerial here shows a lot of shingles and six identical houses. Skip the drone, spend the time on a twilight front exterior and a careful kitchen set.

The same test works for condos: the building's position relative to the park or the waterfront might justify an aerial, but the unit itself does not.

Where aerial shots go wrong

  • Too high. A shot from 120 metres shows a map, not a home. Buyers lose the house in the rooftops.
  • The wrong season. A bare-tree February aerial of a wooded lot looks like a parking lot with sticks. If the trees are the point, wait or reschedule.
  • Showing what you did not want to show. The highway two streets over, the industrial yard behind the fence, the neighbour's junk pile. Look at the aerial as a buyer would before it goes anywhere.
  • Leading with it when it does not deserve to lead. An aerial as the main photo of an ordinary suburban home makes the listing look like it has something to hide at ground level.
  • Rules. Drone flights have airspace and local rules that change by location. A licensed operator handles that; a friend with a drone usually does not.

What to do instead on an ordinary home

Spend the drone money on the things that move an ordinary listing. A well-timed front exterior, shot when the light is on the face of the house, carries more weight than an aerial for a home you can read from the kerb. A photo-based listing video gives buyers a sense of flow through the rooms. A property page with the details, gallery and video in one link gives them somewhere to go after the post. The one thing to do next

Stand on the sidewalk in front of the listing and ask one question: can a buyer understand this property from here. If yes, book the photographer and skip the drone. If no, add the aerial session and plan to use that shot as the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

When is a drone photo worth paying for?

When the lot, the setting or the position of the house is a selling point: acreage, waterfront, a view, a large garden, or a house hidden from the street. For a typical subdivision home the aerial adds little and the budget is better spent on interiors and a twilight front exterior.

How much does a drone session cost?

At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, a drone or aerial session runs $150–350, usually on top of the standard photo shoot. Prices vary by region and by how many stills and clips you need.

Can I fly a drone myself for my listing?

Drone flights have airspace and local rules that vary by location, and commercial use often has its own requirements. Use a licensed operator and check the current rules where the listing is before anyone flies.

Is an AI aerial opening the same as a drone shot?

No. An AI aerial opening is generated footage built from a reviewed front exterior photo, not a recording of the property from the air. It can open a video, but it does not show the real surroundings the way a drone does, and it needs review before use.
