Marketing a listing you did not photograph yourself

To market a listing shot by someone else, get the full-resolution originals, written permission for what you may do with them, and a note on credit.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. To market a listing you did not photograph, get three things from whoever did: the full-resolution originals, written permission for what you may do with them (publish, edit, make video), and a clear note on credit. Then build the video and the page from the originals, not from copies pulled off a portal.

To market a listing you did not photograph, get three things from whoever did: the full-resolution originals, written permission for what you may do with them (publish, edit, make video, reuse after the sale), and a clear note on how they want to be credited. Then build the video and the property page from those originals, not from the compressed copies that come back off a portal or a messaging app. Everything that goes wrong in this situation comes from skipping one of the three.

Get the originals, not the downloads

A photo that has been through a portal, an email thread or a group chat has been resized and recompressed, sometimes more than once. It looks fine on a phone and soft on a buyer's television, and it falls apart when a video moves across it. Ask for the delivery link, or a fresh export at full size. If the photographer is a brokerage staff member, the originals usually sit in a shared drive; ask where, and copy them to your own listing before anything else happens to them. If the seller supplied the photos from a previous listing, ask who shot them, because the seller is often not the owner of those files.

Ask what you may edit, in writing

Unless your agreement says otherwise, assume the photographer holds the rights to the photos, and licences vary; ask your broker if you are unsure what your brokerage's standard terms cover. Some allow the listing period only. Some forbid edits. Some are fine with sky replacement and staging as long as the photographer's credit stays and the disclosure is visible. One message settles it: may I publish these on the MLS, the portals and a property page, make a listing video and a social video from them, apply labelled AI edits such as blue sky or virtual staging, and use them after the sale for a just-sold post. Keep the reply. If the answer to edits is no, the photos still carry the listing; you simply do not edit them.

Decide who publishes the page

If the photographer offers to publish the page with your contact details on it, that can be faster than doing it yourself, and some photographers include it in their delivery. Make sure the page carries your name, your business and the number you answer, and test an enquiry before the link goes anywhere. If you publish, you need the originals on your own phone and the licence in your messages. Either way, one page per address.

Two cases, worked through

The brokerage photographer. A 3-bed in a cul-de-sac is shot by the brokerage's in-house photographer, who delivers thirty-two landscape photos to a shared folder. The brokerage's standard terms cover publishing, video and labelled edits. You copy the originals into the listing, choose the main photo, stage the empty office with the label on and compare it against the original, build the landscape video and a vertical cut, publish the page with your contact card, and put the unbranded link in the MLS. The photographer's name goes in the listing notes as the brokerage requires. Nothing needed a second conversation because the terms already existed.

The seller's own photos. A seller hands you twenty photos from when they bought the house four years ago. They were shot by the previous listing agent's photographer, and the seller does not own them. You thank the seller, explain why those cannot go on the MLS under your name, and book a fresh shoot. If the seller has personal snapshots of the garden in bloom from last June, those can go on the page labelled with the month, with the seller's permission, as supplementary photos; the listing itself is built from the new shoot.

What goes wrong

  • Marketing from screenshots or portal downloads, then wondering why the video looks soft.
  • Applying staging or a twilight edit to a photo whose licence forbids edits, and hearing about it from the photographer after the listing is live.
  • Using photos from a previous listing without knowing who owns them.
  • Two pages for the same address, one by the photographer and one by you, with different prices.
  • Cropping out or covering a credit the photographer's terms require.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Can I use photos from the seller's previous listing?

Only with permission from whoever owns them, which is usually the photographer or the previous brokerage, not the seller. In most cases the right answer is a fresh shoot, with the seller's own snapshots used only as labelled supplementary photos.

What permission do I need to make a video from someone else's photos?

A licence that covers it. Ask in one message whether you may publish, make video, apply labelled AI edits and reuse after the sale, and keep the reply. If video is not covered, ask for it to be added before you build one.

Is it a problem if the photographer publishes the property page?

No, as long as the page carries your name, business and number, enquiries reach you, and there is only one page for the address. Test an enquiry yourself before the link goes on the sign.

Why does it matter whether I use the originals?

Copies that have been through a portal or a chat app are resized and recompressed. They look soft on large screens and worse when a video moves across them. The original delivery link or a fresh full-size export is the only version worth building from.
