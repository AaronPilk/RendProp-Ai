Writing a voiceover script that sounds like you

Say the script out loud first, type what you said, then cut a third. Short sentences, contractions, one idea per scene, no words you would not say aloud.

Write the voiceover script the way you talk: describe the home out loud to a friend, type what you said, then cut a third of it. Short sentences, contractions, one idea per scene, and no word you would never say at a showing. A script written this way sounds like you whether you record it yourself or an AI voice reads it, because the voice is in the sentences, not the microphone.

Most scripts sound like a brochure because they are written like one. The agent sits down to “write a script”, and writing mode produces subordinate clauses, lists of three adjectives and verbs like “boasts” and “features”. Nobody says “this home boasts” to a buyer in the hallway. The fix is to stop writing first and start talking first.

The talk-first method

  1. Stand in the house, or look at the photo set, and record a voice memo describing it to a friend who asked “so what's it like”. Ninety seconds, no notes.
  2. Transcribe the memo. Read it. It will be rambling and it will have the right words in it.
  3. Cut every sentence that repeats one before it, every “um” and “so”, and every sentence that is about you.
  4. Put what remains in the order the video will run, one sentence or two per scene.
  5. Read it aloud with a timer. Cut until it fits the video with a breath at each scene change.

The result is shorter than you expect and sounds like a person, because it started as one.

The tells of a written script

When you read the draft, listen for these. Each one is a sign the writing hand took over.

  • Clauses before the point. “Situated on a quiet cul-de-sac and surrounded by mature trees, this home…” A speaker says “It's the last house on the lane. Big trees all round.”
  • Three adjectives in a row. “Bright, spacious and inviting” is a written rhythm. Out loud you would pick one, or name the window.
  • Brochure verbs. Boasts, features, offers, showcases. In speech: has, is, gets.
  • No contractions. “It is” and “you will” read fine; spoken, they stiffen every line. Write “it's” and “you'll”.
  • Numbers written for the eye. “$467,000” and “2,150 sq ft” are how you type them. Say them: “four sixty-seven” and “twenty-one fifty square feet”. If an AI voice is reading, this matters more, because the voice will read what is on the page.

Matching sentences to scenes

A photo-based listing video moves scene to scene, each one a photo with camera motion. The script should move with it: one idea per scene, said while that scene is up, with a breath at the cut. If the front exterior is on screen for four seconds, that is about ten words. Write ten words about the front, not thirty. When the kitchen appears, talk about the kitchen. Saying the kitchen line over the bathroom shot is the fastest way to make a video feel wrong without the viewer knowing why.

Writing for an AI voice to read

If an AI voice is reading your script, punctuation becomes pacing. A full stop is a pause; a comma is a shorter one. Write short sentences and let the full stops do the breathing. Spell out anything you would not want read literally: “sq ft” becomes “square feet”, “w/” becomes “with”, a street number like “1402” becomes “fourteen oh two” if that is how locals say it. Read the finished script aloud yourself once; if you stumble, the voice will sound stiff there too.

One line, written and then spoken

Before: “Welcome to this exceptional residence, which boasts an open-concept layout, a gourmet kitchen with premium finishes, and a serene, private backyard oasis perfect for entertaining.”

After: “This is the last house on Orchard Lane. The kitchen's open to the living room, and the window over the sink looks straight down the garden. Out back there's a deck, a fence on three sides, and the old orchard behind that.”

Read both out loud. The second one you could say to someone's face.

Where scripts go wrong

  • Writing it separately from the description. The script should be cut from the description, so the facts and the order agree. The post on matching the listing copy to the video covers the sequence.
  • Filling every second. Silence over the best photo is fine. A script that never stops sounds like an advert.
  • Describing edits as the house. If the sky was replaced or the living room is virtually staged, the script describes the real room, and the disclosure sits where the photos are.
  • A sign-off about you. Your name and number are on the page's contact card. End on the house, or on the open house time.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long should a voiceover script be for a listing video?

As long as the video with a breath at each scene change, which usually means about two to three words per second of video. A 30-second reel takes roughly 60 to 80 words; a 90-second landscape video takes 180 to 250. Write it, read it aloud with a timer, and cut until it fits with room to breathe.

Should I write differently for an AI voice than for recording myself?

The sentences should be the same: short, spoken, one idea per scene. For an AI voice, pay more attention to what is literally on the page, since it reads what it sees. Spell out abbreviations, write numbers the way they are said, and use full stops for pauses.

Is it all right to leave gaps with no narration?

Yes, and it is often better. A few seconds of silence over the strongest photo lets the viewer look. A script that fills every second sounds like a commercial. Aim to speak over most scenes and leave one or two to the picture.

How do I get rid of the brochure tone in my script?

Start from speech rather than writing: record yourself describing the home, transcribe it, and edit that. Then hunt for the tells: clauses before the point, three adjectives in a row, verbs like boasts and features, and the absence of contractions. Replace each with what you would say in the hallway.
