Listing descriptions for condos: amenities, views and the walk

A condo description has three jobs: the unit, the building and the walk. Lead with the one a buyer cannot get elsewhere, usually the floor or the view.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A condo description has three jobs in this order: the unit (floor, exposure, what the windows face), the building (what the amenities and the fee actually are) and the walk (what is within a few minutes on foot, stated as a distance). Lead with the one thing the buyer cannot get in the unit two floors down, which is usually the view, the exposure or the corner position.

A condo description has three jobs: describe the unit, describe the building, describe the walk. Do them in that order, and open with the one thing a buyer cannot get by choosing a different unit in the same building, which is usually the floor, the exposure or what the windows face. The building's pool is shared by two hundred owners; the sixth-floor east-facing corner is not.

Condos get described badly because the building does the talking. The amenity list arrives from the management office, the writer pastes it in, and the unit itself gets two lines. But the buyer is choosing between units, often in the same building or the one next door, and the unit paragraph is where the decision gets made.

The unit paragraph: floor, light, layout

Start with what is fixed. Which floor. Which direction the main windows face and what they look at, named plainly: the river, the courtyard, the parking structure, the street with the trees. Corner or interior. Then the layout facts a buyer cannot read from the photos: whether the bedrooms share a wall with the neighbour, whether the kitchen is open or closed, whether there is in-unit laundry or a shared room on the floor. Then the updates with dates.

A worked example for a two-bed on the sixth floor: “A sixth-floor corner unit facing east and south, with the river from the living room and morning light in both bedrooms. The bedrooms are on opposite sides of the living area and neither shares a wall with a neighbouring unit. In-unit washer and dryer. Kitchen and both baths redone in 2021. One deeded parking space and a storage cage on the same level.”

The building paragraph: what you actually get for the fee

State the monthly fee and say what it covers in plain words: heat, water, building insurance, the doorman, the gym. If it covers nothing a buyer would expect, say that too. Then list the amenities a buyer would use, not the ones on the brochure. A roof deck that is open to residents counts. A party room that must be booked three months ahead counts, with that caveat. “Resort-style amenities” counts for nothing.

Say how the building runs: the number of units, the year built, whether there is a live-in super, whether pets are allowed and at what size, whether rentals are permitted. These are the questions that come by text at nine at night, and answering them in the description saves the showing for people who still want it. Check your MLS's current rules on where fee and pet details must go; some fields are separate from the remarks.

The walk paragraph: distances, not promises

Write what is within a short walk as things you can count: “Four minutes on foot to the Main Street station, six to the grocery store, ten to the waterfront path.” Name the starting point as the front door and the pace as a normal walk. Do not write “steps to” unless it is steps. Do not write “close to everything”, which is a way of saying you did not check. Leave out judgments about who lives in the area and how it feels at night; those are claims you cannot stand behind and some touch fair-housing territory, which is a conversation for your broker, not the remarks field. There is more in describing a neighborhood without overpromising.

The same unit, two descriptions

Before: “Luxury living at its finest in this spacious 2-bed with breathtaking views, resort-style amenities and an unbeatable location steps from everything downtown has to offer.”

After: “A sixth-floor east-and-south corner with the river from the living room and sun in both bedrooms in the morning. The $610 monthly fee covers heat, water, the doorman and the gym. Residents use the roof deck without booking; the party room books through the office. Four minutes on foot to the Main Street station, six to the grocery store.”

The mistakes that show up at the viewing

  • Calling a courtyard a view. If the windows face the interior court, say “faces the planted courtyard”. A buyer who arrives expecting the skyline will not forgive it.
  • Hiding the fee. It is the first thing a serious buyer asks, and leaving it out reads as if it is bad. State it and what it buys.
  • “Steps from” a station that is a twelve-minute walk. Buyers walk it on the way to the showing.
  • Amenities that are not available. The pool that is closed for repairs, the gym that is a treadmill in a storage room. Describe what exists today.
  • Skipping parking and storage. Say whether a space is deeded, assigned or rented, and where storage is. For many buyers this decides it.

Start with the window

Stand in the living room of the unit and write down what you see through the main window, which direction it faces, and which floor you are on. That is the first sentence. The building and the walk follow.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a condo description start with the building or the unit?

The unit, with the one thing another unit in the building does not have: the floor, the exposure, the corner, the view. The building paragraph comes second because every unit shares it. The exception is a building so unusual that it is the reason to buy, and even then the unit's position deserves the second sentence.

Should the monthly fee go in the description?

Yes, along with what it covers, unless your MLS has a separate field for it, in which case check its current rules on repeating the figure in the remarks. On your own property page and in your emails, state it plainly. Buyers ask first and assume the worst when it is missing.

How do I describe walkability without exaggerating?

Count minutes on foot from the front door at a normal pace to named places, and write those numbers. Avoid “steps to” and “close to everything”. Leave out judgments about the area's character and about the people who live there; stick to what a buyer can walk and verify.

What about amenities that are temporarily closed?

Say so, or leave them out. A buyer who tours a building expecting a pool and finds it drained will doubt everything else in the description. Describe what a resident can use this month.
