Auto showroom and dealership tours

A dealership tour sells the visit, not the car: the floor, service reception, the waiting area and the lot. Shoot it clean and lit, with hours on one link.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A showroom or dealership tour sells the visit, not a specific car: the floor, the service reception, the waiting area, the handover bay and the lot. Shoot it clean and fully lit at opening, keep the video under a minute, and host it on a page with hours and a contact so the person deciding where to buy or service can see the place before they commit a Saturday to it.

A dealership tour sells the place, not a car: the showroom floor, the service reception, the waiting area with the coffee, the handover bay and the lot, because the cars are already on the inventory site. It answers the question a buyer or a service customer asks before giving up a Saturday morning: what is it like there. Shoot it clean, fully lit, at opening time, keep the video under a minute, and put it on one page with hours and a contact.

Two audiences, one tour

The buyer is choosing between three dealers for the same model and will pick the one that feels least like a fight. The service customer is choosing where to leave the car for a day and wants to see the waiting room and the loaner desk. The tour serves both if it walks the route each of them takes: in from the lot, across the floor, to the desk they will sit at, and to the bay where the car goes. Everything else, the awards wall, the brand signage, the giant flag, is set dressing.

The shot list for a showroom

  1. The building from the road. Sign readable, lot in front, on a clear day.
  2. The showroom floor from the entrance, wide. Cars spaced, floor polished, every light on, no balloons.
  3. The floor from the far corner looking back. Shows depth and the glass front.
  4. A sales desk or a consultation area. Where the buyer will sit for two hours. Make it look like somewhere a person could sit for two hours.
  5. Service reception. The counter, the drive-in lane, the advisor's desk.
  6. The waiting area. Coffee, seats, outlets, the window onto the shop if there is one. This photo brings service customers back.
  7. The service bays, from the door. Clean floor, lifts up, tools put away.
  8. The handover bay or delivery area. Where the new car is revealed. Buyers like to see it.
  9. The lot, from a corner, with rows straight. Used and new separated if they are.

Shoot at opening, before customers, with every light on and the floor freshly cleaned. Move the sale signs and the balloons out of frame; they date the photos. Faces need permission, so shoot before the team is on the floor or ask them.

Worked example: a single-brand dealer with a six-bay service shop

A dealer with a twelve-car showroom, a six-bay service shop and a 140-car lot shoots at 7:30am on a Wednesday after the overnight clean. Twelve landscape photos: the building from the road, the floor from the door, the floor from the back corner, a consultation desk, the service drive-in lane, service reception, the waiting area with the coffee bar, the bays with lifts up, the handover bay with a covered car, the parts counter, the accessible restroom, and the lot from the corner. The listing video runs fifty seconds: road, door, floor, desk, service lane, waiting area, bays, handover, lot. The voiceover gives sales and service hours, the loaner policy in one sentence, and the fact that service customers can watch the shop from the waiting area. The page shows hours by department, the gallery, the video on request, a contact card with the main line and a form that asks for the date the visitor wants. The first enquiry is a service customer asking whether Saturday morning is possible for a brake job and whether the waiting area has wifi.

Hours by department

Sales hours and service hours differ, and the most common reason someone calls a dealership is to find out which one is open. Type both. If parts has its own hours, type those too. The enquiry form's date question works for both audiences: a buyer wants a day to come in, a service customer wants a day to drop off.

Where dealership tours go wrong

  • Making it about one car. That car sells in a week and the video is stale. Keep the cars generic and the place specific.
  • Balloons, sale banners and flags. They date the photos to a weekend.
  • Skipping the service side. Service customers are the repeat business, and they are the ones who want to see the waiting room.
  • A dirty bay. If the bays are not clean, shoot the service reception only.
  • A vertical reel as the page video. The vertical cut is for social. The page video is in landscape orientation so the floor reads.
  • AI edits that add cars or clean up the lot. Buyers come to see the lot. Keep edits disclosed and compared with the original, or do not use them here.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should a dealership video feature a specific car?

No. The car sells and the video goes stale. The tour is about the place: the floor, the desks, service reception, the waiting area, the bays and the lot. Keep the cars in frame generic and the place specific.

What matters most to service customers in a tour?

The waiting area and service reception. They are deciding where to leave the car for a day, and a clean waiting room with coffee and a view of the shop decides it. Shoot the bays only if they are clean.

When should I shoot the showroom?

At opening, after the overnight clean, before customers and before the sale banners go up. Every light on, cars spaced, floor polished. Faces need permission, so shoot before the team is on the floor or ask.

Which Rendprop mode fits a dealership?

The retail mode. The page asks for store details such as hours, which you can state by department, and the enquiry form asks for the date the visitor wants, which works for both buyers and service customers.
