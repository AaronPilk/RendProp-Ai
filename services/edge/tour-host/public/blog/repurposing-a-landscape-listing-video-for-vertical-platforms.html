Repurposing a 16:9 listing video for vertical platforms

Do not crop a 16:9 listing video into 9:16. Build the vertical video from portrait photos, or shoot the key rooms vertically this week and rebuild it.

Do not crop a 16:9 listing video into 9:16. The crop keeps the middle third of every frame and throws away the sides, so the kitchen loses its island, the living room loses its width, and the whole home looks smaller than it is. The right way to get a vertical version is to build it separately from portrait photos. If the landscape video is all you have, the honest options are to post it as it is and accept the bars, or to shoot portrait frames of the six key rooms this week and build the vertical cut from those.

What the crop actually removes

A 16:9 frame is almost twice as wide as it is tall. A 9:16 frame is almost twice as tall as it is wide. To fit one inside the other you keep a vertical strip roughly a third of the original width. Whatever was in the outer two thirds is gone.

In a listing video that outer two thirds is rarely empty. It is the window that lit the room, the end of the counter run, the second sofa that showed the room could hold two, the doorway that explained how the rooms connect. The crop does not just shrink the picture; it removes the evidence that the home has space.

The kitchen frame, cropped and rebuilt

An agent inherits a landscape video from the photographer for a four-bed with a long galley kitchen. The video's best shot is the kitchen from the dining end: counters on both sides running to a window at the far end, the island in the middle.

Cropped to 9:16. The island survives. Both counter runs are cut off at the edges. The window at the far end survives but reads as a narrow slot. The room looks like a corridor with an island in it. A viewer who then walks in at the open house says, this is much bigger than the video.

Rebuilt from a portrait frame. The agent goes back on Wednesday evening with a phone, stands at the dining end one step further back than the photographer did, holds the phone level and shoots a portrait frame. Both counter runs are in. The window is in. The ceiling and the floor are in, and the length of the room reads because the eye travels up the frame to the window. The camera motion on that frame can push toward the window and the room gets longer as it goes.

Thirty minutes on Wednesday for six portrait frames, and the vertical video is a different, better video rather than a damaged copy of the first one.

If you genuinely cannot reshoot

Sometimes the home is three hours away, or the seller has moved on, or the listing goes live tomorrow. Then the options, in order of honesty, are these.

  1. Post the 16:9 video as it is. It appears as a horizontal strip with space above and below. It looks smaller than a native vertical reel, but every room is intact and the viewer sees what the home is.
  2. Post it with the caption on the empty space. Some agents place the price and the open house details in the band above or below the video. It uses the space and reads as deliberate.
  3. Crop only the frames that survive it. A front exterior with sky above and lawn below often crops to vertical without losing much. A galley kitchen does not. If you must crop, crop by frame, not by video, and drop the frames that lose their meaning.

What not to do is a blurred-edge fill, where the video is stretched and blurred to fill the sides. It looks like what it is, and the blur draws the eye to the exact place where the room was cut.

Why the script changes too

A 16:9 listing video often runs sixty to ninety seconds with a full walk through the home. A vertical reel runs twenty to thirty. Even if the frames could survive the crop, the script cannot: a cropped 75-second video is a 75-second reel, and the viewer leaves at second eight. The vertical cut needs its own shorter script with the price or the feature in the first line. See writing a 30-second voiceover script.

Mistakes that make it worse

  • Cropping the whole video in one pass. Every frame is cropped the same way whether it survives or not.
  • Captions placed for the 16:9 frame. They sit at the bottom of the wide frame and get sliced in the crop. The vertical cut needs its own captions, placed for the tall frame.
  • Posting the long script on the short format. The reel is a different piece with a different first line, not a shortened tour.
  • The blurred-edge fill. It advertises the crop.
  • Deciding never to shoot portrait again because this one was hard. The whole problem goes away if the next shoot includes six portrait frames. See shooting with the format in mind.

Book thirty minutes at the home

If you have a landscape video and no vertical one for an active listing, book thirty minutes at the home this week, shoot six portrait frames one step further back than feels right, and build the vertical cut from those. If the home is out of reach, post the 16:9 video as it is and shoot both orientations on the next listing.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Can I crop my 16:9 listing video to 9:16 for Instagram?

You can, but the crop keeps only the middle third of each frame and loses the counters, windows and width that showed the home's size. Build the vertical video from portrait photos instead. If you must crop, do it frame by frame and drop the frames that lose their meaning.

What if I cannot get back to the home to shoot portrait photos?

Post the 16:9 video as it is and accept the space above and below. Every room stays intact. Avoid blurred-edge fills, which draw attention to the crop.

Does the vertical version need a different script?

Yes. A 16:9 listing video often runs over a minute; a reel runs twenty to thirty seconds. The vertical cut needs its own shorter script with the price or the feature in the first line.

How long does it take to shoot portrait frames for a vertical cut?

Around thirty minutes for six rooms if you know the shot list: exterior, kitchen, living room, primary bedroom, the feature and the yard. Stand one step further back than you would for a 16:9 frame and keep the phone level.
