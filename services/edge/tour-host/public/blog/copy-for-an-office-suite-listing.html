Copy for an office suite listing

An office suite listing describes the suite, not the building: square feet, floor, layout, the lease basics you may state, and access. The lobby waits.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. An office suite listing should describe the suite before the building: square feet and which measure that is, the floor, the layout in rooms (private offices, open area, conference rooms, kitchen), the lease basics the owner allows you to state (availability date, term, what rent includes), and access (parking, transit, building hours, elevator). The lobby and the building's amenities come third, because every suite in the building shares them.

An office suite listing should describe the suite before it describes the building. Square feet and which measure that figure is, the floor, the layout counted in rooms, the lease basics the owner allows you to state, and how people get in and out. “Suite 410: about 2,800 square feet rentable on the fourth floor, with six private offices, an open area for ten desks, one conference room for eight, a kitchen and a storage room. Available 1 March on a three- to five-year term; rent includes building operating costs and after-hours HVAC on request. Twelve reserved parking spaces, two blocks from the Main Street station, card access around the clock.” The lobby comes in paragraph three, because every tenant in the building shares it.

Office listings lead with the building because that is what the owner's brochure does. “Class A tower with a renovated lobby and on-site management” is true of forty suites in the same building and says nothing about whether this one fits a twelve-person firm. The tenant is choosing a suite. Write about the suite.

The suite block

  • Size, and which size. State the square footage and say whether it is rentable or usable, since the two differ by the building's load factor and tenants ask. If you do not know which, ask the landlord before you publish a number.
  • Floor and exposure. Fourth floor, windows on the north and east. Natural light matters to office tenants more than most listings admit.
  • Layout counted in rooms. Six private offices (two with windows), an open area that fits ten workstations, a conference room for eight, a kitchen, a storage room, a reception area. Counts and capacities, not “flexible layout”.
  • Condition. Delivered as is, with new carpet and paint, or with a tenant improvement allowance to be negotiated. Say which.
  • What is in it. Furniture staying or not, cabling, server closet, the HVAC zoning if the suite has its own.

The lease paragraph: say what you may

Ask the owner or the listing broker what can be stated publicly before you write a word about terms. Many owners will allow the availability date, the term range and what the rent includes, and prefer the rate itself to be quoted on request; others publish the rate. Whatever the answer, write it plainly and do not hint. “Rate on request” is fine when it is the policy; “competitive rates” is filler. Say what is included (operating expenses, utilities, janitorial, parking) and what is not, since the headline rate means little without that.

Access, hours and getting there

Office tenants ask three practical questions: where staff park, how visitors arrive, and when the building is open. Answer them with numbers. Parking spaces reserved and their cost, visitor parking, the walk to transit in minutes, building hours and after-hours access, the elevator count, loading access for a move-in. Add the floor's restrooms and whether they are inside the suite or in the common corridor.

Now the building

With the suite described, the building earns its paragraph: the year built and renovated, the lobby, on-site management, the fitness room, the cafe on the ground floor, the other tenants if naming them is allowed. Keep it short, because the tenant has already read it on four other listings in the same building. Write for the tenant you expect

A 2,800-square-foot suite with six offices is a twelve- to fifteen-person professional firm. A 600-square-foot suite with one office and a reception area is a solo practitioner. Knowing which lets you pick what to lead with: the conference room and the reception for the firm, the quiet and the lease length for the solo. Describe the suite, not the tenant, but order the facts for the reader you expect.

Suite 410, two ways

Before: “Prime Class A office space in the heart of the business district. Turnkey suite with flexible layout and abundant natural light. Renovated lobby, on-site management, competitive rates. Call for details.”

After: “Suite 410, about 2,800 square feet rentable, fourth floor, windows north and east. Six private offices (two with windows), open area for ten desks, a conference room for eight, kitchen and storage; delivered with new carpet and paint. Available 1 March on a three- to five-year term; rent includes operating costs and janitorial, with after-hours HVAC on request. Twelve reserved spaces, visitor parking in the front lot, two blocks from the Main Street station, card access at all hours. Built 1988, lobby renovated 2021, management on site.”

Where office copy wastes the tenant's time

  • “Class A” with nothing after it. A rating is not a description.
  • Square feet without the measure. The tenant's space planner will ask in the first email.
  • “Flexible layout.” Count the rooms.
  • “Turnkey” and “prime”. Neither survives the tour.
  • Building photos only. Show the suite's rooms, and if any photo is virtually staged with desks, say so in the text.
  • No availability date. It is the first filter for every tenant with a lease expiring.

Count the rooms first

Walk the suite with a notepad, count the offices, the desks the open area fits, the conference seats and the storage, and confirm the square-foot measure with the landlord. Write the suite block from that, then the lease paragraph after you have asked what may be stated, and let the building wait for paragraph three.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should an office listing state rentable or usable square feet?

State the figure and say which measure it is. The two differ by the building's load factor, and a tenant's space planner will ask in the first email. If you are not sure which figure you have, confirm with the landlord before publishing any number.

Can I publish the asking rent for an office suite?

Ask the owner or listing broker first. Some publish the rate; others prefer rate on request and allow the term range, the availability date and what rent includes to be stated. Whatever the policy, write it plainly and avoid phrases like competitive rates, which tell the tenant nothing.

What matters more to office tenants, the suite or the building?

The suite, because that is what they are choosing. The building is shared with every other tenant and appears on every other listing in it. Lead with the size, floor, layout in rooms, condition, lease basics and access, and give the building a short third paragraph.

Should I describe the ideal tenant in the listing?

Describe the suite and order the facts for the tenant you expect, but do not describe the tenant. A 2,800-square-foot suite with six offices will be read by professional firms; lead with the conference room and reception. Phrases about who should lease it add nothing and can cause problems.
