Single-property websites: build, buy or get one with the listing

Three ways to get a single-property website: build it, pay a monthly site tool, or have it generated from the listing content you already made.

There are three ways to get a single-property website: build it yourself on a general site builder, pay a monthly tool that hosts one page per listing, or get the page generated from the photos, video and details you already entered when you made the listing. Building costs time on every listing, and buying costs money on every listing, typically $9 to $150 per month per site at typical U.S. rates as of August 2026. Getting the page with the listing costs neither, because the page is a by-product of work you have already done.

Which one fits depends on how many listings you carry and how much you want to touch each page.

Option one: build it yourself

A general site builder with a template. You upload photos, type the facts, embed the video, add a form, pick a domain or a subpage, and publish. The first one takes an afternoon. The fifth one takes an hour. Every one needs updating when the price changes and marking when the home sells.

This suits an agent with two or three listings a year who enjoys the control. It stops suiting anyone past about six listings a year, because the afternoons add up and the pages drift out of date.

What goes wrong: forms that email a dead address, videos embedded from a platform that shows unrelated suggestions after playback, pages that are never marked sold and keep producing enquiries for a home that closed in spring.

Option two: buy a monthly site tool

Services exist that produce a page per listing from a template, often with a custom domain per property. You upload the same photos and facts, pay per site or per month, and get a page that looks the same for every listing.

At $9 to $150 per month per site, the arithmetic depends on how long a listing stays live and how many you run at once. An agent with eight active listings at, say, $30 a month each is paying $240 a month for pages. An agent on a cheaper flat plan pays less but still pays whether or not a listing is live.

This suits a team that wants a consistent look and has someone whose job is to build pages. What goes wrong: the page is a separate thing from the MLS entry and from the video, so three places need updating on a price change, and the subscription keeps running after the listing closes.

Option three: get the page with the listing

You already chose the photos, typed the price and the beds and baths, and made the video. The page is those same things, laid out. If the tool that makes the video also publishes the page from the same listing, there is nothing extra to build and nothing to update twice. Change the price in one place and the page reflects it, on both the branded and the unbranded link.

This suits anyone who makes a video for every listing anyway, and especially the agent listing twenty homes a year who cannot afford an afternoon per page.

What goes wrong: less control over layout, which is a real trade. If you need a page that looks nothing like a listing page, this is not it.

A worked example: the same 4-bed, three ways

A 4-bed on a half-acre lot lists Tuesday, with 30 photos and a 50-second video from the photographer.

  • Build: Tuesday evening building the page, Wednesday morning testing the form, Wednesday afternoon fixing the video embed. Live Wednesday night. Price cut in week three means another half hour.
  • Buy: Tuesday evening uploading to the tool, live by bedtime. Price cut means logging in and editing. Subscription runs until you remember to cancel after closing.
  • With the listing: The page publishes when the video is done Tuesday evening. Price cut is one edit. Marking it sold updates the page.

How to decide in two questions

First: do you make a video for every listing. If yes, the page should come from the same source as the video. If no, you are choosing between build and buy.

Second: how many listings will be live at once in a busy month. Under three, building is tolerable. Over three, you are either paying per site or getting the page with the listing, and the difference is whether you want a separate tool to maintain.

The next step: count your active listings right now and the pages you have for them. If any listing has no page, or a page with a stale price, that is the gap to close. FAQ

Questions on this topic

Is a custom domain per property worth paying for?

Rarely. Buyers reach the page from a link in a text, a post, a QR code or the MLS, not by typing a domain. A clean path on a shared domain works the same and costs nothing to set up or renew.

What does a single-property website typically cost?

At typical U.S. rates as of August 2026, single-property website tools run $9 to $150 per month per site. The real cost includes the time to build each page and the subscriptions that keep running after a listing closes.

Can I build a property website myself with no technical skill?

Yes, with a general site builder and a template, but each page takes time and needs maintenance. It works for an agent with a few listings a year and stops working when there are several live at once.

What happens to the page after the home sells?

It should say sold, stay reachable for anyone with the old link, and stop generating showing requests. Pages built separately often get forgotten and keep producing enquiries for a closed home. A page tied to the listing updates when you mark it sold.
