Listing marketing for out-of-town buyers

An out-of-town buyer sees your listing on a phone and the home once in person. Make the page the first showing and the trip the second.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. An out-of-town buyer does their first showing on a phone and their second in person, with a trip in between that costs them time and money. The listing has to earn the trip: a video that shows the home in walking order, a page that answers the practical questions they cannot answer by driving past, and a way to request a showing date that fits the days they will be in town.

An out-of-town buyer does the first showing on a phone and the second one in person, with a flight or a long drive between the two, so the listing's job is to earn that trip and then make it count. That means a video that walks the home in the order a visitor would, a page that answers the practical questions a local buyer would settle by driving past, and an enquiry form that lets the buyer name the days they will be in town. Local buyers forgive a thin listing because they can go and look; out-of-town buyers cannot, so they move on.

What an out-of-town buyer decides from a phone

More than you would think. From the phone they decide whether the street looks like somewhere they would live, whether the layout works for them, whether the yard is real, whether the kitchen is the one in the photos or a wider-lens version of it, and whether the agent is responsive. They decide which three homes to see on the one day they have. What they cannot decide from the phone is how the house feels, what the neighbours are like, how the light falls at 4pm and whether the road noise is a problem. The marketing should finish the first list so the trip can be spent on the second.

The things they cannot see by driving past are the things a local never thinks to write down: how far to the grocery store, what the street looks like at the end of the block, whether there is a sidewalk, which way the house faces, how far the airport is. Write them in. Shoot the street from both ends. Shoot the walk from the car to the front door.

A worked example: three homes, one Saturday

A buyer moving for a job has one Saturday in town. They have shortlisted nine listings from a portal on Tuesday night. By Thursday they need to cut to three, because three showings and lunch is a Saturday. They open each listing's tour link. Six have galleries. Three have a video and a page with the street, the walk to the door and the drive times written out. The three with video get the Saturday. The buyer fills in the enquiry form on each with Saturday's date and a morning or afternoon preference. The agents who reply by Thursday evening with a time are the ones on the schedule.

One of the three is a three-bed on a corner lot. Its video opens on the street, walks to the door, moves through the house in the order a visitor would walk it, and ends in the backyard at 5pm. The page gives the drive to the new office at 8am and the airport at any hour. On Saturday the buyer spends twenty minutes there confirming what they already believed and asks about the neighbours, the noise and the light. The offer goes in on Sunday from the airport.

Video before the flight

For a local buyer a listing video is a nice extra. For an out-of-town buyer it is the showing. Make it walk the house in the real order: street, door, entry, main living, kitchen, bedrooms, baths, outside. Keep the camera motion calm so the rooms read as rooms rather than as effects. Caption it, because it will be watched on mute in an airport. Add a voiceover if you can say in thirty seconds what the house is and what the street is like; a buyer who has never heard your voice will trust a plain one.

A handheld walkthrough, if you record one, does what the photo video cannot: it shows how the rooms connect. For a buyer who cannot visit twice, the connection between the kitchen and the family room is worth more than another photo of either.

Where remote-buyer listings fall down

  • Wide-lens interiors with no reference. The out-of-town buyer has no way to check the scale until they arrive, and arriving to a smaller house is the worst outcome for everyone. Include a frame with a door or a person-height object, and keep the wide shots honest.
  • No street. A local can drive past. An out-of-town buyer who cannot see the street assumes the worst.
  • Slow replies. The buyer is scheduling a trip. A reply on Monday to a Thursday enquiry is a reply to someone who has already booked three other showings.
  • Edits that will not survive the showing. Sky, lawn, declutter and staging edits all get compared with reality on the one visit the buyer can make. Compare every edit with the original before you publish, keep the disclosure on it, and ask whether the buyer will find the original at the door.
  • A gallery with no order. Thirty photos in upload order make the buyer reassemble the house in their head. Put them in walking order.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What do out-of-town buyers need from a listing that local buyers do not?

Everything a local would learn by driving past: the street from both ends, the walk from the car to the door, which way the house faces, drive times to work, the airport and the grocery store. And a video that walks the house in order, because it stands in for the first showing.

How quickly should I reply to an enquiry from an out-of-town buyer?

The same day. They are building a schedule for a short trip and the agents who reply first with a time are the ones who get on it. An enquiry form that asks for a date tells you which days they will be in town.

Is a listing video more important for remote buyers than for local ones?

Yes. For a local buyer it is a nice extra; for a remote buyer it is the showing. Walk the home in the real order, keep the camera calm, caption it for viewing on mute and keep the wide shots honest about scale.

Should I use AI photo edits on a listing aimed at out-of-town buyers?

Carefully. The buyer will compare the photos with reality on the one visit they can make, so any edit that changes what they will find at the door costs you the visit. Compare each edit with the original, keep the disclosure, and prefer edits that fix the weather over edits that change the room.
