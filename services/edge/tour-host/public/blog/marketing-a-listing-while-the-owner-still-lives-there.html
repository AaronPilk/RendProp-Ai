Marketing a listing while the owner still lives there

An occupied home needs one perfect morning for the shoot, then a plan that protects the owner's week: video first, grouped showings, tidying by hand.

The owners' cooperation lasts as long as the plan respects their week.

One perfect morning, then normal life

Owners who are told the house must look like a showroom for six weeks give up by week two. Owners who are told it must look like a showroom for one morning, and reasonably tidy for two showing windows a week, can do it. Set that expectation at the listing appointment.

The morning is the shoot. Everything the photos need happens before it: the deep clean, the decluttering, the lamps, the fresh towels. After the photos exist, the house only has to match them well enough that a buyer recognises each room, not reproduce them.

The two days before the shoot

  1. Two days out: remove. Half of what is on every surface goes into boxes in the garage or the car. Countertops down to a kettle and a bowl of fruit. Fridge door bare. Bathroom counters empty. Shoes, coats and bags out of the entry. Personal photos down; anything with a name, a school or a face comes off the walls and the fridge.
  2. One day out: clean. A cleaner if the budget allows, the owners if not. Windows inside, floors, the glass shower door, the oven front. Replace dead bulbs with matching warm ones, because mixed bulb colour is the thing that makes a tidy room photograph badly.
  3. The morning: light and last looks. Every lamp on, every blind open, beds made with the duvet pulled flat, toilet lids down, bins out of sight, pet bowls and beds in the car along with the pets. Cars off the driveway. Then the owners leave for two hours.

Tidy by hand first, tidy by AI second

The tidy the room edit exists for the last few items: a charger cable, a towel left on a rail, a bin that was missed. It is not a substitute for the two days of preparation, because an edit that removes a counter full of appliances has to invent the wall and the counter behind them, and the result will not match the kitchen the buyer walks into. Use it sparingly, compare every edit against the original before you keep it, and remember it carries a disclosure. The full logic is in tidy the room: decluttering a listing photo without misleading buyers.

The test for any edit on an occupied home is simple: when the buyer stands in this room next Saturday, will it look like the photo? If the owners cannot keep it that way for a showing, the edit has over-promised.

The colonial on Orchard: Wednesday morning

A three-bed colonial, a couple with two school-age kids and a large dog, listing planned for a Friday. The shoot was booked for Wednesday at nine, after the school run. The prep list went out the Thursday before. On Monday evening the owners boxed the toys, the fridge art and the countertop appliances into the garage. Tuesday a cleaner did the floors, the bathrooms and the windows. Wednesday morning the owners made the beds, turned on every lamp, put the dog and his bed in the car, and drove to a coffee shop.

The photographer had the house to himself for an hour and forty minutes: landscape photos of every room for the listing video, portrait frames of the kitchen, the family room and the back deck for the social cut, the front from the street and a return at dusk for the twilight. One frame, the kids' bathroom, had a bath toy on the edge of the tub that nobody had seen; the agent tried the tidy the room edit on it, compared it with the original, confirmed the tub edge and the tile were unchanged, and kept it with its disclosure. Everything else was as shot.

By Wednesday evening the toys were back on the family room floor. The listing went live Friday with the video on the page. Showings were grouped into Saturday mornings and Tuesday and Thursday evenings, and the owners' job before each one was a twenty-minute reset: surfaces, beds, dog in the car. The house looked like a showroom for one morning and like a home for the rest of the month, and nobody who visited felt misled.

Managing the showings themselves, with the owner's routine in mind, is covered in showings for an occupied home.

Occupied-home mistakes

Shooting on a weekday evening after work. The light is gone, the kitchen has been used, and everyone is tired. Mid-morning after the owners have left is the slot.

Publishing the household. A child's name on a bedroom door, a work lanyard, a wedding photo, a calendar with appointments. All of it is private and none of it sells the house. What stays off the page is in privacy on a property page.

Letting the edit do the decluttering. The photos stop matching the house, and the buyer notices at the first showing.

Showings at any hour. Owners who are asked to leave the house at an hour's notice three times a week stop saying yes by week three. Windows protect the listing as much as the owners.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long does an occupied home need to stay photo-ready?

One morning. Prepare over the two days before the shoot, shoot everything in a single visit, and let the house go back to normal. After that it only needs a twenty-minute reset before each grouped showing window, not a permanent showroom standard.

What should owners remove before the shoot?

About half of what is on every surface, plus anything personal: photos, names, school art, mail, work badges, medication. Countertops down to a couple of items, fridge door bare, entry clear of shoes and coats, pets and their beds out of the house for the morning.

Can I use the tidy the room edit instead of decluttering?

Only for small leftovers like a cable or a towel. An edit that clears a full counter has to invent what was behind it and will not match the room a buyer walks into. Declutter by hand, use the edit sparingly, compare it against the original, and keep the disclosure.

How do I keep showings manageable for people living in the house?

Put the listing video on the property page so buyers tour from a phone first, then group in-person visits into two or three fixed windows a week. Owners can plan around Saturday morning and two weekday evenings; they cannot plan around an hour's notice at random.
