Marketing a home on a busy road

Do not hide the road. Show the setback, the fence and the back yard, say what the location buys, price for it, and shoot and show in quieter hours.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Sell what the road pays for

A house on a busy road is cheaper than the same house on a quiet one, and the buyers who end up happy there are the ones who did that trade on purpose: more house or more lot for the money, a short drive to work, a bus stop at the end of the drive, a street where a home business can have a sign. The listing's job is to show that trade clearly so the buyer who wants it recognises it.

That means the copy names the location advantages specifically, the lot size sits near the top of the details, and the price is set against comparable homes on comparable roads, not against the cul-de-sac behind. A busy-road house priced like a quiet-street house sits, and sitting is worse for it than for most listings because the price cut confirms what buyers suspected.

Photographing the front honestly

  • Shoot from the property, not from the road. A frame from the far side of a four-lane road puts the road in the foreground. A frame from the front path or the edge of the lawn, looking at the house, puts the house in the foreground and the setback becomes visible as depth.
  • Shoot in a quieter hour. Early on a weekend morning, traffic is light and the frame is honest; it is the same road, just not at rush hour. Waiting for a gap in traffic is fine. Editing cars out of the road is not, because a photo of an empty arterial is a photo of a road that does not exist.
  • Show the buffer. The hedge, the fence, the mature trees, the depth of the front lawn. These are the features a busy-road buyer is looking for, so frame them as features.
  • Include one frame that shows the road. Not as the main photo, but somewhere in the gallery, so nobody can say they were not shown. The honest frame is also the one that makes the rest of the gallery credible.

The general rules for the front exterior still apply, and they are in exterior photos for a listing.

The back yard is the listing

On a busy road, the private side of the property is where the buyer will spend their time, and it is where the gallery and the video should spend theirs. Lead the gallery with the back of the house from the garden if it is strong, or with the best interior that faces the back. Open the video on the back yard. Put the front exterior third or fourth.

Inside, the rooms that face away from the road are the ones to photograph in depth: the kitchen at the back, the primary bedroom over the garden, the family room opening onto the patio. Shoot them with the windows showing green, because the view from those rooms is the argument. The approach for a property where the lot outshines the house is in marketing a listing with a great yard and an average house, and a busy-road listing borrows most of it.

The three-bed on Route 9: a shoot and a showing plan

A three-bed ranch on a four-lane road, set well back behind a tall hedge, on a lot nearly twice the size of anything in the subdivision behind it, with a fenced back yard and a detached workshop. Previous listing had led with a front photo taken from across the road at five in the afternoon, cars in the foreground, and had sat for months.

The relaunch shoot was Sunday at seven in the morning. The photographer shot the front from the lawn with the hedge on both sides of the frame and the road barely visible through the gate. Then the back: the house from the far fence, the patio, the workshop, and the kitchen and primary with the garden in the windows. One frame of the road from the end of the drive went into the gallery, eleventh of twenty. The video opened on the back garden, moved through the house from the back door, and finished on the front from the lawn. The voiceover said it in one sentence: set back from the road behind a hedge, on a lot twice the size of the neighbours', with a workshop.

Showings were scheduled for Saturday and Sunday mornings and weekday evenings after the commute, when the road was quiet, and the agent met buyers at the back gate rather than the front door. The buyer was a woodworker who wanted the workshop and the lot, had seen the road frame, and had priced the trade before he arrived.

What goes wrong on busy-road listings

Hiding the road entirely. The buyer discovers it on arrival and distrusts everything else.

Editing out the traffic. An empty road at midday is a photo of a different road. Shoot early instead.

Leading with the front. The private side is the product.

Showings at rush hour. The one hour the house is at its worst is the one hour most agents schedule.

Pricing against the quiet street. The road is in the price or the listing sits.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should the listing photos show the busy road?

Yes, in one honest frame placed somewhere in the gallery, not as the main photo. Every buyer will drive up the road to the showing, and the ones who were shown it in the listing arrive having already priced it. Hiding it makes the rest of the gallery suspect.

Can I edit the cars out of the road in the front photo?

No. An empty arterial at midday is a road that does not exist, and the buyer will see the real one within the week. Shoot early on a weekend morning when traffic is light, from the property rather than from across the road, and let the setback show as depth.

What should lead the gallery for a home on a busy road?

The private side: the back of the house from the garden, or the best interior that faces away from the road with green in the windows. Open the video the same way. Put the front exterior third or fourth.

When should I schedule showings for a busy-road home?

Weekend mornings and weekday evenings after the commute, when the road is quiet. Avoid rush hour, which is the one time the house is at its worst. Meeting buyers at the back gate instead of the front door changes the first impression.
