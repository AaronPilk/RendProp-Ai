Open house marketing from the photos you already have

You do not need a second shoot to market an open house. The listing photos become the announcement video, the posts, the email and the sign.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. An open house does not need new photos. The set from the listing shoot becomes a short announcement video with the date on it, a story sequence, a text to everyone who has enquired, one email, and the sign at the kerb. Every piece carries the same property page link, and the enquiry form on that page catches the people who cannot make Sunday.

You do not need a second photo shoot to market an open house. The photos from the listing shoot already show the home at its best; the open house just needs a date, a time and a link attached to them. One announcement video, one story sequence, one text, one email and one sign, all pointing to the same property page, is a full campaign.

The mistake is treating the open house as a separate event with separate content. It is the same listing with a deadline. The deadline is what makes people act, and the photos they have already half-noticed are what remind them which house it is.

One listing, one Sunday, five pieces

Take a 3-bed on Birch Lane, shot on a Tuesday and published the same evening. The open house is Sunday, 1 to 3. Here is what comes out of that Tuesday set without picking up a camera again.

  • The announcement video. A vertical social video from the portrait photos, eight to ten scenes, with the date and time said in the voiceover and shown in the captions. Post it Wednesday evening and again Saturday morning.
  • The story sequence. Four or five single photos posted as stories on Friday and Saturday: the front, the kitchen, the back garden, the main bedroom, then a plain text frame with the hours and the link.
  • The text. To everyone who has already enquired about the home or asked about the area: one line, the hours, the property page link. Sent Thursday.
  • The email. To your database on Friday morning: the main photo, the four facts, the hours, the link. Short.
  • The sign. The open house rider on the yard sign, and a QR code on the flyer at the door that opens the property page.

Nothing in that list requires a new asset. The video is a different cut of the same photos. The sign uses the same link as the email. The buyer who sees the story on Friday and walks in on Sunday has seen the kitchen three times before they stand in it.

Why the same photos work harder the second time

Repetition is the point. A buyer scrolling on Wednesday registers a white kitchen and keeps going. On Friday the story shows the same kitchen with a time attached, and now it is a plan. By Sunday the house is familiar before they park. Familiarity is what gets a car to turn into the street rather than past it.

The link does the second job. Anyone who taps through from the video, the story, the text or the email lands on the same property page with the gallery, the video and the enquiry form. The form asks what date they want to visit, so the person who cannot make Sunday tells you so instead of disappearing.

Which photos to pull for the announcement

Not the whole set. For an open house video you want the photos that answer the question a passer-by has: what is it, where is it, what is the one thing I would go to see. For the Birch Lane 3-bed that is the front exterior, the kitchen from the doorway, the living room toward the garden, the main bedroom, the back garden, and the street. Six scenes, the date on the first and the last.

If you have a twilight edit or a virtually staged room in the set, use it only if it is labelled and only if the real room on Sunday will not contradict it badly. An empty living room that was staged in the photos is fine when the label is there and you mention it at the door. A twilight exterior that implies garden lights the house does not have is a problem at 7 p.m. when the lights are not there.

Where this goes wrong on the week

  • The same three photos everywhere. Front, kitchen, done. By Saturday the audience has seen them five times and stops reacting. Rotate: lead the video with the kitchen, the stories with the garden, the email with the front.
  • The hours without the link. A post that says Sunday 1 to 3 and nothing else leaves the viewer with a search to do. Every piece needs the property page link where it can be tapped.
  • The link without the hours. The opposite failure. The page shows the home; it does not say when the door is open. Put the hours in the post, the caption, the voiceover and the email.
  • Posting once. One announcement on Wednesday reaches whoever was looking on Wednesday. Wednesday, Friday, Saturday is the minimum.
  • A new shoot for the sake of it. A rushed Saturday reshoot in flat light usually looks worse than the Tuesday set and costs you the afternoon you needed to prepare the house.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Do I need new photos to promote an open house?

No. The listing shoot already shows the home at its best, and the open house only adds a date, a time and a link. A new announcement video cut from the same photos, with the hours in the voiceover and captions, is enough. Save the reshoot for when the house or the season has actually changed.

How many times should I post about one open house?

Three times at minimum: the announcement midweek, a story sequence on Friday, and a reminder on Saturday morning. Use different photos as the lead each time so the audience does not tune out, and put the hours and the property page link in every post.

Should the open house post link to the property page or to the portal listing?

The property page. It shows your gallery, your video and your contact card, and its enquiry form asks when the visitor wants to come, which catches the people who cannot make the open house. A portal page shows competing homes and may route the enquiry elsewhere.

Can I use a virtually staged photo in the open house announcement?

Yes, if it carries its label and the real room on the day will not contradict it. Mention at the door that the living room was virtually staged in the photos. Avoid any edit that implies something the visitor will not find, such as exterior lighting that does not exist.
