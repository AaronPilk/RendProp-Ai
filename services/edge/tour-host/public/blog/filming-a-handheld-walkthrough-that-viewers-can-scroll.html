Filming a handheld walkthrough that viewers can scroll

A scrollable walkthrough is one slow, level, continuous walk on the wide lens, where every stretch stands on its own. Route, pace, holds and light.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Film one continuous walk from the front door to the best room, slowly, with the phone level and the wide lens on. Hold still for a few seconds in each room and at each turn, because a viewer scrolling through the footage will land on those holds. Why scrollable changes how you film

A walkthrough that plays start to finish is watched like a film: the viewer sits through the hallway to get to the kitchen. A walkthrough that can be scrolled is used like a map: the viewer drags to the part they care about and stops. That means every few seconds of footage has to work as a still. If you were moving fast through a door at the moment they stop, they see a blur and leave.

So the walk is slower than feels natural, the holds are longer, and the route is the order a buyer would want to jump to, not the order that is convenient.

Plan the route first

Walk it once without the phone. Decide:

  • Where it starts. Outside the front door, so the viewer arrives. Or at the street if the approach matters.
  • The order. Entry, main living, kitchen, dining, then bedrooms, then outside. The same walk a showing takes. Do not double back.
  • Where it ends. The best room, the deck, the view. The last thing on screen is what they remember.
  • The turns. Every doorway and corner is a place where the picture will swing. Know where they are so you can slow down before each one.

One take is the goal. If you stop and restart, the viewer feels the cut as a jump in the map.

Pace, holds and the turns

Walk at half speed. Slower than you would walk a client. Your legs will want to speed up; the footage will punish it.

Hold in every room. Stop in the doorway, hold the frame for three to five seconds so the whole room is visible, then move. Those holds are the frames a scrolling viewer will land on.

Hold the turn. Before you turn through a doorway, stop. Turn the phone slowly, over two or three seconds, with your body rather than your wrist. Then stop again before you walk. A fast turn is the single most common fault.

Keep it level. Chest height, horizon flat, no tilting up to the ceiling or down to the floor. If you need to show a high ceiling, stop and tilt slowly, then come back.

Light and the hour

Film when the main rooms are lit by the windows, not by lamps, and not when the sun is straight into the lens. For most homes that is mid-morning or mid-afternoon. Open the curtains, turn on the lights anyway so the corners are not dark, and walk away from the brightest window rather than toward it where you can. The wide lens handles rooms well but blows out a window it is pointed at.

Tidy, then film

Everything in the path is in the footage: the shoes by the door, the dish rack, the bathroom counter. Do the same tidy you would do for a photographer, then open every interior door so rooms read as connected. Pets out. Fans off, because the wide lens shows the blur. Toilet lids down.

A worked example: the open-plan bungalow

A two-bed bungalow where the kitchen, dining and living room are one space and the back wall is glass onto a garden. The agent plans: start at the front door, hold, walk into the open space and hold at the point where all three areas are visible, walk slowly to the glass wall, hold, turn to show the kitchen from the garden side, hold, back through to the hall, hold at each bedroom door, hold in the bathroom doorway, then out the back to the garden and end on the house from outside. One take, about three minutes, filmed at ten in the morning with the garden lit. A viewer scrolling lands on nine clean holds.

What ruins a scrollable walkthrough

  • Walking at normal speed. Every scroll stop lands on motion blur.
  • Wrist turns through doorways. The picture swings and the viewer loses where they are.
  • Narrating square footage. If you talk, say what you would say on a showing, briefly, or say nothing. The footage is the point.
  • Filming at noon in summer. White windows and dark rooms.
  • Two takes stitched together. The jump breaks the map.
  • A four-minute walk posted to a feed. This is for the property page and for buyers who asked, not for a reel.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How slow should I walk when filming a walkthrough?

About half the speed you would walk a client through the home. It will feel too slow while you are doing it and look right on playback. Stop fully before and after every doorway and corner, and hold for three to five seconds in each room.

Should I film the walkthrough in one take?

Yes. A viewer who scrolls through the footage uses it like a map of the home, and a cut between two takes feels like a jump in that map. Plan the route first, walk it once without the phone, then record it end to end.

Which lens should I use for a walkthrough?

The wide lens, which on most iPhones is 0.5x. It shows whole rooms from a doorway and handles tight spaces. Keep it pointed away from the brightest window where you can, because the wide lens blows out a window it faces directly.

Should I talk while filming?

Either say nothing or say what you would say on a showing, briefly, at the holds. Do not read the listing sheet. The footage is the information, and a scrolling viewer will not hear most of what you say anyway.
