AI-edited listing photos: what to disclose and where

Label every AI edit a buyer would notice at the showing, and put the label on the photo, in the remarks, on the property page and in the social post.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Disclose every AI edit that changes what a buyer would see standing in the room, and put that disclosure where the photo is: on the image or its caption, in the listing remarks, and on the property page. Rules differ by MLS and state, so check your MLS's current rules and ask your broker about wording. The practice that holds up when someone complains is simple: label the photo, keep the original, and be able to show both within a minute.

Which edits need a disclosure

The test is not “did AI touch it” but “would a buyer be surprised at the showing.” Sorting the common edits by that test:

  • Virtual staging. Furniture that is not there. Label it every time.
  • Tidy the room. A removed laundry basket is harmless. A removed wall bracket, a removed water stain or a removed radiator is not. If the edit took away anything fixed to the property, label it.
  • Twilight conversion. The light changed, and often the windows now glow. A buyer arriving at 2pm on a Tuesday will not see that. Label it.
  • Blue sky. Weather, not property. Many agents treat it as a light-touch edit, but it is still an alteration. Check your MLS's current rules on whether it needs a note; a caption costs nothing either way.
  • Green lawn. Closer to the line than people think. A brown lawn in August may be a dead lawn, and a dead lawn is a condition of the property. Label it.
  • Custom prompts. Whatever the prompt did, treat the result as altered and say so.

When in doubt, label. A label costs nothing. A buyer who feels misled costs you the deal, the referral and sometimes a formal complaint.

Where the disclosure goes

One disclosure in one place is not enough, because photos travel. The MLS photo gets syndicated to portals. The social reel gets screenshotted. The property page gets texted from one phone to another. The disclosure has to ride with the image.

On the photo or its caption

This is the one that matters most. A caption reading “Virtually staged” or “Twilight edit: sky and lighting altered” under the photo survives most of the places the photo goes. If your MLS supports photo captions, use them. If it does not, a small on-image label is the next best thing.

In the listing remarks

One plain sentence: “Some photos have been virtually staged or digitally altered; originals available on request.” It catches the buyer who reads the text and skips the captions.

On the property page

If the listing has its own website, the gallery there should carry the same labels as the MLS. Buyers compare. A staged photo on the page and an empty room on the portal, with no label on either, is the kind of mismatch that starts arguments.

In the social post

A reel built from edited photos needs a line in the caption. Short is fine: “Photos include virtual staging.” The post on social disclosures has wording you can copy.

A worked example: the cul-de-sac three-bed

A 3-bed in a cul-de-sac, listed in late August. The lawn is brown, the shoot day was overcast, and the living room is empty because the sellers moved out two weeks earlier. You run three edits: green lawn on the front exterior, blue sky on the same shot, and staging in the living room.

  1. Front exterior caption: “Lawn and sky digitally altered.” The remarks add “exterior photo altered; lawn is dormant as of the listing date.” Honest, and it stops the buyer feeling tricked when they pull up.
  2. Living room caption: “Virtually staged.” The empty original sits two slots later in the gallery, so the buyer sees the room both ways.
  3. Remarks: “Some photos virtually staged or digitally altered; originals available.” One sentence.

Total time to add all three: under five minutes. The alternative is a buyer walking into a bare room on Saturday and asking the showing agent where the furniture went.

Where disclosures fall through

  • Disclosure only in the remarks. Portals and social posts strip the remarks. The photo goes out alone.
  • A vague blanket line. “Photos enhanced” tells the buyer nothing. Name the edit.
  • Labels dropped on repost. The photographer labels the staging, the agent crops the label out for Instagram. Now the agent owns the problem.
  • No original on hand. When a buyer asks what the room really looks like, “I'll get back to you” is a weak answer. Keep the untouched file where you can reach it from your phone.
  • Treating removal as safe. Removing a thing is as much an alteration as adding one. A decluttered counter is fine; a removed ceiling crack is not.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Do I need to disclose a blue sky edit on a listing photo?

Treat it as an alteration and add a short caption. Rules differ by MLS and state, so check your MLS's current rules on whether a sky swap needs a note. A caption costs nothing and removes the question.

Is one sentence in the listing remarks enough?

On its own, no. Remarks get stripped when photos are syndicated or screenshotted. Put the label on the photo or its caption as well, and repeat it on the property page and in any social post that uses the image.

What wording should a disclosure use?

Name the edit plainly: “Virtually staged”, “Twilight edit: lighting altered”, “Lawn digitally altered”. Avoid vague phrases like “photos enhanced”. Ask your broker if your office has preferred wording.

Should the original photo also be in the listing?

For staged rooms it helps to include the empty original a few slots after the staged version. At minimum keep the untouched original somewhere you can reach from your phone, so a buyer who asks gets an answer on the spot.
