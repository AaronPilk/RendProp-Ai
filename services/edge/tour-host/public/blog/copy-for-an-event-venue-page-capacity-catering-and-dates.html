Copy for an event venue page: capacity, catering and dates

A venue page states capacity by layout, the catering arrangement and how dates work, all on the first screen. Everything else is the room.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A venue page should state capacity by layout (seated, standing, with a dance floor), the catering arrangement (in-house, approved list or bring your own) and how dates work (how to check, how a hold works, when a deposit is due), all on the first screen. A planner with a date and a headcount decides in seconds; the paragraph about the room comes after they know it fits.

A venue page should state three things before anything else: capacity by layout, the catering arrangement, and how dates work. “Seats 140 at rounds, 180 standing, 110 with a dance floor. Catering from our approved list of six caterers; outside caterers by arrangement. Check a date on the form below; holds last seven days and a deposit confirms.” A planner with a date and a headcount can decide in that paragraph whether to read on. The description of the room comes after they know it fits.

Venue copy usually fails by leading with atmosphere. “An unforgettable setting for your special day” is the first line on hundreds of venue pages, and it tells a planner with 160 guests on a Saturday in June nothing they can use. Capacity, catering and dates are the filter. Pass it and the atmosphere paragraph gets read with interest; skip it and the planner leaves to find the number somewhere else.

Capacity, written so it survives the site visit

One number is never enough. The same room holds different crowds depending on how it is set, and a planner will ask about their layout. Give the numbers by configuration, and say what each assumes.

  • Seated at round tables of eight: 140.
  • Seated at rounds with a dance floor and a band: 110.
  • Theatre-style rows: 200.
  • Standing reception: 180.
  • Ceremony in the garden, weather permitting: 150 chairs; the same ceremony indoors: 120.

If a number depends on something (a tent, a second room, the fire code), say so in the line. A planner who discovers at the walkthrough that 180 standing assumed the terrace, in October, will not forgive the page.

Catering, in one of three shapes

Most venues run one of three catering models, and the copy should say which in a sentence.

  • In-house. “All food and drink is from our kitchen. Sample menus and per-person pricing are on request; the bar can be open, hosted to a limit, or cash.”
  • Approved list. “Catering is from our list of six approved caterers, who know the kitchen and the load-in. Outside caterers are considered by arrangement and carry a kitchen fee.”
  • Bring your own. “You choose and book your own caterer. The kitchen has a walk-in, two ovens and a prep line; caterers must carry their own insurance.”

Say what the bar arrangement is in the same paragraph, because it is the next question, and whether the venue holds the licence or the caterer does.

Dates: how to ask, how a hold works, what confirms it

Tell the planner exactly how to find out whether their date is free, what happens when they ask, and what makes it theirs. “Ask about a date on the form below with your guest count and the kind of event. You will hear back within two business days. A first hold lasts seven days; a signed agreement and a deposit confirm the date.” Only promise a response time you keep. If pricing is seasonal or by day of the week, say that it is, even if you do not publish the figures.

Then, and only then, the room

Now describe the space, with the facts a planner uses: ceiling height, natural light and which way the windows face, the sound system and whether bands may bring their own, load-in access and the lift, parking count, the rain plan, the accessible entrance, what time the music stops. Two short paragraphs. Save adjectives; the gallery and the video carry the feel. The post on what planners want to see in a venue tour covers the visual side.

The first screen, rewritten

Before: “An unforgettable setting for your special day. Our versatile space accommodates up to 300 guests and offers flexible catering options to suit every taste. Contact us to discuss availability.”

After: “Seats 140 at rounds, 110 with a dance floor, 180 standing. Catering is from our approved list of six caterers; outside caterers by arrangement with a kitchen fee. The bar is ours, open or hosted to a limit. Ask about a date on the form below with your guest count; holds last seven days and a signed agreement with deposit confirms. Twelve-foot ceilings, west-facing windows, a covered terrace as the rain plan, parking for 60.”

Where venue copy loses the planner

  • “Up to 300” with no layout. The number is almost always standing, and the planner is almost always seating.
  • “Flexible catering options.” It means nothing until you say which of the three models you run.
  • “Contact us for availability” with no form and no response time. Planners are comparing five venues on a Tuesday night; the one with a form wins.
  • Hiding every price. You need not publish a rate card, but say how pricing works: by season, by day, by headcount, a minimum spend.
  • The rain plan missing. For any outdoor element, say what happens if it rains, in one sentence.
  • Photos of events you did not host, or of a room that has since changed. Planners compare the walkthrough to the page.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How should a venue page state capacity?

By layout, with what each number assumes: seated at rounds, seated with a dance floor, theatre rows, standing, and ceremony indoors and out. A single maximum is almost always the standing figure, and planners are almost always seating. State any dependency, such as a tent or a second room, in the same line.

Should a venue publish pricing on its page?

You need not publish a full rate card, but say how pricing works: by season, by day of the week, by headcount, or as a minimum spend. Planners filter on that before they ask. A page that hides every number loses the comparison on a Tuesday night to the venue that explained its structure.

What should the date enquiry ask for?

The date or a range, the expected guest count, and the kind of event. With those three, you can answer in one reply. Say how long a hold lasts and what confirms the booking, and promise only a response time you keep.

How do I describe the catering arrangement clearly?

Name the model in a sentence: in-house, approved list, or bring your own. Then say who holds the bar and how it can be run. Flexible catering options is not a model; it is a phrase that sends the planner to email you the question.
