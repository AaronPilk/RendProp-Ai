Reels from vertical photos: shooting with the format in mind

Shoot six to ten rooms in portrait orientation on purpose, phone level, standing back, and the reel comes out full-frame with no crops or bars.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A reel built from portrait photos fills the phone screen with no crops or bars. Shoot the six to ten rooms that sell the home in portrait orientation on purpose: phone level, stand further back than feels natural, use the wide lens only when the room needs it, and put something in the foreground. A reel that fills the phone screen without crops, bars or stretched walls comes from photos that were shot in portrait orientation on purpose. Shoot the six to ten rooms that sell the home as vertical frames: phone level, standing further back than feels right, the wide lens only when the room needs it. Keep shooting the landscape photos as well, because the 16:9 video is built from them. Twenty extra minutes on the day saves a reel that looks like a crop.

Why vertical has to be shot, not cut

A landscape photo cropped to 9:16 keeps the middle third and throws away the sides. In a kitchen that is the island or the window. In a living room it is the width that made it feel generous. The result is a reel of narrow slices that makes every room look smaller than it is, which is the opposite of the job. A vertical frame shot in the room shows floor and ceiling and keeps the width the lens actually saw.

The portrait shot list

Six to ten frames. More than that and the reel runs long; fewer and it repeats.

  • The front exterior, from across the street, with the whole roofline and some sky.
  • The kitchen, from the doorway or the far corner, with the island or the run of counters leading into the frame.
  • The main living space, from the entry, with a window on the far wall.
  • The primary bedroom, from the door, bed to the side.
  • The primary bath, if it is a selling point.
  • The one feature: the staircase, the porch, the view, the workshop, the pantry.
  • The back of the house from the yard, or the yard from the deck.
  • One detail that reads tall: a window seat, a fireplace wall, a stack of shelves.

Vertical frames favour tall things. Staircases, double-height rooms, tall windows, trees in the yard, a pantry with shelves to the ceiling. If the home has any of those, they are the frames that will look best in a reel.

A townhouse in twenty-five minutes, both ways

A two-storey townhouse, three beds, with a staircase at the entry and a deck off the kitchen. The photographer shoots the landscape photos first, room by room, forty minutes. Then the portrait pass.

Front exterior from the opposite sidewalk, portrait, two frames. Inside, the staircase from the front door, which lands in vertical the way it never does in landscape orientation. Kitchen from the dining end so the deck door is in the frame. Living room from the foot of the stairs. Primary bedroom from the door. The deck looking back at the kitchen through the glass. The second bedroom with the tall window. Eight portrait frames in a little over twenty minutes, standing in nearly the same spots as the landscape photo pass but a step further back.

That one step back is the habit. Portrait frames show more height and less width, so to keep the room's width you move back and let the lens take in the ceiling line. If there is no room to step back, the wide lens comes out, and the walls at the edges need checking for bending.

Technique that keeps verticals honest

  • Level the phone. Tilting up to get the ceiling makes the walls lean in at the top. Keep the phone upright and step back instead.
  • Height at chest, not eye. A slightly lower camera keeps the floor and ceiling in proportion and stops the ceiling from dominating.
  • Foreground. A counter edge, a chair arm, the newel post. It gives the frame depth and gives the camera motion something to pass.
  • Wide lens sparingly. At 0.5x, the edges of a vertical frame stretch noticeably. Use it for the bathroom and the small bedroom, not the living room.
  • Same light as the 16:9 set. Shoot the portrait frame of each room right after the landscape photo so the exposure matches and the reel does not flicker between warm and cold.

Where vertical shoots go wrong

  • Cropping the landscape photos anyway. The kitchen loses its island and the reel looks like a corridor tour.
  • Tilting for the ceiling. Leaning walls look like a cheap lens even on a good phone.
  • Only shooting portrait. Then there is no 16:9 video and the gallery is nothing but tall frames. Both sets, every time; the reasoning is in landscape photos or portrait photos.
  • Cutting counters off at the bottom. Check the bottom edge of every vertical frame. A counter cut mid-height reads as a mistake.
  • Ten frames of the same room. One portrait frame per room, two for the feature. The reel needs variety more than coverage.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How many portrait photos does a reel need?

Six to ten. Fewer and the reel repeats rooms; more and it runs long for a feed. One frame per room and two for the home's standout feature is a good split.

Can I just crop my landscape photos into vertical?

You can, but the crop keeps the middle third and loses the island, the window or the width that made the room feel large. Shooting a portrait frame in each room takes a few minutes and avoids that.

Should I use the 0.5x wide lens for vertical photos?

Only when the room is too small to step back in, such as a bathroom or a small bedroom. In a vertical frame the wide lens stretches the edges noticeably, so check the walls for bending before you keep the shot.

Do I still need landscape photos if I am shooting for reels?

Yes. The 16:9 listing video is built from the landscape photos. Shoot both orientations in each room, one after the other, so the light matches.
