Word-by-word captions: why listing reels need them

Many people scroll with the sound off. Word-by-word captions carry your voiceover to them, hold the eye on the photo and keep a short script readable.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A lot of social video is watched with the sound off, so a reel with no captions is a silent slideshow to those viewers. Word-by-word captions show each word as it is spoken, which keeps the eye on the frame and makes a short script readable at feed speed. Block subtitles work for a long video someone chose to watch; for a 25-second reel, word by word is the right choice.

Word-by-word captions put each word on screen as it is spoken, which keeps the eye on the photo and makes a 25-second script readable at the speed people scroll. Sound off is the default, not the exception

Think about where reels get watched. On a train. In a waiting room. On the couch next to someone watching television. At a desk. In most of those places the sound is off and will stay off. The voiceover you spent six minutes recording reaches the people who turned sound on and nobody else.

Captions close that gap. They are not an accessibility extra or a nice-to-have; for a reel, they are the script's only route to most of the audience.

Why word by word beats a block of text

A subtitle block shows a full sentence at once. The viewer reads it in a second and then has nothing to do for the next two seconds while the photo drifts, so they scroll. Word-by-word captions reveal the sentence as it is spoken. The eye follows the words, the words sit on the photo, and the photo gets watched for the full three seconds it is on screen.

There is a second effect. A sentence arriving one word at a time reads like speech. It carries rhythm and emphasis that a block does not. The line the evening sun comes straight down that hallway lands differently when hallway arrives on its own.

Writing a script that captions well

Captions are made from the script, so the script decides whether they read well.

  • Short sentences. Eight to twelve words. A 25-word sentence captioned one word at a time has the viewer waiting on the verb.
  • No clauses that need commas. The caption cannot show the comma in a useful way. Split the sentence.
  • Concrete nouns. Island, deck, oak, train. Nouns read at a glance. Adjectives like spacious read as noise.
  • Numbers as digits. Three bedrooms is fine spoken; on screen, 3 bedrooms is read faster. Write the script the way you want it shown.
  • Local spelling. The street name will be on screen. Spell it the way the sign does.

A worked example: the same reel with and without captions

A two-bed with a balcony over a park. The reel opens on the balcony view, the voice says two bedrooms, and this is the view from the sofa, and the next five photos follow with one sentence each.

With sound on, the reel works. With sound off and no captions, the viewer sees a balcony, a living room, a kitchen, a bedroom, a bathroom and a building. They do not know where it is, what it costs, or that the open house is Saturday. They scroll at the bathroom.

With word-by-word captions, the same muted viewer reads two bedrooms on the balcony, reads the neighbourhood on the living room, reads open Saturday one to three on the last frame. They know enough to tap the link. Same photos, same voice, different outcome.

Caption mistakes that undo the work

  • Captions that cover the feature. If the words sit over the island in the kitchen shot, the viewer sees words, not the island. Keep the lead frame clean enough that the subject shows.
  • A typo in the street name. Locals notice and comment on it, and that is the comment everyone reads.
  • Captions that do not match the voice. If you edit the script after recording, the captions and the audio drift apart. Record the final script.
  • Relying on captions and dropping the voice. The people with sound on still want a voice. Captions are in addition, not instead.

Captions on the horizontal listing video

The 16:9 video on the property page is different. A buyer opened the page on purpose, often on a laptop, often with sound available. Captions still help there, but the video is longer and the viewer has chosen to watch, so the pressure to hold every second is lower. The rule for reels is firm; the rule for the page video is a preference.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Are captions necessary if the reel has a voiceover?

Yes. Many viewers watch with the sound off, and for them the voiceover does not exist. Word-by-word captions carry the same script to those viewers and keep their eyes on the photo while it plays.

What is the difference between word-by-word captions and subtitles?

Subtitles show a full sentence or phrase at once. Word-by-word captions reveal each word as it is spoken, which reads like speech and keeps the viewer watching the frame instead of finishing the line early and scrolling.

How should I write a script so the captions read well?

Short sentences of eight to twelve words, no clauses that need commas, concrete nouns, digits for numbers, and the street name spelled the way locals spell it. If a sentence makes sense read one word at a time, it will caption well.

Do I need captions on the property page video too?

They help, but the pressure is lower. A buyer who opened the page chose to watch and often has sound available. Captions on the 9:16 reel are essential; on the 16:9 page video they are a good habit.
