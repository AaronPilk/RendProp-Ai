Marketing a 55-plus community home

A 55-plus community home sells on single-level living, a step-free entry, the shower and the clubhouse. Describe the property; quote the community's rules.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A 55-plus community home sells on the practical things the buyer and often their adult children are checking: single-level living, a step-free entry, a walk-in shower, the path from the garage to the kitchen, low maintenance and what the clubhouse and the calendar offer. Describe the property and the community's facilities, state the community's age rules exactly as the community states them, ask your broker how to phrase anything about who may live there, and keep the page readable at a glance.

A home in a 55-plus community sells on practical facts: everything on one level, an entry without steps, a walk-in shower, a short path from the garage to the kitchen, a yard someone else mows, and a clubhouse with a calendar on it. The buyer is checking those, and so are the adult children who often find the listing first and send it on. Describe the property and the community's facilities in plain terms. State the community's age rules only as the community itself states them, and ask your broker how to word anything about who may live there. The page should be readable in a minute on a phone held at arm's length.

What this buyer is checking

Four things before anything else. Can I live on one floor, and are there steps anywhere between the car and the bed. Is the shower something I can walk into. How much of the outside is mine to look after. And what will I do on a Tuesday: the pool, the pickleball courts, the woodshop, the card room, the trips. Behind those, the fee and what it covers, the distance to a hospital and a grocery store in miles, and whether a visiting grandchild can stay for a week, which is a community rule and not yours to state. Many of these buyers are leaving a larger house, and the downsizer post covers the emotional side of that move; this one is about what to show.

The shot list, built around the walk through the house

  1. Front exterior from the street, with the step-free entry visible. If there is a step, show it rather than hiding it behind a planter.
  2. The entry from the door, and the width of the hall.
  3. The garage interior and the door into the house, with the threshold visible. This is a frame most listings skip and this buyer wants.
  4. Kitchen, with counter height and the distance to the table readable.
  5. Living and dining, bright, blinds up, furniture spaced.
  6. Primary bedroom and the primary bath, with the shower entrance, the bench if there is one and the grab bars if they are installed. Shoot the shower from the door so the step-in height is visible.
  7. The second bedroom or den, set up as the guest room or office it is.
  8. Laundry on the main level.
  9. Patio or lanai, looking out, and whatever it looks out on.
  10. The clubhouse, pool and courts, empty, with the community's permission, and the walk or drive from the house to them.

For the video, give it fewer, calmer frames rather than every angle, and if you add a voiceover, speak slowly and plainly. Word-by-word captions matter here: many viewers watch without sound. Portrait frames of the kitchen, the shower and the patio make a vertical social video that adult children share.

What goes wrong

  • Describing the buyer instead of the house. Lines about who the home is perfect for are a problem. Describe the property, the facilities and the community's stated rules; the post on fair housing language has the basics, and your broker has the final word.
  • Stating the age rule in your own words. Quote the community's published statement and say it is the community's. Do not interpret it.
  • Hiding a step. It is the first thing the buyer will test on arrival.
  • A tidy edit that removes a grab bar or a ramp. Those are features. Compare against the original and keep the disclosure; better still, leave the frame alone.
  • Tiny text and a long page. Short paragraphs, numbers, headings a person can skim.

What the page should say

  • Single-level or not, with the count of any steps inside and at the entry.
  • Shower type, bench, bars, door widths if the seller has measured them.
  • What the fee is, as the community states it, how often, and where its documents say what it covers. Do not summarize the coverage yourself.
  • Exterior maintenance handled by the community or by the owner, as the documents say.
  • Facilities by name, and the calendar's existence rather than a promise about it.
  • The community's own published statement about age, quoted, with a note to confirm with the community. Nothing more.
  • Distances in miles to a hospital, a pharmacy, a grocery store and the nearest airport.
  • Guest, rental and pet rules described as the community's current rules with a note to confirm.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet go in the details fields; everything above goes in the text.

Worked example: a 2-bed villa with a step-free entry

A 2-bed, 2-bath attached villa, all one level, with a step-free front entry, a zero-threshold door from the garage, a walk-in shower with a bench in the primary bath, a screened lanai facing a pond and a clubhouse a four-minute walk away with a pool, courts and a card room. The agent shoots on a Tuesday at 9 a.m. with the community's agreement to photograph the clubhouse before it opens: the front with the flat entry, the hall, the garage with the threshold, the kitchen, the living room, the primary bedroom, the shower from the door with the bench visible, the den set up as a guest room, the laundry, the lanai and the pond, then the clubhouse, pool and courts empty, and the path from the villa to them. Fifteen landscape frames make the listing video, with a recorded voiceover. The page reads: single level, no steps at entry or inside, walk-in shower with bench, fee as stated by the community with documents on request, exterior maintenance by the community per its documents, the community's published age statement quoted, hospital 6 miles, grocery 2 miles, guest and rental rules per the community's current documents. The lanai frame is the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How do I mention the age restriction on the page?

Quote the community's own published statement, say it is the community's, and add a note to confirm with the community. Do not interpret it or describe who may live there in your own words; ask your broker if unsure.

What photos matter most in a 55-plus community listing?

The step-free entry, the garage-to-house threshold, the shower from the door, the kitchen, the lanai or patio, and the clubhouse and facilities empty. Buyers and their adult children look for those first.

Should the listing describe who the home is perfect for?

No. Describe the property and the facilities. Lines about the ideal buyer are a fair housing problem; the community's stated rules are the only reference to age that belongs on the page.

Should the video have a voiceover?

A slow, plain recorded voiceover with word-by-word captions works well. Many viewers watch without sound, and the captions carry the facts.
