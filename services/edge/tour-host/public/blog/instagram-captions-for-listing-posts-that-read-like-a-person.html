Instagram captions for listing posts that read like a person

Write a listing caption like a text to a friend who asked about the house: one observation, three specifics, one ask. Before-and-after rewrites included.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Write the caption the way you would text a friend who asked about the house: open with one observation or fact, give two or three specifics with numbers, end with a single ask. Cut the adjectives, drop the capitalised JUST LISTED, and put any AI edit disclosure in one plain line at the end.

A listing caption reads like a person when it is written the way you would text a friend who asked about the house: one line that says what you noticed, two or three specifics with numbers in them, and one ask at the end. No adjective stacks, no capital letters shouting JUST LISTED, no sentence you would be embarrassed to say out loud at a showing.

The three-part shape

Every caption that works for a listing has the same bones, even when the words change.

  1. The observation. One sentence about the thing you would point at first if the reader were standing next to you. Not the price, not the address. The thing.
  2. The specifics. Two or three facts with numbers: beds and baths, the lot size, the year it was built, the walk to something, the price. Numbers are what make a caption sound like it was written by someone who has been inside.
  3. The ask. One. Open house Saturday 1 to 3. Link in bio for the full gallery. Message me for the inspection report. Pick one and stop.

A rewrite, line by line

Here is a caption for a 1920s bungalow as it was first drafted, then as it went out.

Before: JUST LISTED. Absolutely gorgeous, fully updated 3 bed 2 bath bungalow in a highly sought after neighborhood. Chef's kitchen, spa-like bath, tons of natural light. This one won't last. DM me for details.

After: The front porch on this one is deep enough for two chairs and a dog, which is the whole point of a 1920s bungalow. Three beds, two baths, 1,480 square feet, on a 50 by 120 lot four blocks from the Saturday market. Kitchen was redone in 2023 and the original fir floors survived it. $512,000. Open Saturday 1 to 3, or the full gallery and video are in the link in bio.

What changed: the first line points at something real. Every claim became a number or a date. The adjectives went. The ask became specific. The after version is longer, and it still reads faster, because the reader is not translating marketing words back into facts.

Words that give you away

Some phrases tell the reader a template wrote the caption. Highly sought after. Spa-like. Chef's kitchen in a home with a standard range. Entertainer's dream. Tons of natural light, in a photo that shows a north-facing room. Won't last, on a home that has been listed for six weeks. Each one costs you a little trust, and the reader does not know you well enough to give it back.

Replace each with the fact behind it. Spa-like bath becomes a soaking tub and a separate shower. Tons of natural light becomes south-facing living room, three windows. Chef's kitchen becomes a 36-inch range and a walk-in pantry, or it becomes nothing because the kitchen is ordinary and the porch is the story.

Where captions go wrong

  • The caption is the MLS description pasted in. MLS copy is written for a different reader and a different box. Rewrite it in the three-part shape.
  • DM me for details, with no details. If the price and the beds are not in the caption, the reader assumes there is a reason, and the reason is rarely flattering.
  • Hashtags before the first sentence. Put them at the end, keep the set short and local, and let the first line do its job. There is a separate post on a short local hashtag set.
  • Two or three asks. Follow, share, save, DM, link in bio, come Saturday. The reader does none of them.
  • An edited photo with no mention of it. If the sky was replaced or the living room was virtually staged, say so in one line at the end. The one-line disclosure takes nine words.
  • Describing who the home is for. Perfect for a young family, ideal for retirees. Describe the home, not the buyer, and ask your broker about fair housing language before you publish anything that sounds like a profile.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long should a listing caption be?

Long enough for one observation, two or three specifics with numbers, and one ask. That is usually four to six sentences. A longer caption is fine if every sentence carries a fact; a shorter one is fine if the reel already carries the facts.

Should I put the price in the caption?

Yes, unless your brokerage or seller has asked you not to. Leaving it out makes readers assume it is high, and it sends the ones who would have been a fit to someone else's post.

Can I reuse the MLS description as the caption?

Use it as a source of facts, not as the caption. MLS copy is written for a different reader and reads like a brochure in a feed. Pull the numbers out of it and write the rest the way you talk.

Where does the AI edit disclosure go in a caption?

One plain line at the end, after the ask and before any hashtags. Name the edit: sky replaced, living room virtually staged, lawn edited. It should match whatever label the photo itself carries.
