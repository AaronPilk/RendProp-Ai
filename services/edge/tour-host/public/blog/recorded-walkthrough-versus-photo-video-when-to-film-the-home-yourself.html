Recorded walkthrough or photo video: when to film it yourself

Film a walkthrough when the way rooms connect is the selling point, when buyers are remote, or when photos are weak. Use photo video for the rest.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Film a handheld walkthrough yourself when the flow between rooms is what sells the home, when a serious buyer is out of town, or when the photo set is weak and cannot be reshot. Use a photo-based video for social reels and for most property page tours, because it is built from controlled frames and exports in both 9:16 and 16:9. The two are separate things, and many listings benefit from both.

Film the home yourself when the flow between rooms is the selling point, when a serious buyer is out of town and wants the walk before they fly in, or when the photos are weak and a reshoot is not happening. For everything else, a photo-based video does the job better: it is built from frames that were composed one at a time, it exports as both a vertical reel and a horizontal tour, and it does not show the laundry basket in the hall. The two are different products, and the right answer on many listings is both.

What a walkthrough shows that photos cannot

Connection. A still photo shows a room. A continuous walk shows how the kitchen opens to the dining room, how the dining room looks back at the living room, how far the main bedroom is from the kids' rooms, how the deck meets the yard. Buyers form a mental map on a showing, and a walkthrough gives a remote buyer that map.

Scale, too. A photo at a wide angle can make a room look bigger than it is. A walk through it, with the door frame passing and the far wall arriving, is harder to misread.

And the sound. Footsteps on the hardwood, the quiet at the back of the house, the door closing. None of it is in a photo.

What photo video does better

  • Control. Each frame was composed with time to move the chair and wait for the light. A walkthrough records what was there when you walked past it.
  • Two formats from one set. Portrait frames make the 9:16 reel, landscape photos make the 16:9 tour. A walkthrough is one orientation, and cropping it to the other loses the room.
  • A script. A photo video carries a voiceover written in advance and word-by-word captions. A walkthrough carries whatever you said while walking, or silence.
  • Occupied homes. Photos were taken with things moved. A walk shows the dog bed, the open closet and the seller's calendar on the fridge.
  • Speed. If the photos exist, the video can exist the same afternoon.

A decision list

Film it yourself when at least one of these is true.

  • The home is open-plan, a loft, or arranged around a view, and the connection is the story.
  • A specific buyer has asked to see the walk and cannot get there.
  • The photo set is poor and there is no reshoot.
  • It is a large property where the distance between things matters: a main house and a guest house, a barn, a long drive.

Use the photo-based video when at least one of these is true.

  • You need a reel for social this week.
  • The home is occupied and busy.
  • You want a scripted voiceover and captions.
  • You need both 9:16 and 16:9.

If both lists have a tick, make both. They go in different places.

A worked example: the lake house and the townhouse

A lake house where every room on the main floor turns toward the water, and a buyer in another state has asked for a tour before booking a flight. The agent films a four-minute handheld walkthrough from the front door, through the kitchen, into the great room, out to the deck and down to the dock. The buyer scrolls through it twice and books the flight. The agent also makes a photo-based reel for social and a 16:9 photo tour for the page, because the walkthrough is for buyers who have already decided to look and the reel is for people who have not.

A three-storey townhouse, occupied by a family with two kids and a dog. The photographer's set is clean. A walkthrough would show toys on every landing. The agent makes the photo-based reel and the page tour, and skips the walkthrough. A buyer who asks gets a private showing instead.

What goes wrong with each

Walkthroughs. Walking too fast. Turning the phone through a doorway so the picture swings. Narrating square footage while walking. Filming at noon with every window blown out. Filming an occupied home without a tidy first. Treating it as the social video and posting four minutes to a feed.

Photo video. Too many frames, so it becomes a slideshow. Mixed orientations. Edited photos with the disclosure dropped. A script that reads the listing sheet. There is a longer list in reviewing a listing video before you post it.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Is a recorded walkthrough better than a photo-based listing video?

Neither is better; they do different jobs. A walkthrough shows how rooms connect and gives a remote buyer the walk. A photo-based video is built from controlled frames, carries a script and captions, and exports in both vertical and horizontal formats for social and the page.

Can I film the walkthrough on my phone?

Yes. Handheld phone footage with the wide lens, walked slowly and level, is what a walkthrough is. It is not drone footage and does not need to be. The common faults are speed, swinging through doorways and filming at the wrong hour.

Should the walkthrough go on social media?

Usually not as-is. A walkthrough is long and horizontal or vertical but not both, and it is for buyers who have already decided to look. Use the photo-based 9:16 reel for feeds and publish the walkthrough separately for people who want the full walk.

Do I need a walkthrough for every listing?

No. Film one when the flow between rooms sells the home, when a remote buyer asks, or when the photos are weak. For an occupied home with a clean photo set, the photo-based video and a private showing cover it.
