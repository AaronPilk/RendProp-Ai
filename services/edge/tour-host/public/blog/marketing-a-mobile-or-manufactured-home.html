Marketing a mobile or manufactured home

A manufactured home sells on owned or leased land, the year and make, and how it sits on the site. Shoot it like a house and state the lot terms plainly.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A mobile or manufactured home sells on three facts a buyer asks before anything else: whether the land is owned or leased and on what terms, the year and manufacturer from the data plate, and how the home sits on the site. State those in the first line, then shoot it exactly as you would shoot any house, because the interior is usually the strongest part and most listings waste it.

A manufactured home sells on three facts, and a buyer wants them before they look at a single room: is the land owned or leased and on what terms, what year and make is the home according to its data plate, and how does it sit on the site, meaning foundation, skirting and tie-downs as they exist. Put those in the first line of the page. Then photograph it the way you would photograph any house, with every light on and the counters clear, because the interior is often the strongest part of the listing and most agents shoot it from the doorway and leave.

The mistakes that get made with this property type

  • Apologizing with the photos. Three frames from the road and one of the living room. A buyer reads it as a listing nobody cares about. Shoot the full set.
  • Leaving out the lot terms. If the lot is leased, the monthly rent and what it includes, as the park states it, is the first thing a buyer needs. Leaving it out makes them assume it is bad.
  • Guessing the year. Read the data plate. If it is missing, say so and give the seller's best information as the seller's.
  • Hiding the skirting and the steps. They are the first thing a buyer looks at on arrival. Show them clean and in good light.
  • Stating financing or title rules. How a manufactured home is titled and financed varies and changes. Say what the seller's documents show and ask your broker how to describe it.

What the page states, in order

  • Land owned with the home, or leased lot. If leased: the current lot rent as the park states it, what it includes, and where a buyer reads the park's rules. Do not summarize park rules yourself.
  • Year, manufacturer and model from the data plate; single or double section; dimensions.
  • How it sits: foundation type, skirting, tie-downs, as they exist. Whether the seller has any documentation.
  • Additions: porch, deck, carport, shed, and whether they were permitted as far as the seller knows.
  • Systems: roof, heating and cooling, water heater, with ages where known.
  • Park or community amenities, named plainly.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in the details fields, the same as a site-built home.

Write it the way you would write a starter home: honest, warm, no brochure voice. The post on starter home descriptions has the tone.

Shooting it like a house

  1. Front exterior from the street, with the steps, porch and skirting in frame, at an hour when the sun is on the front.
  2. The lot. Side yard, back yard, the carport or parking, the shed. If the land is owned, the boundaries matter more; walk them.
  3. Living room from the entry, then from the far end back toward the door.
  4. Kitchen, two frames, counters clear, appliances in.
  5. Dining area.
  6. Primary bedroom and bath. Blinds up, bed made, bath counters empty.
  7. Each other bedroom and bath.
  8. Laundry and utility. The water heater and furnace frame is useful.
  9. Deck or porch looking out, and the view from it, whatever it is.
  10. Community amenities if there are any: pool, clubhouse, mailboxes, the entrance sign.

The listing video runs front, living, kitchen, bedrooms, outdoor space, community. For the vertical social video, portrait frames of the kitchen and the living room do the work; the audience for this price point scrolls on a phone. Narrow rooms read wider from the far end, so include the frame from each end of the living room.

Worked example: a 2003 double-wide on a leased lot

A 3-bed, 2-bath 2003 double-section home, 28 by 56 feet, on a leased lot in a community with a pool and a clubhouse, with a covered deck and a two-car carport added in 2015. The agent reads the data plate in the master closet, gets the current lot rent and what it includes from the park office, and shoots on a Saturday at 9 a.m. when the front is in sun: the front with the steps and skirting, the carport, the deck, the back yard, then eleven interior frames with every light on. The page opens with: leased lot, current rent as stated by the park, water and trash included per the park, 2003 double-section, foundation and tie-downs as they exist with the seller's documentation available on request, roof replaced 2019 per invoice, central air 2021. Fourteen landscape frames make the listing video, which opens on the deck. The living room is the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What is the first thing to state in a manufactured home listing?

Whether the land is owned or leased and on what terms, then the year and manufacturer from the data plate, then how the home sits on the site. Buyers ask those three questions before they look at rooms.

Should a manufactured home get a full photo set?

Yes, the same set as any house: exterior, every room, outdoor space and community amenities. A short set reads as a listing nobody cares about, and the interior is usually the strongest part.

How do I describe lot rent and park rules?

State the current lot rent as the park gives it and what it includes, and point to where the park publishes its rules. Do not paraphrase the park's rules yourself.

Can I say how a manufactured home can be financed or titled?

Say what the seller's documents show and nothing more. Titling and financing vary and change; ask your broker how to describe it on the page.
