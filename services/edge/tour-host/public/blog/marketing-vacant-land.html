Marketing vacant land

Vacant land sells on access, boundaries, utilities and what the county lists. Photograph the entrance, the corners and the best feature; put facts first.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Vacant land sells on facts a photo cannot show: access, boundaries, utilities, topography and what the county lists for use. Photograph the entrance from the road, the corners, the best feature and the likely building site so a buyer can orient themselves, then put the facts, with their sources, at the top of the page and leave the beds and baths fields empty.

Vacant land sells on facts a photo cannot show on its own: how you get to it, where the lines are, whether power and water are at the road, how it slopes and what the county lists for its use. The photos exist to orient a buyer and to prove the best feature is real. Shoot the entrance from the road, the corners, the feature and the likely building site, in that order, then put the facts with their sources in the first lines of the page. Leave beds and baths empty; the page should not pretend there is a house.

Why land is harder to show than a house

A house has rooms; land has a shape, and the shape is invisible from inside it. Twelve frames of grass and trees look like one frame taken twelve times. The job of the photo set is to give the buyer a mental map: here is the road, here is where you turn in, here is the north line, here is the creek, here is where you would put the house, here is what the neighbors look like. Every frame should answer one of those. A hired drone session (typical U.S. rates as of August 2026: $150 to $350) is more useful on land than on almost any house, because one overhead still shows the shape; the drone stills go into the set as landscape photos like any others.

The shot list that gives a buyer a map

  1. The entrance from the road. Standing on the public road, looking at the gate, the culvert or the gap in the fence. Include the road surface.
  2. The drive in. Two or three frames along the track from the road to the interior.
  3. Each corner, with a flag or a stake visible. Shoot from the corner along each boundary line and note which corner in the text.
  4. The best feature, close. Creek, pond, mature oaks, the view off the ridge. Give it two frames.
  5. The likely building site, from where the driveway would meet it, with the slope visible.
  6. Utilities as they exist. The power pole at the road, the well head, the meter, the hydrant. If they are not there, do not photograph something that looks like them.
  7. The neighbors' context. From the boundary, looking out: pasture, woods, a subdivision, a highway. Buyers want to know.
  8. Overhead or high-angle frames, if you have them, with north noted in the text.

Shoot after rain has cleared and the grass has been cut, in low morning or evening light that gives the ground texture. For the listing video, open at the entrance, drive in, walk to the feature, finish at the building site looking back toward the road. A handheld walkthrough of that same walk, recorded at a slow pace, gives a buyer the distances in a way still photos cannot.

Worked example: 12 acres on a county road

A 12.3-acre parcel, per the 2022 survey, with 410 feet of frontage on a paved county road, power at the road, no well, a seasonal creek along the north line and a gentle rise near the center. The agent walks it on a Saturday at 8 a.m. with the survey, flags all four corners, and shoots: the gate from the road, three frames along the track, each corner looking down its line, the creek at two spots, the rise from the track with the road behind it, the power pole with the parcel behind it, the woods on the east boundary and the pasture on the west, then a hired drone still from above with the lines roughly visible. Fifteen landscape frames make the listing video, which opens at the gate. The page's first lines read: 12.3 acres per survey, paved county road frontage of 410 feet, electric at the road, no well or septic, seasonal creek on the north line, listed by the county as agricultural, buyer to verify intended use with the county. The drone frame is the main photo.

The page: facts first, each with a source

  • Acreage and frontage, from the survey or plat, with the date.
  • Access: public road or easement, paved or gravel, year-round or seasonal.
  • Utilities at the road or on the parcel, as they exist, with none assumed.
  • Use as the county currently lists it, with a plain note that the buyer should verify intended use with the county. Do not state what can be built.
  • Water features, with whether they run all year, as the seller knows it.
  • Topography in one sentence, and soil or perc information only if a report exists.
  • Flood status per your state's disclosure rules; ask your broker what to include.
  • Price in its field and acreage in the text; leave the beds, baths and square feet fields empty so those sections do not show.

Where land listings go wrong

  • Twelve identical frames. No entrance, no corners, no feature. The buyer cannot place themselves on the parcel and moves on.
  • A green lawn edit on a pasture. Brown grass in August is what the buyer will find. Leave it, or disclose it.
  • A blue sky on every frame. One overcast day is fine. A set where every sky is identical looks processed.
  • Stating buildability. If the county has not told you in writing, you do not know. Say what the county lists and tell the buyer to verify.
  • Dropped pins in the wrong place. Check the location you share against the survey before you send it.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What photos does a vacant land listing need?

The entrance from the road, the drive in, each corner looking down its line, the best feature, the likely building site, the utilities that exist and the neighbors' context. Each frame should help the buyer place themselves on the parcel.

Should I say what can be built on the land?

No. State how the county currently lists the parcel and tell the buyer to verify intended use with the county. Stating buildability you have not confirmed in writing is how land listings go wrong.

Is a drone worth hiring for a land listing?

Usually yes. One overhead still shows the parcel's shape, which no ground photo can. Typical U.S. rates as of August 2026 are $150 to $350 for a session, and the stills go into the photo set like any landscape photo.

What do I put in the beds and baths fields for land?

Nothing. On a Rendprop property website, sections hide when their fields are empty, so the page shows price, gallery, video, text and the enquiry form without pretending there is a house.
