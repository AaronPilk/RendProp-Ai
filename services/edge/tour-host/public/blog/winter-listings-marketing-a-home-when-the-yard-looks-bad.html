Winter listings: marketing a home when the yard looks bad

Market a winter listing when the yard looks bad: lead with interiors, shoot the exterior at real twilight, use sky and lawn edits only with a disclosure.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. When the yard looks bad, make the interiors carry the listing, shoot the exterior at the time of day that flatters it (winter twilight comes early and is real), and use blue sky or green lawn edits only where they describe a condition the buyer will find in another season, with a disclosure. Do not paint July onto a January lot. A buyer who arrives to find snow where the listing showed a lawn trusts nothing else on the page.

Start inside

A winter listing is an interiors listing. Winter light is low and warm for most of the day, which flatters wood floors and south-facing rooms. Schedule the shoot for late morning when the sun is as high as it gets, open every blind, turn on every lamp, and clear the entry of boots and coats. Lead the gallery and the video with the main living space or the kitchen, not the front of the house. On the property page the main photo can be an interior; buyers scrolling in January are comparing warm rooms, not lawns.

The exterior in winter: what to shoot and when

Three options, in order of preference.

  1. Real twilight. In winter it arrives around dinner time, so a photographer can shoot it without a late evening. Every window lit, the porch light on, a dusting of snow on the roof. Winter twilight exteriors are often the best exterior a house will ever have, and nothing about them needs an edit.
  2. Midday after a fresh snowfall. Clean snow is honest and reads as seasonal rather than neglected. Shovel the path first. The brown lawn is covered and no one is misled.
  3. Overcast midday with a dormant lawn. This is the one that tempts edits. Shoot it anyway, because it is the truth, and decide about edits with the original in hand.

Which edits are honest in winter and which are not

A blue sky edit on an overcast exterior changes the weather, not the house. Most buyers understand that the sky on the day of the shoot is not a feature of the property, and a labelled sky edit rarely causes trouble. A green lawn edit on a dormant lawn is different: it depends on whether that lawn is green in May. If the seller has photos from last summer showing a healthy lawn, a labelled green-lawn edit describes a real condition in another season. If the yard is bare dirt and gravel under the snow, a green lawn is a fiction. Edits that remove snow from a roof, hide a tarp over the pool, or add leaves to bare trees cross into describing a different property. Every edit carries its disclosure, the original stays in the set, and you check your MLS rules on altered images before any of them is posted.

A January colonial with a dead lawn

A 4-bed colonial listed in the second week of January. The lawn is brown, the maples are bare, and the sky has been grey for a week. The photographer shoots interiors at 11 a.m. with every lamp on, and comes back at 4:45 p.m. for twilight: lights in every window, the porch lantern on, the brown lawn now a dark shape that reads as evening ground. That twilight frame becomes the main photo. The overcast midday exterior gets a labelled blue sky edit for the gallery, compared with the original first. The seller has a June photo of the same lawn, green and mown, so a labelled green-lawn version goes in as well, and the listing description says the yard is dormant for the season. The video opens on the kitchen, not the front. Showings in the first week go fine, because what buyers saw was what they found, plus lamps.

What goes wrong in winter

  • Shooting at 2 p.m. in flat light with no lamps on. Interiors look like a rental listing. Lamps on, blinds open, late morning.
  • A summer lawn on a snowy lot. Buyers notice, and a buyer's agent will mention it in the offer.
  • Removing the snow. It looks like a different day and a different climate, and it is not the lot the buyer is buying.
  • Using last summer's photos as if they were current. If the seller has older exteriors, label them with the month they were taken.
  • Leaving the walk unshovelled for the shoot. The cheapest fix in winter marketing is a shovel.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should the main photo of a winter listing be the exterior?

Not necessarily. A lit winter twilight exterior makes a strong main photo; a grey midday one does not. If twilight is not possible, lead with the kitchen or the main living space and put the exterior second.

Is a green lawn edit honest on a dormant winter lawn?

Only if the lawn is green in another season and you can show it, and only with a disclosure on the photo. On bare dirt or gravel, a green lawn describes a yard that does not exist. Check your MLS rules on altered images either way.

Can I use the seller's summer exterior photos?

Yes, labelled with the month they were taken and alongside a current exterior. Presenting last July's photo as the current state of the lot is the kind of thing buyers remember at the showing.

Does snow hurt a listing?

Fresh, clean snow with a shovelled path reads as seasonal and honest. Old grey snow, a buried walk and unlit windows read as neglect. The fix is a shovel, lamps and a twilight shoot, not an edit.
