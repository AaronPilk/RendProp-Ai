Copy for a rental listing: rules, dates and the application

Rental copy answers four questions in the first five lines: the rent and what it includes, the move-in date, the rules, and how to apply. Then the unit.

Rental copy should answer the four questions a tenant asks before they call, in the first five lines: the rent and exactly what it includes, the date the unit is available, the rules (pets, smoking, parking, lease length) and how to apply. Describe the unit after that, briefly and honestly. A tenant who gets the four answers up front either applies or moves on, and both outcomes save you a phone call.

This post is not legal advice. Rental advertising and tenant screening are governed by rules that differ by state and city, and the words you use about who may apply matter. For screening criteria, application fees and any phrase about the kind of tenant you want, ask your broker or property manager and check the current local rules before publishing.

The five lines at the top

  • Rent and what it includes. “$1,850 a month. Water, trash and one parking space included; tenant pays gas and electric.” Not “utilities included” without saying which.
  • Available date. A real date. “Available March 1.” If it depends on the current tenant moving out, say “expected March 1”.
  • Lease length and deposit. “12-month lease. Security deposit equal to one month's rent.” Check local rules on what you may ask for and state.
  • Rules. Pets (yes, no, which, any fee), smoking, parking, laundry access, anything a tenant would be upset to learn at the showing.
  • How to apply. Where the application is, what it asks for, the fee if there is one, and roughly when applicants hear back. “Apply through the link on the page; ID, proof of income and references; $35 fee; decisions within three business days.” Confirm the fee and the process with your property manager; many places regulate both.

The unit, in a short paragraph

After the five lines, describe the unit the way you would describe it to a friend who asked: size, floor, light, what was updated and when, what is included (appliances, window units, storage). Condition stated plainly beats adjectives, because the tenant will stand in it within a week and compare. “Second-floor one-bed, about 650 square feet, windows on two sides. Kitchen appliances replaced in 2023; bathroom is original 1980s tile, clean and working. In-building laundry on the ground floor.”

If the photos are of a different unit in the same building, say so in the text and on the photos. Tenants screenshot listings and compare them with what they see; see AI edits on rental listings for why that matters more here than in sales.

Words to be careful with

Phrases that describe the tenant rather than the unit are where rental ads get into trouble. “Perfect for a young professional”, “ideal for a single person”, “no students”, “great for families” and anything about background, family status, religion, ability or source of income all describe people. Describe the unit: one bedroom, third floor, no elevator, street parking. The reader will decide whether it suits them. If you are unsure about a phrase, that is the signal to ask your broker or property manager before publishing. The post on fair housing language in listing descriptions covers the reading pass, with the same caveat that it is not legal advice.

The same apartment, two ways

Before: “Charming 1BR in a great neighbourhood, perfect for a young professional. Utilities included. Available soon. No pets. Serious inquiries only, contact for details.”

After: “$1,850 a month, water and trash included, tenant pays gas and electric. Available March 1 on a 12-month lease; deposit equal to one month's rent. No pets, no smoking, one off-street parking space. Apply through the link on the page: ID, proof of income and two references, $35 fee, decisions within three business days. Second-floor one-bed, about 650 square feet, windows on two sides, kitchen appliances from 2023, in-building laundry.”

The second version is longer and gets fewer calls, because the calls it gets are from people who already fit.

Where rental copy goes wrong

  • Hiding the rent. “Contact for pricing” on a rental reads as either too high or a bait listing.
  • “Utilities included” with no list. The argument comes with the first electric bill.
  • “Available now” when it is not. Tenants plan around dates.
  • No application path. A listing that ends with “contact for details” turns every reader into a phone call and most of them into nothing.
  • Photos of a different unit, unlabelled. The showing becomes an argument about which window faces which way.
  • Describing the tenant. See above, and ask your broker.
  • Edited photos without a note. If the living room is virtually staged or the sky replaced, say so in the text as you would for a sale.

Write the five lines first

Before you write a word about the unit, type the rent and inclusions, the date, the lease terms, the rules and the application path, and run the rules and the application wording past your property manager. Then describe the unit under them, and publish.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should go first in a rental listing description?

The rent and what it includes, the available date, the lease length and deposit, the rules on pets, smoking and parking, and how to apply. Those five lines answer the questions every tenant asks before calling. The description of the unit comes after.

Should I state the application fee and screening criteria in the listing?

State the application path, what it asks for and the fee if there is one, after confirming the fee and process with your broker or property manager; many places regulate application fees and what may be asked. Screening criteria wording is a legal question, not a copy question. This is not legal advice.

Can I say the apartment is ideal for a certain kind of tenant?

Describe the unit rather than the tenant. Phrases about who the apartment suits describe people, and some of them raise fair-housing problems. One bedroom, third floor, no elevator and street parking tell a reader what they need to know. If a phrase feels borderline, ask your broker before publishing.

Is it all right to use photos from a similar unit in the building?

Only if the text and the photos both say so clearly, and the differences are described. Tenants screenshot listings and compare them with what they see at the showing. A labelled photo of a similar unit is understandable; an unlabelled one reads as a bait listing.
