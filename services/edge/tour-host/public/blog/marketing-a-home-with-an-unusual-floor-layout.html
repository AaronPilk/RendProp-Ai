Marketing a home with an unusual floor layout

An odd layout sells to the buyer who needs it. Shoot the transitions, film the real walk through the house, and describe the layout instead of hiding it.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. An unusual layout sells to the buyer who needs exactly that layout, so the marketing should explain it rather than hide it: photograph the transitions, standing in one room looking into the next, film a handheld walkthrough of the real route so viewers can scroll it, and describe on the page where each room sits and what it connects to. Hiding the half-flights and the bedroom off the kitchen only fills the showing calendar with people who will leave in four minutes.

An unusual layout has a buyer, and it is the person who needs rooms arranged that way: the family with a parent moving in, the couple who both work from home, the roommates, the owner of a home business who needs a room with its own door. The marketing job is to explain the layout so that person recognizes it and everyone else self-selects out before the showing. Photograph the transitions between rooms, film the actual walk through the house so a viewer can scroll it, and describe on the page where each room sits and what it connects to. A hidden half-flight of stairs costs you nothing on the page and a showing in person.

A worked example, because the principle is easier to see

A 1960s split-level with an addition: the front door opens onto a landing with six steps up to the living room, kitchen and two bedrooms, and six steps down to a family room, a third bedroom, a full bath and a door to the garage. A 1990s addition off the kitchen added a large room with its own exterior door and a half bath, reached through the kitchen. On paper it is a 4-bed, 2.5-bath; in person it is three distinct zones. The agent shoots it as zones. From the front door: the landing with both stairs visible. Up: living room, kitchen, the doorway from the kitchen into the addition, the addition from its own door, the two bedrooms, the bath. Down: the family room from the bottom of the stairs, the third bedroom, the bath, the garage door. Then a two-minute handheld walkthrough that starts at the front door, goes up, through the kitchen into the addition, back and down, and ends at the garage. The page describes it as three zones and names what each has been used for: the addition as an in-home studio with its own entrance, the lower level as a teenager's floor. The listing gets fewer showings than the one next door and more second showings.

How to photograph a layout instead of rooms

  1. The entry, with every direction visible. If there are two staircases from the landing, both are in the first interior frame.
  2. Transitions. Stand in one room and shoot into the next through its doorway, so the buyer learns what connects to what. These frames rarely win awards and they are the ones that make the layout make sense.
  3. Each zone from its entry point, then its rooms in walking order.
  4. Any room with its own exterior door, shot from outside and from inside, because separate access is often the whole value of the odd layout.
  5. Stairs and half-flights, looking up and looking down.
  6. The kitchen's relationship to the rest. If the only route to a room is through the kitchen, show that route.
  7. Bedrooms, baths, outdoor space as usual, but placed in the gallery in walking order rather than by importance.

For video, this is the property type where a recorded walkthrough does something the photo-based video cannot: it shows the route. Film it slowly, from the front door, following the path a visitor would take, with the camera level through the stair transitions. Viewers can watch it or scroll through it. Pair it with the photo-based listing video for the overview; the two posts on when to film the home yourself and filming a scrollable walkthrough cover technique.

The page: describe the plan in words

  • One sentence per zone or level: what is there and how you reach it.
  • Which rooms have exterior doors, and which can be closed off from the rest.
  • Where the kitchen sits relative to the bedrooms and the living space.
  • Step counts between levels, if there are half-flights.
  • What each unusual space has actually been used for, stated as the seller's use, which gives the right buyer the idea without you claiming a purpose the room may not be permitted for; ask your broker if you are describing anything that could read as a separate unit.
  • Bedroom count only for rooms that are bedrooms in your market; call the others what they are.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet in the details fields, counted honestly.

What goes wrong with odd layouts

  • Hiding the stairs. Every showing becomes a surprise and most of them end early.
  • Galleries ordered by prettiness. The buyer sees a nice bedroom, a nice kitchen and a nice family room and cannot tell how they relate. Order by the walk.
  • Staging the odd room as something generic. An add-furniture edit that turns the room off the kitchen into a second living room throws away the point. If you stage, stage it for the use the right buyer wants, keep the Virtually staged label and compare against the original so no doorway has been moved.
  • A custom edit that removes a step, a post or a half-wall. It is the layout. Leave it.
  • Calling it a bedroom when it is a walk-through. Describe it and let the buyer decide.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I hide an awkward layout in the listing photos?

No. Shoot the transitions and the stairs so the layout is clear, and describe it on the page. The right buyer recognizes it, and the wrong ones stop booking showings that end in four minutes.

Is a recorded walkthrough better than a photo video for an unusual layout?

It does a different job. The photo-based video gives the overview; the handheld walkthrough shows the route through the house, and viewers can scroll it. Use both.

How should I order the gallery for a home with an odd layout?

In walking order from the front door, zone by zone, not by which rooms look best. The buyer should be able to reconstruct the house from the sequence.

Can I describe a room with its own entrance as a separate unit?

Describe what it is and how the seller has used it. Whether it may be used or rented as a separate unit is not yours to state; ask your broker how to word it.
