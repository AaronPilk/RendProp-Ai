Marketing a listing in a new neighborhood nobody knows

In a neighborhood with no reputation, sell the map as well as the house: photograph beyond the lot line, put context in the video, say only what exists.

In a neighborhood nobody knows yet, the listing sells the map as well as the house. Buyers cannot fall back on a reputation, so you give them the facts a reputation would have carried: how you get there, what is within walking distance, what the street looks like from the front door, and what the rest of the development is right now. Photograph beyond the lot line, put that context into the video and the copy, and describe only what exists today. A buyer who was told the street is half built and the park is a field is fine with it; a buyer who found out on arrival is not.

You are selling the address and the map

In an established neighborhood the buyer already knows the coffee shop, the school route, the commute and what the neighbours' houses sold for. In a new one they know none of it.

So the listing answers the questions a local would already know. Distance and time to the main road, the highway, the nearest grocery, the nearest train. What is built and occupied on the street now. What the lot backs onto. These go in the copy, in the voiceover and, where you can photograph them, in the gallery.

What to photograph beyond the lot line

  • The drive in. One frame from the entrance of the development looking down the street toward the house. It shows the buyer what arriving feels like, including the parts that are still construction.
  • The street from the front door. Honest. If the house opposite is framed and roofless, that is what the frame shows.
  • What the lot backs onto. Woodland, a retention pond, a future phase, a road. Shoot it from the back fence and say what it is.
  • The nearest things that exist. The park that is open, the trail head, the shop at the corner of the main road, the bus stop. Not the ones on the developer's plan.
  • A frame that places the house. From the end of the street or a nearby rise, showing the house in its row, so the buyer understands the setting rather than a single facade.

Context in the video and the copy

The listing video for a new-neighborhood house should open on the drive in, not the front door, and the voiceover should say where you are before it says what the house is. The vertical social video can do the reverse, hooking on the best interior and ending on the street, because social viewers want the house first and the map second.

In the copy, lead with the location facts that exist and are checkable, then the house. Name distances and times the buyer can verify on a map. Describe what is built and what is a field. If the developer has published plans for a school, a shopping centre or a park, say that the developer has published plans and point the buyer to the developer or the municipality, rather than describing those things as if they exist. The line between describing a neighborhood and overselling it is drawn in describing a neighborhood without overpromising, and a new development is where it is easiest to cross. Describe the place and the amenities; do not describe the kind of people who live there or who it suits, and ask your broker about fair housing language if you are unsure.

The new build in Phase Two of Larkspur

A four-bed two-storey in the second of three phases of a development off a county road, listed in September by owners relocating a year after they bought. Phase one was finished and occupied, phase two was half built, phase three was graded dirt behind a fence. The nearest shop was at the county road junction; the development's own park was open but its retail parcel was a sign in a field.

The shoot covered the house in the normal way and then spent twenty minutes outside the lot. The entrance from the county road looking in. The street from the front door, with the framed house opposite in plain view. The back fence, looking at the phase three dirt. The open park two streets over. A frame from the slight rise at the end of the street showing the house in its row of finished homes.

The video opened on the county road turn-in and the drive down the finished street, with a voiceover that gave the time to the highway and the nearest train, named the park as open and the retail as planned by the developer, and only then introduced the house. The copy did the same in that order. Supporting posts about the area ran alongside; the approach is in neighborhood content that supports a listing. The buyers came from out of state, had never been to the town, and said afterwards that the video of the drive in was what made them book the flight: they knew what they were going to find.

Where new-neighborhood marketing overpromises

Describing the plan as the place. The school, the shops and the pool on the developer's drawing are not amenities until they open. Say planned, and say by whom.

Hiding the construction. The buyer drives past it to reach the showing.

Editing the view. A green lawn edit over graded dirt behind the fence, or a sky edit that also softened the framed houses opposite, misrepresents what is there. Compare any edit against the original and reject anything that changes the surroundings.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a listing in a brand-new neighborhood show beyond the house?

The drive in from the main road, the street from the front door, what the lot backs onto, the nearest amenities that are actually open, and a frame that places the house in its row. Buyers with no reputation to rely on need the map as much as the rooms.

Can I mention the school and shops the developer is planning?

Say that the developer has published plans and point buyers to the developer or the municipality for details. Do not describe planned amenities as if they exist. Open amenities can be named and photographed; planned ones are a claim you cannot back.

Should the photos show the construction on the street?

Yes. The buyer will drive past it to reach the showing. An honest frame of the street from the front door, framed houses and all, builds trust in the rest of the gallery and filters for buyers who are fine with a developing street.

How should the video be different for a new neighborhood?

Open the listing video on the drive in rather than the front door, with a voiceover that gives location facts before describing the house. The social cut can reverse that order. For an out-of-town buyer the drive-in footage is often what makes them book the visit.
