Directional signs, QR codes and the property page

Directional signs get cars to the door. A QR code at the door gets phones to the property page. Build it from the branded link and test it from the lawn.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Directional signs do one job: get the car from the main road to the driveway. The QR code does a different job: get the phone from the lawn to the property page. Build the code from the branded property page link, print it large on matte stock, put the written link beside it, and test it from the kerb before Sunday.

Directional signs and QR codes are two separate systems that happen to share a Sunday. The signs move cars: from the main road, through the turns, to the driveway. The QR code moves phones: from the lawn, the flyer or the hall table to the property page, where the gallery, the video, the details and the enquiry form are. Treat them as two jobs and both get done properly.

Build the code from the branded property page link. Print it big, on matte stock, with the written address of the page beside it for people who will not scan. Then walk to the kerb and test it with your own phone. If it does not read from where a visitor stands, it does not exist.

Where five signs go for one corner lot

A 3-bed on a corner lot at 48 Orchard Way, open Sunday 12 to 2. The subdivision has one entrance off the county road and two turns to reach the house. Five signs:

  1. Main road, at the subdivision entrance. Big arrow, “Open House,” the hours. Placed so a driver at 40 miles an hour reads it before the turn, not at it.
  2. First turn. Arrow only, plus the hours. The driver already knows it is an open house.
  3. Second turn. Same.
  4. At the house, on the corner the cars approach from. The listing sign with the open house rider, facing the approach. On a corner lot the sign faces the wrong street half the time.
  5. At the door. A small sign or a flyer stand with the QR code and the written link. This is the only sign that needs a code.

Put them out at 11, drive the route yourself as if you had never been there, and fix the one that points into the hedge. Collect them at 2:15; the neighbours notice the ones left out, and so does the council.

Why the code does not belong on the directional signs

Nobody scans a sign from a moving car, and nobody should. Putting a code on the arrow signs clutters them and slows the one piece of information they carry, which is the direction. The code lives where people are standing still: the sign at the door, the flyer in the stand, the card on the hall table, the printed flyer they take home.

The exception is the main listing sign on the lawn, which gets looked at by people walking a dog on a Tuesday. A code on that sign, at eye height, pointing at the property page, works all week. It should be large and it should be the branded link.

Building a code that works

  • Source link. The branded property page link. Not a portal page, not your homepage, not a video platform. The visitor should land on the gallery, the video, the details and the form.
  • Size. On the door sign or a flyer, at least five centimetres square. On the lawn sign, larger. Small codes fail in bright light and in shade.
  • Stock. Matte. Glossy paper reflects the sky and the phone cannot read it.
  • Contrast. Black code on white. Brand colours look nice and scan badly.
  • The written link beside it. Short enough to type. People over a certain age and people with older phones will type it; people in a hurry will photograph it.
  • A test from the kerb. Your phone, Sunday morning, from where a visitor would stand, in the light they will have. If it reads, it works.

What goes wrong, in rough order of frequency

  • The code opens a portal page. The visitor sees your house and four others, and the contact button goes to whoever bought the postcode.
  • The code is on the directional sign. Nobody scans it, and the arrow is harder to read.
  • The code points to a link that changes. A shortener that expires, a video that gets re-uploaded. The property page link is the stable one; build from that.
  • Glare. A laminated flyer in a stand in the sun. Matte, or put the stand in shade.
  • Arrows that work at noon and fail at dusk. For a twilight open house, use reflective stock or add a light. A black arrow on dark blue is invisible at 6 p.m.
  • The written link is missing. Half the people who look at a code do not scan it. The address in text beside it catches them.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Should I put a QR code on open house directional signs?

No. Directional signs are read from moving cars and only need the arrow and the hours. Put the code where people stand still: the sign at the door, the flyer stand, the hall table and the flyer they take home. A code on the main lawn sign also works for people walking past during the week.

What link should the QR code on a real estate sign open?

The branded property page link. It opens the gallery, the video, the details, your contact card and the enquiry form. A portal page shows competing homes and may route the enquiry to another agent, and a video platform gives the visitor nowhere to ask for a visit.

How big does a QR code need to be on a flyer or sign?

At least five centimetres square on a flyer or door sign, and larger on a lawn sign. Print black on white on matte stock, and write the link in text beside it for people who will not scan. Then test it with your own phone from where a visitor would stand.

Does Rendprop generate QR codes?

No. Rendprop publishes the property page and gives you its branded link in the Share sheet. You make the code from that link in any QR tool, and the page handles what happens after the scan: the gallery, the video and an enquiry form whose submissions land in your inbox.
