Marketing a listing in a tourist area

In a tourist area buyers know the holiday version. Add the resident's version: the quiet months, the parking, the rental rules and the walk in minutes.

In a tourist area most buyers first saw the place on holiday. They already have a picture of it, and the picture is the best week of the year with the sun out and somebody else doing the parking. The marketing has to add the resident's version without destroying the visitor's: what the town is like in the quiet months, where the crowds and the cars go in the busy ones, what the current rules on short-term rentals are with a note to verify, and how far the house really is, in minutes on foot, from the beach, the trail or the main street. Two buyer types shop the same house, the second-home owner and the person who intends to rent it out, and one page can serve both if it is built on facts.

The visitor's version and the resident's version

The visitor's version is what sells the location, and you should shoot it: the walk to the water, the view from the deck at the golden hour, the main street with the lights on. The resident's version is what keeps the sale together after the inspection and the first off-season weekend: the parking situation in August, the noise from the street on a Saturday night, the shuttered shops in February, the distance to a grocery store that is open year-round, the drive to the hospital. Put both on the page. A buyer who knows the February version and still wants the house is a buyer who closes and does not come back to renegotiate.

Season decides the shoot. If you can only shoot once, shoot in the season the town is known for, and write the other season honestly. If the listing runs across seasons, reshoot the exterior and the walk; a new main photo in the shoulder season is a new listing to the buyer who looked in July.

Who is buying and what each one needs

The second-home buyer wants the view, the walk, the deck and the practical facts of owning from a distance: who manages the place, what the association covers, what the utilities run to when the house is empty. The buyer who intends to rent it wants the current local rules on short-term rentals, stated as what the seller understands them to be with a clear note to verify with the town, plus the parking count, the bedroom count, the condition of the systems and any rental history the seller can document. A local buyer, who exists too, wants the resident's version and is suspicious of the brochure. The page that states facts in plain order serves all three; the page that sells a dream serves the first for a week and then falls over.

A cottage four blocks from the beach, listed in May

A two-bed cottage with a screened porch, off-street parking for two, four blocks from the beach and two from the main street. The agent shoots in mid-May: the porch at 6pm, the walk to the beach in portrait with the minutes in the captions, the main street in the evening, the interiors through the morning, the parking with two cars in it. The page gives the walk to the beach and the main street in minutes, the parking, the year-round grocery store and its drive time, the short-term rental rules as the seller understands them with a note to verify with the town, the association situation, the ages of the roof and the heat pump, and a plain paragraph about the off-season: which shops close, what the street is like in January.

A couple who have rented on that street for three summers find it through a portal, recognise the porch, and read the off-season paragraph twice. They fill in the enquiry form for the weekend they were already coming. A second enquiry comes from someone who plans to rent it and asks about the rules; the agent points them to the paragraph and the town's office. The couple offer after one visit, in which they walked to the beach with the video's minutes in their heads and found them accurate.

Where tourist-area listings go wrong

  • Selling only the holiday. The buyer already bought that in their head. The page that adds the February paragraph is the one they trust.
  • Vague distances. Steps from the beach, close to town. Walk it with the phone and give the minutes.
  • Guessing at rental rules. State what the seller understands, cite the town, tell the buyer to verify, and do not predict what the rules will be.
  • No parking photo. In a tourist town parking is the argument of every summer. Show it with cars in it.
  • Sky and twilight edits that out-promise the climate. A buyer who visits in the fog or the rain and finds the page full of blue skies has had a less honest introduction than they deserve. Use the edits for a grey day, not a grey season, and keep the disclosure.
  • No year-round facts. The grocery store, the hospital, the school if relevant, the mail. The second-home buyer who becomes a full-time resident will remember whether you told them.

Next step: write the off-season paragraph, five sentences about what the town is like in its quietest month, and put it on the page under the walk to the beach. It is the paragraph that turns a holiday into a purchase.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a listing in a tourist area include beyond the usual?

The walk in minutes to the beach, trail or main street, and the parking shown with cars in it. The short-term rental rules as the seller understands them, with a note to verify with the town. The year-round grocery store and hospital drive times, and a plain paragraph about the off-season.

Should I describe the off-season if it might put buyers off?

Yes. Buyers already have the holiday version in their heads; the off-season paragraph is what makes the page trustworthy and keeps the sale together after the first quiet weekend. A buyer who knows February and still wants the house is the buyer who closes.

Can I state the short-term rental rules on the listing?

State what the seller understands the current rules to be, name the town office that sets them, and tell the buyer to verify. Do not predict future rules or summarise them as settled; they change and the buyer's plan depends on them.

When should I photograph a home in a tourist town?

In the season the town is known for if you can only shoot once, with the other season described honestly in the copy. If the listing runs across seasons, reshoot the exterior and the walk so the buyer who looked in summer sees something new in the shoulder season.
