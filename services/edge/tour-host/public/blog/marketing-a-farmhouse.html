Marketing a farmhouse

A farmhouse sells on the drive in, the porch, the kitchen and what comes with the land. Show every outbuilding and state acreage and water with sources.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A farmhouse sells on the drive in, the porch, the kitchen and an honest account of what comes with the land: acreage from the survey, which outbuildings are sound, where the water comes from and what the fields are used for now. A farmhouse sells on the drive in, the porch, the kitchen and a straight account of what comes with the land. Before you shoot, decide which listing this is: a working property with acreage and outbuildings, where the land facts sit beside the house facts, or a farmhouse-style home on a lot, where the house is the whole story. For the first, the approach is the opening frame and the barns get their own section. For the second, shoot it as a house and do not borrow the vocabulary of a farm. This post is about the first kind.

Photos, from the road to the back field

  1. The drive in. Two or three frames from the road, up the lane, to the point where the house appears. Buyers of country property are buying that moment.
  2. Front exterior with the porch, late afternoon, furniture on the porch, screen door closed.
  3. The porch looking out. What you see from the chair.
  4. Mudroom or back entry. Hooks, bench, boot space. Country buyers look for it.
  5. Kitchen, wide and then the range or the table. The kitchen is the room a farmhouse buyer imagines living in; give it three frames.
  6. Living and dining, bedrooms, baths, in the usual order, with any original floors or beams shot close once.
  7. Each outbuilding from outside and from the door, with its condition visible: barn, shop, equipment shed, chicken house, greenhouse. Do not shoot only the good ones.
  8. Well head, pump house, septic area if it is marked, propane tank. Plain frames; the buyer will ask.
  9. Fields, pasture, garden, orchard, each from a fence line with a boundary visible, in the season that shows them working.
  10. The view back at the house from the far field.

Season matters more than time of day. Green fields in late spring or a cut hayfield in summer photograph better than stubble in March; if the listing cannot wait, say what the fields look like in season and consider a second shoot. A hired drone session (typical U.S. rates as of August 2026: $150 to $350) shows the lane, the house and the fields in one still, and the stills join the set as landscape photos. The listing video opens on the lane and ends on the far-field view. A handheld walkthrough from the porch, through the kitchen and out the back door to the barn suits this property type; viewers can scroll it.

The page: house facts and land facts side by side

  • Acreage per the survey or plat, with the date, and road frontage.
  • What the land is used for now: hay, pasture, crops, leased to a neighbor, fallow. State the current arrangement as the seller describes it.
  • Outbuildings, each with size and condition in a few words, and power or water in it or not.
  • Water: well with any yield information the seller has, spring, municipal; septic with its age if documented.
  • Heating fuel and delivery, internet as it exists, distance to the nearest town and services in miles.
  • Fencing type and condition, gates, access to fields for equipment.
  • Year built and systems updates with years, as for any older house.
  • Any easements, leases or tax programs on the land described as the documents describe them, with a note to read the documents. Ask your broker how to word anything you are unsure of.
  • Price, beds, baths and square feet for the house in the details fields.

If horses are the point, the equestrian property post reorders all of this around the barn. If the land is the point and the house is incidental, marketing vacant land covers the map-style shot list.

What goes wrong with farmhouse listings

  • Only the good barn. The buyer finds the collapsing one at the showing and wonders what else was left out.
  • A green lawn edit on a pasture or a field. It changes what the land is doing. Leave fields as they are, or disclose the edit.
  • A tidy edit that removes equipment, fencing or a propane tank. Compare against the original before using any edit; the things an edit removes are often things the buyer needs to see.
  • Farm vocabulary on a suburban build. A board-and-batten house on a half acre is not a homestead. Describe it as a house.
  • No water facts. A country buyer's first question is the well. Answer it on the page.

Worked example: a 1920s farmhouse on 22 acres

A 4-bed, 2-bath 1920s farmhouse on 22.4 acres per a 2021 survey, with a wraparound porch, a timber-frame barn in good repair, a pole shed with a bad roof, a drilled well, a 2015 septic, fifteen acres in hay cut by a neighbor under a verbal arrangement, and a quarter-mile gravel lane. The agent shoots in early June at 5 p.m.: three frames up the lane, the porch, the porch looking out across the hayfield, the mudroom, the kitchen in three frames, the rest of the house, the barn outside and in, the pole shed with its roof as it is, the well head and the septic markers, the hayfield from the fence with the barn behind it, and the house from the far corner of the field. A drone still from a hired session shows the lane, house and fields together. Twenty landscape frames make the listing video, opening on the lane. The page reads: 22.4 acres per survey, 15 in hay under a current informal arrangement with a neighbor, timber barn sound, pole shed needs a roof, drilled well, septic 2015, propane heat, 6 miles to town. The lane frame is the main photo.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should the first photo of a farmhouse listing be?

The drive in: the lane from the road with the house appearing at the end of it. Country buyers are buying that approach, and it tells them the setting before they see a room.

Do I photograph outbuildings that are in poor condition?

Yes, from outside and from the door, with the condition visible, and describe them plainly on the page. A buyer who finds a hidden collapsing shed at the showing distrusts the rest of the listing.

What land facts belong on a farmhouse page?

Acreage with its source, current use of the fields, water source, septic age, fencing, fuel, distance to town, and any easements or leases described as the documents describe them. Put them beside the house facts rather than beneath them, because the country buyer reads the land first.

Should I use a green lawn edit on fields or pasture?

No. The condition of the fields is part of what the buyer is evaluating. Leave them as they are in season, or shoot again in a better one.
