Listing descriptions for luxury homes: fewer adjectives

Luxury copy earns the price by naming things precisely: the maker, the material, the measure, the year. Adjectives signal the writer could not.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Luxury listing copy works by naming things precisely: the maker of the range, the species of the floor, the acreage to the decimal, the year of the roof. Every adjective is a place where a name should be. Cut them, add the specifics, and the price reads as earned rather than asserted.

A luxury listing description should name things, not praise them. The maker of the range, the species and width of the floorboards, the acreage to one decimal, the year the roof and the systems went in. Each adjective in a luxury description marks a spot where the writer did not know the specific, and buyers at this price point can tell. Cut the adjectives, put the names in, and the price reads as earned.

There is a reason adjectives get cheaper as the price goes up. At $400,000 a buyer is comparing with other homes they can afford, and “updated kitchen” is useful shorthand. At $3 million the buyer, or their agent, has seen fifty kitchens this year with the same adjective attached. What they have not seen is a sentence that tells them the range is a 48-inch dual-fuel from a named maker, installed in 2023, with a dedicated gas line and a vented hood. That sentence does the work of a page of superlatives.

The specificity ladder

For every feature you want to mention, try to climb four rungs before you write it down.

  • Material. Not “hardwood” but “rift-sawn white oak”. Not “stone” but “honed Calacatta”.
  • Maker or source. The appliance brand, the window manufacturer, the quarry if the seller knows it, the architect or builder if the home has one worth naming.
  • Measure. Ceiling height in feet, the length of the pool, the acreage, the square footage of the primary suite, the number of cars the garage holds with the doors closed.
  • Date. When it was built, when it was done, when it was last serviced. A 2024 roof is a fact; a “newer roof” is an argument waiting to happen.

You will not reach the top rung on every feature, and you should not try to list every feature. Choose the eight or ten that justify the price and climb as far as the facts let you on each.

A structure for a long description

Luxury remarks tend to run long, and your MLS may allow it; check its current limit. Length is fine if it is organised. A shape that holds up:

  1. One sentence that states the property in its simplest true form: “A 1931 Georgian on 2.4 acres, renovated in 2022, with a guest house and a pool.”
  2. The three or four rooms that carry the price, each with its specifics.
  3. The land and the outside: boundaries, orientation, trees, water, outbuildings, privacy stated as a fact (“not visible from the road”).
  4. The practical paragraph buyers at this level care about more than sellers expect: systems and their dates, generator, security, well or municipal water, the drive, the distance to the airport.
  5. Showings, and nothing else.

The same estate, with and without adjectives

Before: “An exquisite, one-of-a-kind estate offering unparalleled luxury and sophistication. The gourmet chef's kitchen is an entertainer's dream, while the resort-like grounds provide the ultimate in privacy and tranquillity.”

After: “A 1931 Georgian on 2.4 acres, renovated in 2022 by its current owners with the original plan kept. The kitchen runs 26 feet along the garden side with a 48-inch dual-fuel range, two dishwashers and a walk-in pantry behind the panelled wall. The grounds are enclosed by a 1930s brick wall on three sides and mature beech on the fourth; the house is not visible from the road. The 20 by 44 pool was re-plastered in 2023. Whole-house generator, 2022.”

The second version has no adjectives of praise and is far more persuasive, because every sentence tells the buyer something they would otherwise have to ask.

Where luxury copy goes wrong

  • Superlatives. “Unparalleled”, “unrivalled”, “the finest”. They are unprovable, and the buyer's agent has read them this morning on a different house.
  • The imagined lifestyle. Paragraphs about sipping wine on the terrace as the sun sets describe the writer's evening, not the property. State that the terrace faces west; the buyer can picture the wine.
  • Listing what every house has. Central air, a two-car garage and granite counters are not features at this price. Including them lowers the register of everything around them.
  • Vague renovation claims. “Fully renovated” invites the inspector to find what was not. Name what was done and when.
  • Exclamation marks and capitals. Neither belongs in a sentence about a $3 million house.
  • Describing edits as the home. A twilight edit or a virtually staged library is a presentation choice. Describe the real room and put the disclosure where it belongs. The buyer at this level will have the originals pulled.

One pass to make now

Print the current description and circle every adjective. For each one, write in the margin the specific that would replace it: the maker, the material, the measure or the date. Where you cannot find a specific, cut the adjective and the claim with it. What remains is the luxury description.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long should a luxury listing description be?

As long as the specifics justify and your MLS allows; check its current character limit. Three hundred organised words naming eight real features will outperform six hundred words of praise. Keep the longer version for the property page and trim for the MLS.

Should I name the appliance and fixture brands?

Yes, when they are brands a buyer at this level recognises, and when the seller confirms them. A named 48-inch range tells the buyer more than any adjective. If the brand is ordinary, name the size and the fuel instead and move on.

Is it a problem to mention the architect or builder?

It is usually a strength if the name means something locally and the seller can document it. Confirm the attribution before publishing. A wrong architect claim is the kind of error that surfaces at the closing table.

Do luxury buyers read the systems paragraph?

Their agents and inspectors do, and the buyers hear about it. A paragraph with the dates of the roof, mechanicals, generator and pool work removes a round of questions and signals a well-kept house. It is often the paragraph that keeps a deal together after inspection.
