Office space marketing: a page for the suite, not the building

Market each office suite on its own page with its own photos, video and link. A tenant is choosing a suite, not a lobby, and a building page buries it.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. Give each available office suite its own page with its own photos, video and link, rather than one building page with a list of availabilities. A tenant is choosing a suite with a specific layout, light and floor, and a building page makes them dig for it. One link per suite means you can send the exact space to the exact prospect.

Market the suite, not the building. A tenant looking for 2,400 square feet on a high floor with a corner office is not helped by a page about the lobby, the parking ratio and the building's year of renovation. Give each available suite its own page with its own photos, its own short video and its own link, and send that link to the prospect who asked for that size.

The building details can sit on each page in two lines.

Why building pages lose the tenant

Most office marketing is organised the way owners think: building first, floors second, suites as a spreadsheet at the bottom. Tenants think the other way round. They have a headcount, a budget and a move date, and they want to see the space that fits. On a building page they scroll past the amenity photos, find a stacking table, click a PDF, and lose interest somewhere around the second download.

A suite page cuts that to one tap. The broker texts a link; the tenant sees the suite's reception, the open area, the corner office and the view, with the square footage and the floor typed in. The decision to tour is made on the phone in the car park.

What to shoot in an office suite

  • Entry and reception. From the corridor through the suite door. The first impression a client of the tenant would get.
  • The open area, wide, from the entry. Shows the depth of the floor plate and where the columns are.
  • The open area from the window wall looking back. Shows the ceiling, the lighting grid and the finish.
  • Each private office or meeting room. One shot each, from the door, with the window in frame.
  • The view. Stand at the glass. If the suite has a view, it is half the rent.
  • Kitchen, server room, storage. Short and practical. Tenants ask.
  • Two building shots only. The entrance and the lobby. Enough to orient, not enough to distract.

Shoot mid-morning when the light is on the window wall. Turn every light on, including the ones in the offices with the doors open. If the suite is in shell condition, say so on the page rather than editing furniture in; a tenant who turns up expecting fit-out will not stay for the conversation.

Worked example: suite 1104, 2,400 square feet

A broker has three vacancies in a twelve-storey building: 1,100 square feet on four, 2,400 on eleven and a full floor on nine. She builds three pages. For suite 1104 she shoots nine landscape photos, including the corner office with the river in the window and the open area from both ends. The listing video opens on the building entrance, cuts to the suite door, walks the open area, shows the two offices and the meeting room, holds on the view and ends on the kitchen. A thirty-second voiceover gives the square footage, the floor, the availability date and the fact that furniture can stay. The page shows the details she typed, the gallery, the video and her contact card. A tenant rep who asked for 2,000 to 3,000 square feet gets one text with one link. The reply comes back as a tour request for Thursday.

Put the square footage where it is readable

Tenants scan for three numbers: size, floor, availability. Type them into the details where the mode has a field for them and say them in the voiceover, rather than burying them in a paragraph. If you quote rent, keep it current or leave it off; an old number does more harm than none. The enquiry form asks the visitor for a date, which for an office is the tour date, and that is the right question to be asking.

Mistakes that make a suite look smaller or worse

  • Shooting from the corner at 0.5x. Ultra-wide from a corner bends the walls and makes the far end look a mile away. Step into the room and shoot from the door at a moderate width.
  • Blinds down. The view is the rent. Blinds up, lights on.
  • Old tenant's branding. Take the sign off the reception wall or crop it out.
  • A vertical reel as the main video. Fine for a social post, wrong for the page. Offices read wide.
  • One video for three suites. The prospect for 1,100 square feet does not want to sit through the full floor.

Do this for the next vacancy

Take the suite that has been sitting longest, shoot it mid-morning with the blinds up, and give it its own page and link. Send that link, not the building brochure, to the last five tenant reps who asked about that size.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Why not one page for the whole building?

Because a tenant is choosing a suite, not a building. They have a headcount, a budget and a move date, and a building page makes them dig through amenity photos and a stacking plan to find the space that fits. One page per suite lets you send the exact space to the exact prospect.

What are the most important photos of an office suite?

The open area wide from the entry, the view from the window wall, and each private office from its door. Shoot mid-morning with every light on and the blinds up. Keep building shots to the entrance and the lobby.

Should the rent be on the page?

Only if you will keep it current. An out-of-date number does more harm than leaving it off. Size, floor and availability date are the three numbers tenants scan for; put those in the details and the voiceover.

Which Rendprop mode fits an office suite?

The other businesses mode, which covers offices, studios and workshops. Create one listing per suite so each gets its own photos, video and links.
