Enhancement versus alteration in listing photos

An enhancement changes how a photo looks; an alteration changes what the buyer thinks is there. Which edits fall on each side, and how to label them.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. An enhancement changes how the photo looks without changing what a buyer would expect to find: exposure, white balance, straightening, lens correction. An alteration changes what the buyer thinks is there: added furniture, removed fixtures, new skies, green lawns, twilight light. Enhancements usually need no label; alterations do. Check your MLS's current rules, because the exact line differs by MLS and state.

An enhancement changes how the photo looks without changing what a buyer would expect to find in the home: exposure, white balance, straightening verticals, lens correction, noise reduction. An alteration changes what the buyer thinks is there: added furniture, removed fixtures, a new sky, a green lawn, a twilight glow that was not in the sky that day. Enhancements usually need no label; alterations do, and the exact line differs by MLS and state, so check your MLS's current rules and ask your broker where your office draws it.

The test that sorts them

Ask: if the buyer stood where the camera stood, at the time the photo was taken, would they see what the photo shows?

A brightened room passes. The room was that bright to the eye; the camera just underexposed it. A straightened wall passes. The wall was straight; the lens bent it. A sky swapped from grey to blue fails. The sky was grey. A staged sofa fails. There is no sofa. A removed outlet fails. There is an outlet.

Notice the test says nothing about AI. A sky swap done by hand in a desktop editor is the same alteration as one done by a prompt. The tool does not decide the category; the effect on the buyer does.

Common edits, sorted

Enhancement

  • Exposure, contrast, shadows and highlights brought back to what the eye saw.
  • White balance corrected so white walls read white.
  • Vertical lines straightened.
  • Lens distortion and vignetting corrected.
  • Noise reduced, sharpening applied within reason.
  • Cropping that does not remove a feature the buyer would want to know about.
  • Window pull that balances interior and exterior exposure, as long as the view outside is the actual view.

Alteration

  • Virtual staging: furniture, rugs, art, plants that are not there.
  • Decluttering that removes anything fixed: fixtures, vents, bars, outlets, cracks, stains.
  • Sky replacement.
  • Green lawn, or any change to the condition of the yard.
  • Day-to-twilight conversion.
  • Removing power lines, poles, neighbouring buildings, cars on the street, anything that tells the buyer about the surroundings.
  • Changing materials: floors, counters, cabinet colour, appliance finish.
  • Any custom AI prompt that adds, removes or reshapes.

The grey zone

A few edits sit between the two, and reasonable people disagree. Treat them as alterations and label them; a label on a borderline edit costs you nothing, and a missing label on an edit someone else calls an alteration costs you an argument.

  • Removing loose clutter. A laundry basket, a phone charger, a stack of mail. The item is not part of the home. Many agents treat this as harmless. Label it anyway if the removal was extensive enough to change the impression of the room.
  • Brightening beyond what the eye saw. A north-facing room pushed to look sunlit. Technically exposure, practically an alteration. If you would not recognise the room at noon, you have crossed.
  • Colour saturation on the yard. A nudge is enhancement; a dead lawn turned emerald is alteration.
  • Removing a photographer's reflection in a mirror. Harmless and standard.

A worked example: one kitchen, six edits

A kitchen photographed at 10am on an overcast Thursday. The edits an agent might run:

  1. Lift the exposure so the counters are not muddy. Enhancement.
  2. Correct the white balance so the cabinets are not yellow. Enhancement.
  3. Straighten the verticals. Enhancement.
  4. Remove the dish rack and the mail on the counter. Grey zone; loose items, likely fine without a label unless the office policy says otherwise.
  5. Remove the dated pendant and replace it with a modern one. Alteration. Label it or, better, do not do it.
  6. Add a bowl of fruit and a vase. Alteration, and staging. Label it “Virtually staged.”

Three edits made the photo honest. One made it debatable. Two made it a different kitchen. The buyer at Saturday's showing will notice the pendant.

Why the distinction matters for the label

If everything gets a label, labels mean nothing. If nothing gets a label, buyers get surprised. The enhancement-versus-alteration split is what makes a label meaningful: when a buyer sees “Virtually staged” or “Sky altered,” they know something in the photo is not in the home, and when they see no label, they can trust what they see. That trust is the whole point. FAQ

Questions on this topic

Is brightening a dark room an enhancement or an alteration?

Bringing the exposure back to what the eye saw is an enhancement. Pushing a north-facing room to look sunlit goes past that and reads as an alteration. If you would not recognise the room at noon, label it.

Does it matter whether the edit was done by AI or by hand?

No. The category depends on the effect on the buyer, not the tool. A sky swap in a desktop editor is the same alteration as one done with a prompt, and both should be labelled.

What about removing clutter like mail or a dish rack?

Loose items that are not part of the home sit in a grey zone many agents treat as harmless. If the removal changes the impression of the room, label it. Removing anything fixed, such as an outlet or a vent, is an alteration.

Where is the official line between enhancement and alteration?

It differs by MLS and state, and offices set their own policies. Check your MLS's current rules and ask your broker. The buyer test, whether they would see what the photo shows standing where the camera stood, is a reliable practical guide.
