Marketing a medical or dental office space

A medical or dental suite is leased on plumbing, room count, parking and the waiting room. Show those first, state size and date, and ask for a tour date.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A medical or dental office space is leased on the things a practice cannot change cheaply: the plumbing and the number of operatories or exam rooms, the parking, the waiting room and the ground-floor access. Show those first in the photos and video, state the size and the availability date near the top, and give the practice a form that asks when they want to tour.

A practice choosing a medical or dental suite is buying the things it cannot change cheaply: the plumbing already in the walls, the number of operatories or exam rooms, the parking, the waiting room, and whether a patient with a walker can get from the car to the chair. Show those first, in that order, and state the size and the availability date near the top. Then give the practice manager a form that asks when they want to tour, because that is the message she is going to send anyway.

What a practice is actually assessing

A dentist looking at a former dental suite is counting operatories and checking whether the plumbing and the compressor lines are still there. A physiotherapist is looking at open floor and a treatment room count. A GP practice wants exam rooms with sinks, a reception that can hold a queue, and a records room. All of them want ground-floor access or a lift, parking close to the door, and a waiting room that does not feel like a corridor. If the listing shows a reception desk and a view, they go back to the brief and move on.

The shot list for a medical suite

  1. The entrance from the car park. Show the distance, the ramp, the automatic door if there is one.
  2. Reception and the waiting room, wide. From the entrance. Count the chairs in your head; the viewer will.
  3. Each operatory or exam room, from the door. Sink in frame, chair or bed in frame if it stays. One shot per room, every room.
  4. The plumbing and services where they can be seen. The mechanical room, the compressor space, the wall with the capped lines. Not pretty, and the most useful photo in the set.
  5. Sterilisation or lab area. Counters clear, sink visible.
  6. Staff areas. Break room, office, records.
  7. Restrooms, including the accessible one. Lit and clean.
  8. The parking lot with the entrance in the background. Count visible.

Shoot with every light on. Leave old signage out of frame or crop it. Do not stage with furniture or equipment the next tenant will not get; if an AI tidy edit is used on a cluttered store room, keep the disclosure and compare it with the original. A practice will tour with a checklist and notice.

Worked example: a four-operatory dental suite

A broker lists a 2,100 square foot ground-floor suite, formerly a dental practice, with four operatories plumbed, a sterilisation bay, a small lab, a reception that seats ten and a lot with 18 spaces. He shoots eleven landscape photos on a Tuesday morning: the car park and the automatic door, reception, the waiting room from the desk, four operatories from their doors with the chairs still in place, the sterilisation bay, the mechanical room with the compressor lines visible, the lab, the staff room, and the accessible restroom. The listing video runs forty-five seconds from the car park through reception, down the corridor past each operatory, to the sterilisation bay and the mechanical room. The voiceover states the square footage, the four plumbed operatories, the availability date and that the chairs can stay by negotiation. The page shows the details typed in and the gallery, and the enquiry form asks for the date the visitor wants. A practice manager for a dental group books a tour for Thursday with a note that she needs to see the compressor room; she has already seen it, and the tour confirms it.

The two numbers in the first screen

Square footage and the availability date. A practice relocating has a lease end date in mind and a headcount, and those two numbers decide whether the rest of the page is worth reading. Operatory or exam room count goes in the voiceover and the details. Rent, if quoted, must be kept current; an old number costs more than no number.

Where medical listings lose the practice

  • Leading with the lobby. The practice does not lease the lobby.
  • No room count. Four exam rooms and two exam rooms look the same in a photo of one.
  • Hiding the mechanical room. For a dental tenant it is the most valuable thing in the suite.
  • No parking shot. Patients drive. The practice manager counts spaces.
  • Renderings of a fit-out that is not there. They tour expecting it and leave when it is not.
  • Building brochure instead of a suite page. One page per suite, one link per prospect.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What matters most when marketing a dental or medical suite?

The things a practice cannot change cheaply: plumbing, the count of operatories or exam rooms, parking, the waiting room and ground-floor access. Show those first and put the square footage and availability date in the first screen.

Should I photograph the mechanical room?

Yes. For a dental tenant the compressor lines and the plumbing are the most valuable things in the suite, and a photo of them saves a tour that would otherwise be wasted.

Should the chairs and equipment be in the photos?

Only if they stay, or if you say in the voiceover that they are negotiable. Staging a suite with equipment the next tenant will not get leads to a short tour and a lost prospect.
