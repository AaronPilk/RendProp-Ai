Buyer questions at the open house the page should answer

The questions you answer twenty times on a Sunday belong on the property page, so visitors arrive with the next question instead of the first one.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. The questions you answer twenty times on a Sunday, price, square footage, year built, taxes, the fee, what is included, when you can move in, should already be on the property page. The ones about the seller's reasons and about offers stay in person. Put the facts in the page's fields, the explanations in the description, and keep Sunday for the questions that need a human.

The questions you answer twenty times on a Sunday should be on the property page by Thursday. Price, square footage, beds and baths, year built, the taxes, the association fee and what it covers, what is included, when a buyer could move in, whether there is parking, which way the garden faces. A visitor who read those on Thursday arrives on Sunday with the next question, which is the one worth your time.

Some questions should stay in person: why the seller is moving, whether there are offers, what the seller would take. Those are conversations, and the page is not where they happen. The job is to sort the questions into the ones the page answers and the ones you do.

The ten questions from Juniper Row

A townhouse at 8 Juniper Row, open Sunday 1 to 3. Here are the questions, roughly in order of how often they came, and where each one should have lived.

  1. “What is the price?” On the page, in the details. It was. People still ask, because they want to hear you say it; say it.
  2. “How many square feet?” Details. On the page.
  3. “What is the monthly fee and what does it cover?” Description. It was not on the page, and it was asked eleven times. Added Sunday night.
  4. “Year built?” Description. Added.
  5. “What are the taxes?” Description, with the year. Added.
  6. “Is parking included?” Description: one assigned space, number 8, visitor parking by the entrance. Added.
  7. “Are the appliances included?” Description: a sentence listing what stays. Added.
  8. “When could we move in?” Description: the seller's preferred timing, in general terms. Added.
  9. “Why are they selling?” In person, briefly, honestly, without detail the seller would not want shared.
  10. “Are there offers?” In person, with whatever the seller has agreed you can say.

Eight of ten belonged on the page. After Sunday night the page answered them, and the following Sunday the questions at the door were about the roof, the schools and the neighbour's extension, which are the conversations that move a buyer along.

Where each kind of answer goes

  • The fields. Price, beds, baths, square feet. These are structured, they appear at the top, and they are the first thing a visitor checks against what they remember.
  • The description. Everything that needs a sentence: the fee and what it covers, taxes and year, parking, what is included, year built, the roof and boiler ages, the garden's aspect, the timing. Short paragraphs, facts first, no adjectives doing the work of information.
  • The gallery. Questions that are really “show me”: the parking space, the storage, the basement, the view from the back.
  • The video's voiceover. The things that need context: “the road behind the fence is quieter in the evening,” “the third bedroom takes a single and a desk.”
  • In person. The seller's reasons, offers, negotiation, anything about the neighbours, anything where the honest answer is “it depends.”

The questions you should not answer on the page

Anything about the seller that the seller has not agreed to share. Anything that invites a negotiation by text. Anything speculative about the area's future. And anything you do not know for certain; a wrong fact on a page is a wrong fact in writing, which is worse than an honest “I will check” on a Sunday.

Where the page lets Sunday down

  • The page says three beds and the third is a den. Visitors feel misled before they reach the stairs. Call it what it is.
  • The price on the page is last month's. After a reduction, the page is the first thing to change, before the post and the sign rider.
  • The fee is missing. On a townhouse or condo it is the first or second question every time.
  • A description that is all adjectives. “Charming, bright, must-see” answers nothing. Buyers skip it and ask you.
  • Answering “why are they selling” in writing. It is the seller's story, not the page's.
  • Not updating after Sunday. The questions you were asked are a list of what the page lacks. Add them that night.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

Which buyer questions should the property page answer?

The factual ones: price, square footage, beds and baths, year built, taxes, the association fee and what it covers, what is included in the sale, parking, the garden's aspect, the ages of the roof and boiler, and the seller's general timing. If you answer it more than twice on a Sunday, it belongs on the page.

Which questions should stay in person at the open house?

Why the seller is moving, whether there are offers, what the seller would accept, anything about the neighbours, and anything where the honest answer depends on circumstances. Those are conversations, and putting them on the page either shares what the seller did not agree to or starts a negotiation by text.

Where on the page should the association fee go?

In the description, as a plain sentence with the amount and what it covers, because the page's structured fields hold price, beds, baths and square feet. On a condo or townhouse the fee is one of the first two questions every visitor asks, so it should be near the top of the description.

How do I know what is missing from my property page?

Keep a list of every question asked at the open house, in order. The ones that are facts and came up more than once are the gaps. Add them that evening, and compare the questions at the next open house; they should have moved on to the roof, the schools and the neighbours.
