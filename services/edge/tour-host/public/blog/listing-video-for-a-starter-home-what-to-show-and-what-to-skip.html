Listing video for a starter home: what to show and what to skip

A starter home video shows the kitchen, living space, bedroom count, yard, parking and location. It skips the small bath, the unfinished corner and hype.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

A starter home video shows six things: the kitchen, the main living space, proof of how many bedrooms there are, the yard or whatever outdoor space exists, where the car goes, and the location in words a buyer can picture. It skips the small bathroom, the unfinished corner of the basement, the view of the neighbour's fence and every adjective. The buyer is usually first-time, comparing this home to the rent they pay and to the six other homes they toured this week. Honest and quick wins.

Who is watching and what they are checking

The starter home buyer has a list. Can I cook here, can I sit here, is there a room for each of us, is there somewhere outside, where does the car go, how far is work. They are not asking whether the home is beautiful. They are asking whether it is enough, and whether it is in reach.

They are also tired and a little suspicious. Listing language has burned them. A video that says what the home is, shows it, and stops will do better than one that performs.

What to show, in order

  1. The kitchen, first. Even a modest kitchen leads if it is clean and lit. It is the room first-time buyers judge hardest, so put it on screen before they have a chance to wonder.
  2. The exterior, second. So they know what kind of home it is and can picture the street. If the front is plain, keep it brief.
  3. The main living space. One frame, wide, from the doorway. This is where they will sit.
  4. Proof of bedrooms. The main bedroom and one frame of a second bedroom. If it is a three-bed, three doors in a hallway can stand in for the third. The count is what they need.
  5. The yard or the outdoor space. A deck, a patch of grass, a balcony. Starter buyers often come from apartments and outside space is a big part of why.
  6. Parking. The driveway, the garage, the carport, or the street with a space in it. A frame, not a feature. It answers a question that would otherwise be the first message.

Six to eight photos. Twenty-five to thirty seconds. The property page has the rest.

What to skip

  • The small bathroom. It is fine. It is also the room where a wide lens lies most and a buyer feels it most at the showing. One honest frame in the gallery; not in the reel.
  • The unfinished basement. Say it exists in the caption as storage or potential. Do not film the furnace.
  • The neighbour's fence, the power line, the busy road. Not hidden, just not featured. The buyer will see them at the showing and judge you on whether the video felt honest about everything else.
  • Adjectives. Cosy means small. Charming means old. Buyers know the code. Use nouns.
  • Square footage read aloud. It is in the caption and on the page.

The script: plain and specific

Fifty to seventy words. Place, price point, what works, what to do.

Two bedrooms on a quiet street, fifteen minutes from downtown, under $300,000. The kitchen was updated two years ago. There is a fenced back yard and a driveway for two cars. Open Saturday from one to three. Full details and the enquiry form at the link.

Forty-six words. Every sentence is a fact a buyer can check. Record it yourself if you are the agent they will meet; a starter buyer wants to know who they are dealing with.

Honest edits on a starter home

Starter homes are where AI edits are most tempting and most risky. A greened lawn on a brown yard, a cleared counter, furniture in an empty living room: each can help a buyer picture the home, and each needs its label. Compare every edit against the original before it goes in the video, keep Virtually staged on staged frames, and check your MLS rules. A first-time buyer who feels tricked at the showing does not make an offer, and tells their friends.

A worked example: the two-bed on Birch Street

A 1960s two-bed with a small bath, an updated kitchen, a fenced yard, a two-car driveway and a half-finished basement, listed at $289,000. Reel: kitchen, front, living room, main bedroom, second bedroom, yard from the deck, driveway. Seven photos, 27 seconds, the agent's voice, light music, captions on. The bath is in the gallery with an honest frame. The basement is a line in the caption. Lead photo is the kitchen with the window over the sink. Posted Wednesday; open house Saturday.

Where starter home videos go wrong

  • Leading with the plain front and losing the viewer before the kitchen.
  • Skipping the parking frame and getting the parking question twenty times by message.
  • A script full of cosy and charming that a buyer reads as small and old.
  • A wide-lens bathroom that looks twice its size and costs you the showing.
  • Dropping the staging label because the room looked better without it.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should a starter home listing video lead with?

The kitchen, if it is clean and lit, because it is the room first-time buyers judge hardest. Put the exterior second so they know what kind of home it is. A plain facade as the first frame loses the viewer before the good part.

Should I show the small bathroom in the video?

Not in the reel. A wide lens makes a small bath look twice its size and the buyer feels the difference at the showing. Put one honest frame of it in the property page gallery instead.

How long should a starter home video be?

Twenty-five to thirty seconds for the social reel, six to eight photos, with a script of fifty to seventy plain words. The property page holds the numbers and the full gallery, so the video can stay short.

Are AI edits a good idea on a starter home?

They can help a buyer picture an empty or tired space, and they are also where trust is most easily lost. Label staged frames as virtually staged, compare every edit against the original, and check your MLS rules before posting any altered image.
