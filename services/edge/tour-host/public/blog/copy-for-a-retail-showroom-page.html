Copy for a retail showroom page

A showroom page says what you sell in plain category words, what a visitor can do there that a website cannot offer, and hours, parking and appointments.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A showroom page should say what you sell in the plain category words a shopper would search, what a visitor can do in the room that they cannot do online (sit on it, see the colour in daylight, talk to someone who knows), and the practical block: hours, parking, whether appointments exist and who the showroom is open to. The description of the room comes after those three.

A showroom page should say three things before it says anything else: what you sell, in the plain category words a shopper would type into a search; what a visitor can do in the room that they cannot do on a website; and the practical block of hours, parking, appointments and who the showroom is open to. “Sofas, dining tables and beds from twelve makers. Sit on everything, see the fabrics in daylight, and talk to someone who has had each piece in their own house. Open Tuesday to Saturday 10 to 6, parking behind the building, no appointment needed; trade customers by appointment on Mondays.” Then the room.

Showroom copy drifts into brand language because the brands supply it. “Curated”, “timeless”, “redefining the everyday” arrive in the supplier's press kit and end up on your page, where they tell a shopper nothing about whether you have a sofa under $2,000 in a colour that is not grey. The page is for the shopper, and the shopper has a question.

The category sentence

Write the first sentence for someone searching. Name the categories in ordinary words and the number or kind of makers you carry. “Kitchen and bathroom tile, slab stone, and fitted vanities, from stock and to order.” “Lighting: pendants, floor lamps, outdoor fittings; about 40 brands on the floor.” If you are a single-brand showroom, say the brand and the categories. If a shopper cannot tell from the first sentence whether you sell what they need, they leave to check the next page.

Why visit, in specifics

Everyone can buy online. The showroom exists for the things a screen cannot do, and the copy should name them: sitting on the sofa, seeing the oak against the walnut, feeling the weight of the tap, holding the tile against the paint chip in north light, asking the person who has installed two hundred of them. Write the paragraph around three or four of those, and make at least one of them about the staff. “Two of our floor staff are former kitchen fitters” is a reason to come; “our knowledgeable team” is not.

The practical block

  • Hours by day, with any late evening or Sunday opening called out.
  • Parking: where, how much, whether loading is possible for collections.
  • Appointments: not needed, recommended for design consultations, required for trade. Say how to book one.
  • Who it is for: open to the public, trade only, or both with different days. A designer who drives over to find a public-only floor, or a shopper who finds a trade-only one, will not come back.
  • Services: delivery area and lead times, installation, samples to take home, returns.

The room

Now describe the floor as it is laid out this season: what is on display, how it is arranged (by room, by maker, by material), how big it is, what is new since last month. Keep it current; a shopper who comes for the dining table on the page and finds it sold and gone has learned that the page is decoration. One showroom introduction, twice

Before: “A curated destination for timeless design. Our expertly selected collections bring together the finest makers to complete every room of your home. Visit us to experience the difference.”

After: “Sofas, armchairs, dining tables and beds from twelve makers, with fabric and timber samples for all of them. Sit on everything, see the oak next to the walnut in daylight, and ask the two members of staff who used to make furniture. Open Tuesday to Saturday 10 to 6, late until 8 on Thursday; parking behind the building; no appointment needed, and design consultations by appointment on the form below. Delivery within 50 miles in two to four weeks; samples go home with you.”

Where showroom copy loses the shopper

  • Brand-speak. “Curated”, “timeless”, “bespoke” on a page that never names a product category.
  • Every brand listed, no categories. Forty logos tell a shopper nothing about sofas.
  • No hours or parking. The two reasons a planned visit does not happen.
  • Trade and public confusion. Say who may come and when.
  • No word on what happens after. Delivery area, lead time, returns. The questions a shopper asks before committing a Saturday.
  • Photos of the supplier's catalogue instead of your floor. The visit is the product; show the real room, and if any photo was edited, say so.

Rewrite the first sentence for a searcher

Read the first line of your showroom page and ask whether a stranger could tell what you sell. If not, replace it with the plain category sentence, add the why-visit paragraph and the practical block beneath it, and move the brand language to the end or out.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

What should the first sentence of a showroom page say?

What you sell, in the ordinary category words a shopper would search, and the number or kind of makers you carry. Sofas, dining tables and beds from twelve makers tells a stranger whether to keep reading. A sentence about design philosophy does not.

Should a showroom page list prices?

Price ranges by category help a shopper decide whether to make the trip: sofas from about $1,800, dining tables from $900. Full price lists change too often to maintain. Say what the range is and that staff will quote on the floor.

How do I handle trade and public visitors on the same page?

State plainly who may visit and when: open to the public Tuesday to Saturday, trade by appointment on Mondays, or whatever the arrangement is. Give each a way to book. Confusion here costs you a wasted drive and a visitor who does not return.

How often should the room description be updated?

Whenever the floor changes in a way a visitor would notice: a new range, a sold display piece, a seasonal relayout. A shopper who comes for something pictured on the page and finds it gone learns that the page is not reliable. A monthly check is a reasonable habit.
