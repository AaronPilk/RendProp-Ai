Listing after a renovation: showing the work

A renovated listing sells on what changed, when and by whom. Photograph the details, list the dates, keep permits and receipts ready, and skip the edits.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. A renovated listing is marketed on three things: what was done, when it was done, and the proof. Photograph the details a buyer would otherwise have to touch, list the work with dates in plain language, have the permits and receipts ready to send, and leave the AI edits alone because the real work is the point.

A renovated listing sells on what changed, when it changed and whether you can prove it. Photograph the details a buyer would otherwise have to touch: the tile edge, the new panel, the window label. List the work with dates in plain words rather than adjectives, and keep the permits, receipts and warranties in a folder you can send the same day someone asks. And leave the AI edits alone on this one; the real work is the point, and a disclosure next to a renovated kitchen raises a question it did not need.

Show what changed, say when, keep the receipts

Buyers price renovations by age and quality, and they cannot judge either from a wide photo of a new kitchen. A kitchen done last year and a kitchen done twelve years ago can look the same at the width of a phone screen. The listing has to do the work the buyer's eye cannot.

  • What. Name the work. New roof, new furnace and air handler, kitchen taken back to the studs, both bathrooms, new windows throughout, electrical panel upgraded. Specific nouns, not improvements throughout.
  • When. The year for each item, from the receipts. A buyer reading a list with dates trusts the list.
  • Who and with what. Where it helps, the contractor's name and the materials: the counter stone, the window brand, the flooring. Buyers look these up.
  • Proof. Permits closed, receipts, warranties that transfer. Have them scanned in one folder before the listing goes live and say in the remarks that they are available.

The shot list for renovated spaces

Wide shots show the room. Detail shots show the work. A renovated listing needs both, and most galleries have only the first.

  1. The wide frame of each renovated room, as for any listing, lit well, landscape orientation for the video.
  2. One detail per room that proves quality. The tile meeting the tub edge. The drawer open to show the soft-close hardware. The counter edge and the seam, or the absence of one. The window label still on the glass if the windows are new.
  3. The mechanicals. A clean frame of the new furnace with its label, the water heater, the electrical panel with the door open. These are not pretty and buyers want them.
  4. The roof from the ground, with a frame that shows the new shingles or the metal at the eave.
  5. Portrait frames of the two best renovated spaces for the social cut, usually the kitchen and the primary bath.

Kitchen specifics, including what to remove from the counters before the frame, are in kitchen photos that sell.

Before photos: use them or not

Sellers who did the work want the before photos shown. Sometimes that helps and sometimes it hurts. A before-and-after pair in a social post is good content for the renovation story and gets shared. On the property page and in the MLS gallery it mostly reminds the buyer what the bones of the house were. The rule: before photos in the social video if the transformation is dramatic and the sellers want it told, never in the main gallery, and never as the main photo.

The bungalow on Prescott: a renovation listing

A 1950s two-bed bungalow bought by a couple three years earlier and renovated room by room: roof and gutters the first year, kitchen and electrical panel the second, both bathrooms and new windows the third. Listing planned for the second week of March, when the work was finally done.

The agent spent the first meeting at the kitchen table with the receipts folder, building a dated list: roof, year one, with the contractor's name; panel upgrade and the closed permit, year two; windows, brand and year three; and so on down to the bathroom tile. That list became the remarks and the basis of the voiceover. The shoot took two hours: wide frames of every room, then the detail set, including the panel with its door open, the window label, the shower tile, and the roof from the back garden. Nothing was edited. The sky was flat that morning and it stayed flat, because the agent did not want a disclosure anywhere near a listing whose whole argument was that the work was real.

The video walked the house in order with a recorded voiceover that read the list: what, when, who. The social cut used three before-and-after pairs the sellers had on their phones, which got more shares than anything the agent had posted that year. Two of the first three buyers through asked for the receipts folder; it went out the same afternoon. The offer came from one of them, with no inspection renegotiation, because the buyer had already read what was behind the walls.

Renovation marketing mistakes

Adjectives instead of dates. Fully updated means nothing. Kitchen, year two, contractor named, means something.

Only wide shots. A new kitchen at phone width looks like any new kitchen. The detail frame is where the quality shows.

Editing a renovated room. A blue sky or a tidy edit on a renovation listing attaches an AI-alteration disclosure to a listing whose claim is authenticity. Shoot it right and leave it alone.

No paperwork ready. The buyer who asks for permits on Saturday and gets them Wednesday has had four days to wonder. Scan the folder before launch.

Before photos in the main gallery. They belong in the social story, not next to the price.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How should I describe renovations in a listing?

Name each item, give the year it was done from the receipts, and where it helps name the contractor and the materials. Buyers price renovations by age and quality, and a dated list is what lets them do it. Avoid blanket phrases like fully updated.

What photos does a renovated home need beyond the usual set?

One detail frame per renovated room that proves the quality, such as tile edges, hardware, counter seams or a window label, plus clean frames of the new mechanicals and the panel, and the roof from the ground. Wide shots show the room; details show the work.

Should I include before photos?

In the social video, if the change is dramatic and the sellers want the story told. Not in the main gallery and never as the main photo, where they only remind buyers what the house used to be.

Should I use AI photo edits on a renovated listing?

Generally no. The listing's argument is that the work is real, and an AI-alteration disclosure on a photo invites a question it did not need. Shoot the rooms well and leave the originals untouched.
