The first sentence of a listing description

The first sentence should state the one checkable fact that makes this home different from the other three a buyer is looking at this week.

October 28, 2026 · 4 min read · by Rendprop

Short answer. The first sentence should state the one thing that makes this home different from the other three a buyer is looking at this week, as a fact they can check, not an adjective. “A single-level 3-bed on a cul-de-sac with the only fenced half-acre on the street” does the job. “Welcome to this charming home” does not, because it could open any listing in the county.

The first sentence of a listing description should state the one thing that makes this home different from the other three a buyer is comparing it with this week, as a fact they can check. A year, a lot size, a position, a renovation, a number. If the sentence could open any other listing in the county, it is not doing its job.

This matters more than any other line because it is often the only line that gets read in full. Portals and feeds cut the remarks short and show the first thirty or forty words next to the photo. Your own property page shows it at the top of the story section. If a buyer has to get past “Welcome to this lovely home” to find out what the home is, many will not.

What the first sentence has to carry

It has to answer “what is this” and “why this one” in the same breath. What is this: a 1920s bungalow, a corner-unit condo, a split-level on an acre. Why this one: the thing you would say first if a friend asked you about it in the car. Those two together, in plain words, and nothing else. The second sentence can slow down.

Five openers that work, with examples

  • The fact-first opener. “A 1931 stone cottage with its original slate roof and a 2022 kitchen, two blocks from the park.” Everything in it can be checked.
  • The position opener. “The highest lot on the street, backing onto the town woods with no rear neighbour.” Position is the one thing a renovation cannot change, so it is often the strongest lead.
  • The change opener. “Taken down to the studs in 2022: new wiring, plumbing, roof and kitchen, with the 1950s footprint kept.” Good for renovations, where the buyer's fear is what was skipped.
  • The number opener. “Four bedrooms on one floor, 2,400 square feet, on a third of an acre.” Plain and useful when the numbers are the reason to look.
  • The one-room opener. “The kitchen is the reason to see this house: 14 feet of counter, a 36-inch gas range, and a window wall onto the garden.” Use it when one room will carry the showing, and make sure photo one or two shows it.

Three rewrites

Before: “Welcome home to this beautiful and charming 3-bedroom in a sought-after neighbourhood.”

After: “A single-level 3-bed on a cul-de-sac, with the only fenced half-acre on the street.”

Before: “Rare opportunity to own a piece of history in the heart of downtown.”

After: “A corner unit on the fourth floor of the 1911 Mercantile Building, with 12-foot ceilings and windows on two sides.”

Before: “Don't miss this gorgeous, move-in ready gem.”

After: “New roof, furnace and windows in the last three years; original 1968 layout with a 24 by 24 garage.”

In each case the rewrite is longer, and in each case a buyer who reads nothing else knows what they would be driving to see.

Openers that lose the reader

  • Anything starting with “Welcome”. It spends the most valuable words on a greeting.
  • The address again. It is already on the page, the portal and the sign.
  • Three adjectives in a row. “Gorgeous, spacious, updated” tells the buyer that you either did not look closely or would rather not say.
  • “Rare” and “opportunity”. If it is rare, say what is rare about it. The word on its own has been used on too many ordinary homes to carry weight.
  • A sentence about you or the brokerage. Save that for the contact card.
  • A sentence about the ideal buyer. “Perfect for young families” describes people, not the house, and is the kind of phrase to run past your broker. Describe the three bedrooms and the fenced yard instead; the buyer will work out who it suits.

The same sentence, three places

Once you have the first sentence, it should be the first thing in the MLS remarks, the first line of the story section on the property page, and the first line a voice says in the listing video. Write it once, well, and reuse it. Buyers who meet the home in a reel and later on the page should hear the same promise, in the same words. What to do now

Take your current listing's description and read only the first sentence. If it could open another agent's listing without changes, rewrite it around one checkable fact and move on to the second sentence only when the first one is earned.

FAQ

Questions on this topic

How long should the first sentence of a listing description be?

Short enough to survive truncation in a feed or portal, which usually means under about twenty-five words. Put the most specific words early: the type of home, the year or the position, and the one fact that sets it apart. The second sentence can take its time.

Is it wrong to start with Welcome home?

It is not wrong, but it wastes the only words most buyers will read. A greeting tells the buyer nothing about the property. Replace it with the one fact you would mention first if a friend asked about the house.

Should the first sentence match the main photo?

Yes. If the main photo is the front exterior, the opener should be about the house as a whole or its position. If the main photo is the kitchen, the opener should say why the kitchen is the reason to come. A mismatch makes the buyer feel they opened the wrong listing.

Can the same opener be used in the voiceover script?

It should be. The spoken version may lose a clause so it reads naturally out loud, but the fact it leads with should be the same. A buyer who hears one promise in a reel and reads a different one on the page trusts neither.
